P11.1 · textbook pages 136–137

The nature of waves

A wave carries energy and information without transferring matter overall. Mechanical waves require a material medium; electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum.

Transverse waves

Oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Examples: waves on a rope, electromagnetic waves.

Longitudinal waves

Oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound waves in air produce compressions and rarefactions.

Mechanical vs electromagnetic

Mechanical waves need a medium; electromagnetic waves do not. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

P11.2 · textbook pages 138–139

The properties of waves

Amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium. Wavelength is one complete cycle, frequency is cycles per second, and period is the duration of a cycle.

Wave measurements

Amplitude A (m) is measured from the rest line to a crest or trough; wavelength λ (m) is crest-to-crest (or corresponding points).

Frequency and period

Frequency f is the number of oscillations each second in hertz (Hz). Period T = 1/f, in seconds.

Wave speed

Wave speed v = f × λ. Use metres and seconds: v in m/s, f in Hz, λ in m.

P11.3 · textbook pages 140–141

Reflection and refraction

In reflection a wave bounces off a boundary. In refraction a wave changes speed on entering another material, so its direction may change at an oblique boundary.

Reflection

The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, measured from the normal. Frequency, wavelength and speed in the original medium remain unchanged.

Refraction

When water waves enter shallower water, speed falls and wavelength decreases; frequency stays constant. If they approach at an angle, they bend towards the normal.

Transmission and absorption

At a material boundary some energy may be reflected, some transmitted and some absorbed. Absorbed energy can increase internal energy of the material.

P11.4 · textbook pages 142–143

More about waves

Sound is made by vibrating sources and travels through air as longitudinal compressions and rarefactions. You can investigate wave speeds using a loudspeaker, a stretched string or a ripple tank.

Sound and vacuum

Sound needs particles to pass on vibrations. Air removed from a bell jar makes the sound weaker even while the bell still vibrates.

Echoes

A sound echo travels to a wall and back. Wall distance = speed of sound × time delay ÷ 2; the total journey is twice the wall distance.

Wave investigations

On a string, one loop in a standing-wave pattern is half a wavelength; for n loops spanning length L, λ = 2L/n. In ripple tanks use v = distance/time or fλ.
