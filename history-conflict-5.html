Diplomacy outside the League · pp 38–39

How did international agreements help the League of Nations?

In the 1920s governments negotiated separate agreements to reduce tension, promote disarmament and renounce war. These could support peace, but bypassing the League also exposed its limited influence.

What does “outside the League” mean?

The governments involved negotiated and signed an agreement without the League organising it. The result might still help peace, but it suggested that leading states preferred direct diplomacy to collective decision-making.

Exam link: Explain both sides of the relationship: more cooperation but potentially less prestige for the League.

Why did it matter?

Postwar mistrust→Talks and treaties→Reduced tensions→League sidelined?

Germany was recovering from the war and the 1923 Ruhr crisis; France feared another German attack. Agreements offered a way to rebuild trust.

Locarno Treaties, October 1925

German Foreign Minister Gustav Stresemann and French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand helped organise negotiations at Locarno, Switzerland. Britain, Belgium, Italy and Czechoslovakia were also involved in the wider set of agreements.

What Germany accepted

Germany accepted the western borders established by Versailles, including the frontier with France and Belgium. Britain and Italy guaranteed the western settlement, and states pledged to settle disputes peacefully.

What Locarno did not guarantee

Germany's eastern borders with Poland and Czechoslovakia were not similarly guaranteed. Germany did not formally renounce the possibility of peaceful revision in the east. This left a significant source of tension.

1925 · Agreement

Former enemies made promises to respect western frontiers and resolve disputes peacefully. Germany signed voluntarily, unlike the 1919 Treaty of Versailles.

1926 · Recognition

Germany was admitted to the League of Nations — a major diplomatic step after its exclusion in 1920. Stresemann and Briand received the 1926 Nobel Peace Prize.

Limitation

Negotiations took place outside League machinery. The unguaranteed eastern borders limited the security settlement, while goodwill could disappear when governments changed.

Kellogg–Briand Pact, 1928

French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand and US Secretary of State Frank B. Kellogg promoted an international declaration renouncing war as an instrument of national policy. The pact was signed in Paris in August 1928 by an initial group of 15 states; many more later joined.

Why people welcomed it

Countries including the USA, France, Britain and Germany publicly committed to resolving disagreements without aggressive war. US participation was striking because the USA had not joined the League of Nations.

Long-term significance: renouncing aggressive war strengthened an emerging international norm against using war as a political tool.

The fatal weakness

The pact did not provide a workable enforcement system or its own armed forces. It did not remove national rivalries, and states could still claim self-defence. Later aggression showed the gap between promises and action.

League connection: the pact appeared to advance the League's aims while highlighting that the League itself had been bypassed.

Two other agreements to know

Rapallo Treaty · 1922

Germany and Soviet Russia, both diplomatically isolated, restored relations and settled outstanding wartime claims. Germany and Soviet Russia waived claims arising from wartime costs and damages and developed economic cooperation.

Neither state was a League member at the time. Rapallo showed that excluded powers could negotiate their own agreements independently.

Washington Naval Conference · 1921–22

Organised in the USA to limit naval competition. The Five-Power Treaty set capital-ship tonnage ratios of 5 : 5 : 3 : 1.75 : 1.75 for the USA, Britain, Japan, France and Italy respectively, alongside limits on battleship construction.

A disarmament success outside the League — suggesting that major powers preferred to decide important military questions themselves.

Accuracy check: Washington's famous 5:5:3 ratio compares the USA, Britain and Japan; France and Italy also participated under a lower ratio. Rapallo did not mean that Germany returned all territory lost by Soviet Russia in the 1918 Brest-Litovsk settlement.
