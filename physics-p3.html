P3.1 · textbook pages 32–33

Energy demands

Energy demand is met by fossil fuels, nuclear energy, biofuels and renewables. Electricity generation commonly uses heat to produce steam to turn a turbine and generator. Fossil fuels are finite. Nuclear fuel releases energy from nuclei; it creates radioactive waste but no direct carbon dioxide during generation.

Learn

Power station chain

Fuel → heats water → steam → turbine → generator → electricity. In a nuclear power station the reactor supplies the heating instead of combustion.

Renewable / non-renewable

Coal, oil, gas and nuclear fuels are non-renewable. Wind, solar, water and sustainably replenished biofuels are renewable.

Biofuels

Biomass can be converted to fuel such as ethanol or biogas. Whether it is genuinely carbon neutral depends on cultivation, processing and transport.

P3.2 · textbook pages 34–35

Energy from wind and water

Wind turbines, wave devices, hydroelectric generators and tidal barrages transfer movement energy to generators. Renewable does not always mean reliable: each method depends on weather, rainfall or tide patterns.

Learn

Wind and waves

Wind turns turbine blades. Waves move floating devices which drive generators; output is variable.

Hydroelectric / pumped storage

Water in an elevated reservoir transfers gravitational potential energy to kinetic energy as it flows downhill. Pumped storage can move water uphill using surplus electricity and release it later.

Tidal power

A tidal barrage holds back water at high tide, then releases it through turbines as water levels differ. Tides are predictable, but not continuous.

P3.3 · textbook pages 36–37

Power from the Sun and the Earth

Solar photovoltaic cells produce electricity directly from sunlight, while solar heating panels warm water. Geothermal energy comes mainly from energy inside the Earth, including radioactive decay. Geothermal steam can drive generators.

Learn

Solar photovoltaic

Sunlight → solar cells → electric current. Output depends on light conditions and available area.

Solar thermal

Solar radiation heats fluid or water in a solar collector. It does not directly produce electricity.

Geothermal

Hot rocks heat water underground; steam may drive a turbine and generator. It can give relatively reliable output in suitable regions.

P3.4 · textbook pages 38–39

Energy and the environment

Energy sources must be evaluated using greenhouse gases, acid rain, radioactive waste, local habitat damage, land use and reliability. Burning fossil fuels emits carbon dioxide; sulfur dioxide impurities can cause acid rain. Nuclear stations create radioactive waste.

Learn

Fossil fuels

Emissions from combustion include carbon dioxide; sulfur dioxide can contribute to acid rain. Pollution and depletion are costs.

Nuclear power

Generates electricity without direct greenhouse gas emissions during operation but uses finite fuel and produces long-lived radioactive waste.

Renewable trade-offs

Low operational emissions but weather dependence, visual impact, noise, flood risk or habitat disruption can matter. Consider specific local evidence.

P3.5 · textbook pages 40–41

Big energy issues

Electricity demand varies through a day and across seasons. Power generation must balance this demand with availability, start-up time, cost and environmental effects. Base load may be supplied by relatively steady sources; rapid-response stations and storage help with peaks.

Learn

Meeting demand

Gas-fired stations and stored hydroelectric energy can respond to peaks more quickly than nuclear plants, which are relatively slow to start.

Storage

Pumped storage consumes electricity to raise water, then generates when water is released. It balances supply and demand but loses energy overall.

Costs

Capital, operation, fuel, maintenance and decommissioning costs all matter; cost and reliability claims depend on location and technology.
