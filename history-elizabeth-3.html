‘Golden Age’?

Prosperity, fashion, country houses and theatre can make Elizabeth's reign look dazzling. But who benefited — and how far was it really a golden age?

3.1
Prosperity and status

Wealth and fashion in Elizabethan England

Elizabethan society was hierarchical. Nobility remained at the top, but a growing gentry used land, trade, education and display to increase their status.

Population pressure: England's population rose from about 2.8 million in 1538 to about 4 million by 1603. Growth helped create opportunity, but also pressure on food, work and living standards.

Who had power and wealth?

Select a group.

The rise of the gentry

Gentry were wealthy landowners below the nobility. Some also held important local offices such as Justice of the Peace. Their numbers and influence grew during Elizabeth's reign.

Trade / income→Buy land→Higher status→Local power

Fashion = a status signal

Wealthy Elizabethans displayed status through expensive clothes, elaborate ruffs, jewellery and fine fabrics. Clothing made hierarchy visible.

Key idea: prosperity did not mean equality. The richest could display wealth in ways ordinary people could not.

3.2
Architecture as evidence

Elizabethan country houses

The grand houses of the gentry and nobility were designed for comfort, privacy and display. Their architecture is evidence of growing wealth and changing fashions.

Study example: Hardwick Hall helps us understand Elizabethan wealth and architecture. The official AQA Historic Environment is a separately specified site that changes by exam series.

Hardwick Hall — spot the evidence

Bess of Hardwick rebuilt the house in Derbyshire in the 1590s. Select a feature to see what it reveals.

Comfort

Rooms became more specialised and private. Wealthy households could afford spaces designed for particular purposes rather than one hall doing almost everything.

Renaissance style

Classical ideas influenced design: symmetry, columns, decorative plasterwork and carefully planned façades showed fashionable taste.

Status

A spectacular house advertised wealth, family importance and connections. Architecture was a way of showing that the owner belonged among the elite.

3.3
A new mass entertainment

The role of theatre

Theatre moved from court and travelling performances towards professional companies and permanent playhouses. It became entertainment for a remarkably broad audience.

How theatre changed

1 · Court & travelling playersPlays had long been performed at court, in halls, inns and by travelling companies.
2 · Professional companiesSuccessful actors and writers increasingly worked in organised companies under noble patronage.
3 · Permanent theatresPurpose-built theatres created regular venues and much larger audiences.
4 · Cultural achievementWriters such as William Shakespeare and performers such as Richard Burbage became famous.

William Shakespeare (1564–1616)

Playwright, actor and shareholder. His plays included histories, comedies and tragedies. They were written to entertain audiences, not as modern history textbooks.

Richard Burbage (1568–1619)

A leading actor and member of the Lord Chamberlain's Men. He played major roles including Hamlet and King Lear and was also a theatre owner.

Why people loved theatre

It was exciting, relatively affordable and offered drama, comedy, music, costumes and spectacle. Different social groups could attend, although they paid for different places.

Why some opposed it

Critics linked theatres with disorder, drunkenness, crime and disease. Puritans could also object to plays as immoral distractions and disliked men playing female roles.

3.5
Make a historical judgement

Was it really a ‘Golden Age’?

The phrase fits real achievements — but it can hide inequality. A strong historian asks: golden for whom?

Art & literature

Portraiture, decorative arts and writers such as Shakespeare flourished.

Exploration & technology

Navigation, astronomy, printing and overseas voyages expanded knowledge and ambition.

Education

Education mattered increasingly, especially for wealthier boys, but access remained unequal.

Theatre

Professional theatre became hugely popular and created a lasting cultural legacy.

Buildings

Elite country houses reflected wealth, comfort and Renaissance fashions.

The problem

Many ordinary people remained poor and insecure. Population growth and inequality mean the label cannot describe everyone's experience.

1 · Explain what was important about the rise of the gentry in Elizabethan England.

8 marks

How to make an 8-mark explanation strong

  • Choose two or three ways the gentry mattered.
  • Use precise knowledge: land, wealth, trade, local office and JPs.
  • After each fact, explain the consequence for Elizabethan society or government.
Useful frame: “The rise of the gentry was important because… This meant… Therefore…”
Revision marking points — original revision question

Relevant ideas include growing numbers of wealthy landowners below the nobility; wealth from land and trade; purchase of land as a route to status; local influence; service as JPs and other offices; and the way growing prosperity changed the social hierarchy without removing inequality.

2 · How convincing is the view that Elizabethan theatre was “entertainment for all”?

8 marks

Interpretation-style answer

  • Test the claim using contextual knowledge — do not just describe theatre.
  • Support it with cheap standing places and mixed audiences.
  • Qualify it: different groups occupied different spaces, and theatre also faced opposition.
  • Keep judging: “This makes the view convincing because…”
Best structure: claim → evidence → explanation → judgement; then test a limitation.
Revision marking points — original revision question

Useful knowledge includes low-cost standing entry, audiences from different social groups, purpose-built theatres and large crowds, noble patronage, the Queen's interest in drama, social differences between pit and galleries, and Puritan/public-order objections.

3 · Explain what Elizabethan country houses can tell us about the growing prosperity of the elite.

8 marks

Turn features into historical evidence

  • Do not simply list windows, galleries and decoration.
  • For each feature, explain what it reveals about money, comfort, privacy or status.
  • Use Hardwick Hall as an example, not as proof that every house was identical.
Example move: “Large areas of expensive glass suggest… This mattered because…”
Revision marking points — original revision question

Possible evidence: expensive glass and many windows; symmetry and Renaissance/classical design; decorative plasterwork; long galleries; more specialised/private rooms; fireplaces/chimneys; and the use of architecture to advertise elite wealth and family status.

4 · “Elizabeth's reign deserves to be called a Golden Age.” How far do you agree?

Extended judgement

How to build a judgement

  • Define what “Golden Age” means before judging it.
  • Use achievements: theatre, literature, art, exploration, technology, education or elite prosperity.
  • Balance them against inequality and the experience of poorer people.
  • Finish with a clear answer to golden for whom?
Avoid: “Yes because Shakespeare was famous.” Aim for: a criterion-based judgement about how widely prosperity and cultural achievement were experienced.
Revision marking points — original revision question

A strong answer can recognise genuine cultural and technological achievements while questioning how representative they were of the whole population. The conclusion should weigh breadth of benefit, not merely count achievements.
