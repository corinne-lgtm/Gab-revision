3.1
Political violence, 1919–23

Murders and uprisings

The young Weimar Republic was attacked from both left and right. Uprisings, assassinations and attempted takeovers made its first years dangerously unstable.

Left-wing beliefs

Workers should have power through workers' councils; society should be more equal; major change was welcomed; left-wing ideas should spread internationally; many left-wing groups were anti-right-wing.

Right-wing beliefs

Strong leaders should rule; differences between classes and races were accepted; Germany should be a strong and powerful empire; major change was unwelcome; right-wing groups were anti-left-wing.

March 1920

The Kapp Putsch

Wolfgang Kapp + about 5,000 Free Corps→Take Berlin→Government flees→Workers strike→Gas, water, electricity & trains stop→Kapp flees after about 100 hours

Kapp wanted to overthrow Weimar, strengthen the army and recover territory lost at Versailles. The putsch failed because he did not have the support of workers.

Red Rising in the Ruhr

After Kapp fled, left-wing workers in the Ruhr continued their strike and took over towns. The government sent soldiers and some Free Corps units to crush the rebellion. Over 1,000 workers were killed.

Political assassinations

Between 1919 and 1922 there were over 350 political murders, mostly by right-wing extremists. Matthias Erzberger was murdered in August 1921; Foreign Minister Walter Rathenau was also killed. Many right-wing killers received short sentences.

3.2A
The early Nazi movement

Hitler and the Munich Putsch

Hitler entered politics after the First World War and transformed a tiny Munich political group into the Nazi Party.

Hitler after the war

Hitler had served in the German army, won medals and was wounded. In May 1919 the army sent him to investigate the German Workers' Party. He liked what he heard and soon joined.

A powerful speaker

Hitler attracted members through newspaper advertisements and public meetings. His powerful speeches were especially important in an age before radio and television were widespread.

The Nazi Party

Hitler changed the party's name to the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP), adopted the swastika and created the Stormtroopers (SA), a violent group mainly made up of ex-soldiers.

Growth: the textbook gives about 3,000 members in 1920 and 5,000 in 1921. By 1923 Hitler believed the crisis in Germany gave him a chance to seize power.
8 November 1923

Hitler launches the Munich Putsch

Hitler interrupted a meeting in a Munich beer hall where Bavarian leader Gustav von Kahr was speaking. He fired a shot into the ceiling, announced that he was taking over Bavaria and planned to march on Berlin. General Ludendorff joined him, while Stormtroopers occupied government buildings around Munich.

3.2B
Failure — but a strategic turning point

What happened to the Munich Putsch?

The armed takeover collapsed quickly. Yet Hitler's trial, imprisonment and lessons from failure helped reshape his future strategy.

Cause → consequence

Why the putsch failed

Kahr withdraws support→Police contacted→Hitler + ~2,000 march→Armed police confront them→Gun battle→16 Nazis + 3 police killed→Hitler arrested

Trial

The trial lasted 24 days and became a media sensation. Hitler used it to attack the government and spread his political views. Judge Ludendorff was acquitted; Hitler received five years.

Prison & Mein Kampf

Hitler was released in December 1924 after only nine months. In prison he organised his ideas and wrote Mein Kampf, combining autobiography with political and racial views.

A new strategy

Hitler concluded that armed rebellion had failed. The Nazis would instead stand in elections and win votes, using democracy to gain power.

Interpretations: the chapter contrasts a Nazi-produced 1934 account presenting Hitler heroically with historian Joachim Fest's 1973 account portraying confusion, fear and retreat. Who produced an interpretation — and why — matters.
3.3
The Stresemann era

To what extent did the Weimar Republic recover after 1923?

Between 1924 and 1929 Germany recovered from many immediate crises, largely under Gustav Stresemann — but serious weaknesses remained underneath.

Key biography

Gustav Stresemann (1878–1929)

Chancellor in 1923 and Foreign Minister from 1924 until his death in 1929. He believed Germany needed international acceptance and worked to stabilise the economy and improve relations abroad.

1 · Currency

Stresemann stopped printing old paper money and introduced the temporary Rentenmark. In 1924 it was replaced by the stable Reichsmark. Hyperinflation ended — but savers did not recover what they had lost.

2 · Dawes & Young Plans

The Dawes Plan brought US loans and a new reparations schedule; French and Belgian troops left the Ruhr. In 1929 the Young Plan reduced reparations from £6.6 billion to £1.8 billion and gave Germany longer to pay.

3 · Foreign relations

Germany signed the Locarno Pact (1925), joined the League of Nations (1926) and signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928). Germany regained international status.

4 · Industry

American loans also helped build factories, houses, schools and roads. Employment and prosperity improved, and some American companies opened factories in Germany.

Recovery test

Had Germany fully recovered?

Choose the best judgement.

Underlying problems: coalition politics remained unstable; extremist parties still existed; farmers and parts of the middle class struggled; and much prosperity depended on American loans that could be recalled.
3.4
Culture in the 1920s

Germany's Golden Age

Greater freedom after the war helped cinema, theatre, literature, art and design flourish — but not every German welcomed the new culture.

Cinema & nightlife

Fritz Lang's Metropolis was highly advanced, while Marlene Dietrich became an international star. Berlin became famous for theatre, cabaret, jazz, nightclubs and experimental performances.

Literature & art

Publishing boomed. All Quiet on the Western Front sold half a million copies in three months. Avant-garde artists such as Otto Dix and George Grosz showed social inequality and the harsh realities of German life.

Bauhaus design

The Bauhaus promoted modern, simple and practical design rather than ornate older styles, influencing everything from chairs and coffee pots to office buildings.

Culture war

Freedom or moral decline?

Supporters

Many Germans embraced artistic freedom and experimentation. Berlin attracted visitors from across Europe and its cultural scene became internationally famous.

Critics

Traditionalists believed nightlife, modern art and experimental culture were corrupting German values. The Nazis later attacked this culture; after 1933 artists and performers such as Fritz Lang and George Grosz left Germany.

1 · Explain why the Kapp Putsch failed.

8 marks

Tip

  • Explain causes of failure, not just what happened.
  • Think about support, the government's response and the workers.
  • Build a chain showing how actions made Kapp unable to govern.
Revision marking points

Kapp and about 5,000 Free Corps seized Berlin and forced the government to flee, but workers refused to support him and went on strike. Gas, water, electricity and trains stopped. Without workers' cooperation Kapp could not govern and fled after about 100 hours.

2 · How does Interpretation D differ from Interpretation C about what happened at the Munich Putsch?

4 marks

Interpretation tip

  • State the view in D.
  • Contrast it directly with C.
  • Use the interpretations' presentation of Hitler and the putsch, rather than retelling the whole event.
Revision marking points

Interpretation C, a Nazi Party biography, portrays Hitler as brave and heroic, helping an injured child. Interpretation D, historian Joachim Fest, presents the scene as chaotic and Hitler as frightened and scrambling away, with the heroic child story described as a later invention.

3 · Why might the authors of Interpretations C and D have a different interpretation about what happened at the Munich Putsch?

4 marks

Provenance tip

  • Don't just say “they have different opinions”.
  • Think about who produced each account, when, and for what purpose.
  • Connect provenance to the way Hitler is portrayed.
Revision marking points

C was adapted from an official Nazi Party biography published in 1934, giving the regime a reason to glorify Hitler. D was adapted from historian Joachim Fest's later 1973 account, which was not produced as Nazi propaganda and portrays the failure much less heroically.

4 · To what extent had the Weimar Republic recovered by 1929?

12 marks

Judgement tip

  • Cover both recovery and remaining weakness.
  • Use precise evidence from economy, foreign policy and politics.
  • Ask whether Stresemann solved problems or merely postponed them.
  • Finish with a supported overall judgement.
Revision marking points — original revision question

Recovery: Rentenmark/Reichsmark, end of hyperinflation, Dawes Plan, Ruhr evacuation, Young Plan, Locarno, League of Nations, Kellogg-Briand Pact and industrial improvement. Limits: lost savings were not restored, coalition instability continued, extremists remained, farmers and parts of the middle class struggled, and prosperity relied heavily on American loans.

5 · In what ways did German culture change in the 1920s? Explain your answer.

8 marks

Culture question tip

  • Choose several areas of culture rather than one example.
  • Give named evidence.
  • Explain what was new or different compared with the more controlled culture before 1918.
Revision marking points

Relevant areas include cinema (Metropolis, Marlene Dietrich), nightlife and cabaret, literature such as All Quiet on the Western Front, avant-garde art by Otto Dix and George Grosz, and modern Bauhaus design. Strong answers also recognise that traditionalists rejected some changes as immoral or un-German.
