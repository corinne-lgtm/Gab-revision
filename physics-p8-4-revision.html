📍 What is Centre of Mass?
Core Definition The centre of mass (CoM) of an object is the point at which its mass can be thought of as being concentrated. The weight of the object acts at this single point.

Why does it matter?

🪑 Engineers designing furniture use CoM to ensure objects won't tip over — a chair must stay stable even when someone leans back on it.
🏎️ Racing cars are kept low to the ground so their CoM is low — this prevents them from overturning at high speeds when cornering.
⚠️ Objects with a HIGH centre of mass are more likely to tip over. Objects with a LOW and WIDE base are more stable.
⚖️ Suspended Equilibrium
The Rule When a freely suspended object is released, it comes to rest with its centre of mass directly below the point of suspension. An object in this position is in equilibrium — its weight produces no turning force.
✅ Freely suspended means: if you push the object away and release it, it swings back to equilibrium. It's because the weight creates a turning force that restores it.

Think about flower baskets on a hook:

In Equilibrium ✅

  • CoM directly below the hook
  • Weight acts straight down through the pivot
  • No turning force → stays still

Non-Equilibrium 🔄

  • CoM to the side of the hook
  • Weight creates a turning force
  • Object swings → returns to equilibrium

🪢 Finding CoM of an Irregular Shape

The Plumb Line Method — used in experiments with irregular cards

1
Pierce & Suspend Put a hole in one corner of the irregular card and suspend it from a rod. The card hangs freely.
2
Draw a Plumb Line Hang a plumb line (a string with a small weight) from the same rod. Draw a vertical line on the card along the plumb line. The CoM lies somewhere on this line.
3
Repeat from a Different Corner Move the suspension point to another corner. Draw a second plumb line.
4
Find the Intersection The centre of mass is where the two lines cross. ✔️ Verify: the card should balance on the flat end of a pencil placed at this point.
💡 Why does this work? Because in each hanging position, the CoM must be directly below the pivot — so it must lie along every plumb line drawn. The only point on all lines is their intersection.
🔬 Exam tip: You need a minimum of two plumb lines. A third line through the same point confirms accuracy.
🔷 Centre of Mass of Symmetrical Objects
The Rule for Symmetrical Shapes For a flat symmetrical object, its centre of mass lies along its axis of symmetry. If it has more than one axis, the CoM is where they meet.
Rectangle / Ruler Two axes of symmetry → CoM is at the midpoint (centre of the shape)
Equilateral Triangle Three axes of symmetry, all bisecting angles → meet at one point in the centre
Circle Infinite axes of symmetry → CoM is at the geometric centre
Semicircle One axis of symmetry (vertical) → CoM is on that axis (not at geometric midpoint)
⚠️ Don't confuse: The CoM of a semicircle is NOT at its midpoint — it's closer to the flat edge. For uniform semicircle of radius r, it's at 4r/3π from the flat edge.

📖 Key Terms Glossary
Centre of mass The point where an object's mass (and weight) can be considered to act.
Equilibrium A state where there is no net force and no net turning force on an object.
Freely suspended Hanging freely from a pivot point, able to swing until the CoM is below the pivot.
Plumb line A string with a small weight on the end, always hanging perfectly vertically due to gravity.
Axis of symmetry A line that divides a shape into two mirror-image halves.
Turning force (moment) A force that causes rotation around a pivot point.
⭐ Key Points Summary
⭐ The centre of mass of an object is the point where its mass can be thought of as concentrated.
⭐ The centre of mass of a uniform ruler (or any uniform rectangle) is at its midpoint.
⭐ When an object is freely suspended, it comes to rest with its CoM directly underneath the point of suspension.
⭐ The centre of mass of a symmetrical object lies along its axis of symmetry.
✅ Use 2 plumb lines from different corners to find the CoM of any irregular shape — the CoM is where they cross.
