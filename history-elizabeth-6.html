6.1
1558–1559

How did England's religion change under Elizabeth?

Elizabeth inherited a country that had switched between Protestant and Catholic rule. Her 1559 Religious Settlement aimed to create a Protestant Church that most people could live with.

Catholic beliefs & practices

The Pope was head of the Church. Services and the Bible were in Latin. Churches could contain statues, stained glass and decoration. Priests did not marry. Catholics taught transubstantiation: the bread and wine became Christ's body and blood.

Protestant beliefs & practices

The monarch should head the English Church. Services and the Bible should be in English. Churches and clergy should be simpler. Priests could marry. Bread and wine were treated as symbols of Christ's body and blood.

The 1559 Religious Settlement

Royal Supremacy

Elizabeth became Supreme Governor of the Church of England, rejecting papal authority over the English Church.

Uniform worship

The Book of Common Prayer set out services. English worship was Protestant, but Elizabeth avoided some of the most extreme changes.

Compromise

The aim was political as well as religious: create enough uniformity to reduce division while keeping broad obedience to the Crown.

Why religion was such a big issue

Henry VIII had broken with Rome, Edward VI pushed England further towards Protestantism, and Mary I restored Catholicism and persecuted Protestants. Elizabeth therefore inherited deep disagreement and memories of rapid change. Her settlement was designed to prevent religion from destabilising the kingdom.

6.2
Resistance and response

How did Catholics react?

Elizabeth initially hoped outward conformity would be enough. But rebellion, excommunication and plots made Catholic opposition look increasingly connected to political danger.

From compromise to tougher control

Click the dates to see why policy hardened.

1569

Northern Rebellion

Catholic nobles in the north rebelled, restored Catholic worship at Durham and hoped to free Mary, Queen of Scots. The rising failed, but it showed religion could combine with political rebellion.

1570

Papal bull

Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth. This intensified the problem of Catholic loyalty because opponents could claim that obedience to Elizabeth conflicted with obedience to the Pope.

1571–1586

Catholic plots

Ridolfi, Throckmorton and Babington all involved plans linked to replacing Elizabeth with Mary, Queen of Scots and, in some cases, foreign intervention.

Key distinction: being Catholic did not automatically mean being a rebel. The government became especially concerned when Catholic belief appeared connected to disobedience, missionary activity, foreign powers or plots against Elizabeth.
6.3
Missionaries and Europe

Reactions from abroad

The Catholic challenge was not confined to England. Seminaries abroad trained priests to return to England, while powerful Catholic states created the possibility of foreign support.

Douai and missionary priests

William Allen established a seminary at Douai in 1568 to train English Catholic priests. Missionary priests returned secretly to England to support Catholic worship and strengthen the faith.

Jesuits

The Society of Jesus was a major force in the Catholic Counter-Reformation. Jesuit missionaries such as Edmund Campion entered England in 1580. To Elizabeth's government, missionary work could look like part of a wider international Catholic threat.

Why did the threat feel international?

Catholic powers in Europe

France and Spain were major Catholic powers. Elizabeth tried to avoid provoking a full-scale Catholic attack, but relations changed over time. By the 1580s, conflict with Spain and Catholic missionary activity made religion and foreign policy increasingly difficult to separate.

6.4
1580s–1590s

Elizabeth and the Catholic threat

As missionary activity and plots increased, Elizabeth's government introduced harsher laws. The central issue became loyalty: could Catholics remain loyal subjects while rejecting the religious settlement?

Edmund Campion

Campion returned to England as a Jesuit missionary in 1580. He preached secretly and helped sustain Catholic belief. He was captured in 1581 and executed for treason. His case shows how the government increasingly treated missionary activity as a political and security issue.

Recusancy

Recusants were people who refused to attend Church of England services. Fines became much heavier in 1581, increasing pressure on Catholics to conform outwardly.

How the laws became tougher

1571

Measures against Catholic practices and papal authority were strengthened.

1581

Recusancy fines rose sharply; converting people to Catholicism became a serious offence.

1585

Jesuit and seminary priests were ordered to leave; sheltering them could be treated as treasonous.

1593

Persistent recusants faced tighter restrictions, including limits on travelling far from home without permission.

Why tougher?

Missionaries, plots, Mary, Queen of Scots, papal hostility and worsening relations with Spain made the government more suspicious.

But remember

Elizabeth's policy was not simply a campaign against private belief: the government focused heavily on conformity, obedience and threats to political security.

6.5
A Protestant challenge

Puritans and their beliefs

Puritans were Protestants who believed Elizabeth's Church had not gone far enough. They wanted to remove more Catholic-looking practices and make worship and Church organisation more thoroughly Protestant.

What did Puritans want?

Prophesyings and Grindal

Puritan clergy held prophesyings: meetings for preaching and discussion of scripture. Archbishop Edmund Grindal was sympathetic to preaching and resisted Elizabeth's order to suppress the meetings. Elizabeth suspended him, showing that even Protestant reform had limits when it challenged royal control.

Whitgift and tighter control

Archbishop John Whitgift, appointed in 1583, enforced conformity more firmly. Puritan ministers could be suspended or removed, and radical publications and organisation were restricted.

How serious was the Puritan threat?

Puritans included some MPs and influential Protestants, but they did not command enough support to overturn Elizabeth's settlement. Elizabeth opposed them because she wanted religious uniformity and because attempts to reshape the Church could challenge her authority as Supreme Governor.

AQA focus: know both sides of the religious challenge. Catholic opposition could connect England to the Pope, missionaries, plots and foreign powers; Puritans challenged the settlement from within Protestantism. Elizabeth's priority was to defend the settlement and royal authority.

1 · Explain why Elizabeth's Religious Settlement was designed as a compromise.

Revision practice

How to answer a “why” question well

  • Build two or three explained reasons, not a list.
  • Use the instability Elizabeth inherited after Edward VI and Mary I.
  • Explain how compromise could support both religious conformity and political stability.
  • Keep linking each reason back to Elizabeth's problem in 1558–59.
Useful structure: “One reason was… This mattered because… Therefore Elizabeth…”
Revision marking points

Useful material includes the rapid religious changes before 1558, divisions between Catholics and Protestants, Elizabeth's need for obedience, royal supremacy, uniform worship and her wish to avoid unnecessarily provoking subjects or foreign Catholic powers.

2 · Explain how the Catholic threat to Elizabeth changed between 1569 and 1586.

Revision practice

This is about change over time

  • Start with the Northern Rebellion and explain what made it dangerous.
  • Then show how the papal bull, missionaries and plots widened the threat.
  • Use dates and named examples: 1569, 1570, Ridolfi, Throckmorton, Babington.
  • Explain the direction of change: from domestic rebellion towards a threat increasingly linked to Mary and foreign Catholic support.
Revision marking points

The 1569 Northern Rebellion showed domestic Catholic resistance. The 1570 excommunication sharpened the loyalty issue. Seminary and Jesuit missionaries strengthened Catholic networks. Ridolfi, Throckmorton and Babington connected opposition to Mary, Queen of Scots and possible foreign intervention, making the threat appear more political and international.

3 · Explain why Elizabeth's policy towards Catholics became harsher after 1570.

Revision practice

Turn events into reasons

  • Do not simply list laws. Explain what caused the government to introduce them.
  • Link the Pope's excommunication to the problem of loyalty.
  • Use missionaries and plots as evidence that the government feared organised opposition.
  • Finish by explaining why religion increasingly became a security issue.
Revision marking points

Relevant evidence includes the papal bull of 1570, missionary priests, Campion, Catholic plots, Mary, Queen of Scots and deteriorating relations with Spain. Laws in 1581, 1585 and 1593 increased pressure on recusants and Catholic priests.

4 · “Puritans were a serious threat to Elizabeth's religious settlement.” How far do you agree?

Extended judgement

Build an argument, not two disconnected lists

  • For the statement: use prophesyings, Puritan MPs/clergy and attempts to change Church organisation.
  • Against it: explain their limited support and Elizabeth's ability to control Grindal, ministers and publications.
  • Compare the threat to Elizabeth's authority with the threat to her life or throne.
  • End with a clear judgement about how serious Puritanism was and why.
Revision marking points

Puritans challenged uniformity and royal control and had some influential supporters. However, Elizabeth retained authority over the Church, disciplined Grindal, backed Whitgift's enforcement of conformity and prevented Puritanism from replacing the settlement. A strong judgement distinguishes religious/political challenge from a direct threat to Elizabeth's throne.
