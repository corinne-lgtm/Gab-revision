B1.8 Osmosis in plants

Why osmosis is essential in plant cells, the states of turgid, flaccid and plasmolysed, and how to investigate osmosis in plant tissue.

Osmosis keeps plant cells turgid — giving stems and leaves their rigidity and support. Without it, plants wilt.
🎯

Learning objectives

  • Why osmosis is so important in plant cells
  • How to investigate the effect of osmosis on plant tissues

Osmosis in plant cells — the three states

🌿 Turgid
💪
Normal, healthy state. Water moves in by osmosis → vacuole swells → pushes cytoplasm against the cell wall → pressure builds up (turgor pressure) → cell hard and rigid.
😞 Flaccid
🥀
Cell loses water. Surrounding solution is hypertonic → water leaves cell → vacuole shrinks → no pressure on cell wall → cell soft. Plant wilts as turgor no longer supports tissues.
☠️ Plasmolysed
💀
More severe water loss. Even more water leaves → vacuole and cytoplasm shrink → cell membrane pulls away from cell wall. Usually only in lab experiments. Cell dies unless balance is restored.
⚠️ Turgid = good. Flaccid = wilting. Plasmolysed = cell membrane detached from wall = usually fatal.

Why turgor pressure matters

🌱

Support for plant tissues

Plants need the fluid surrounding cells to always be hypotonic to the cytoplasm — so water keeps moving in by osmosis, keeping cells turgid.

Turgid cells press against each other — this gives leaves and stems their rigidity and firmness.

🏗️

Structural importance

Turgor pressure is the main source of support for non-woody plants. If a plant is deprived of water, cells become flaccid → the plant wilts → leaves droop → stems collapse.

💧

Water moves in when…

The surrounding solution is hypotonic (more dilute than the cytoplasm). Water concentration outside is higher → osmosis moves water into the cell → vacuole swells → turgor pressure builds.

🌵

Water moves out when…

The surrounding solution is hypertonic (more concentrated than cytoplasm). Water moves out → cells become flaccid → if severe, plasmolysis occurs.

Investigating osmosis in plant cells

🔬 Method — measuring mass change

Plant tissue (e.g. potato cylinders) is placed in different concentrations of salt or sugar solutions for 30 minutes. The mass change is measured:

  • Gain in mass → solution was hypotonic → water moved IN by osmosis
  • Loss in mass → solution was hypertonic → water moved OUT by osmosis
  • No change → solution was isotonic → no net movement of water
⚠️ Always dry the surface of plant tissue before measuring mass — any liquid left on the outside will affect your results.

🥔 Why potato?

Potato is used because it is cheap, easy to cut into uniform shapes (cylinders, chips, discs), widely available, and reacts strongly to changes in osmotic concentration.

🧪 Salt vs sugar solutions

Both create concentration gradients. Sugar solutions may be more accurate as they are less likely to affect cell chemistry. Potato reacts visibly and measurably to both.

📐 Surface area effect

Smaller pieces (larger surface area to volume ratio) → faster osmosis. Cylinders vs discs vs chips all give different rates — shape and size must be kept consistent within an experiment.

Exam tip

Common questions:
  • Define osmosis [1 mark]
  • Explain beetroot results (gained/lost/no change in mass) in terms of osmosis [6 marks]
  • Suggest and explain why osmosis is important in the structural support of plants [6 marks]

Top tip for 6-mark questions: Link each point clearly — mention turgid, turgor pressure, vacuole swelling, cell wall, support of stem/leaves, and what happens when cells become flaccid (plant wilts).
