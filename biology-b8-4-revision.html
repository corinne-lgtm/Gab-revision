B8.4 Making the most of photosynthesis

How factors interact to limit photosynthesis, and how humans manipulate the environment in which plants grow.

📖 Revision ✏️ Questions
More photosynthesis = more biomass = faster growth. Greenhouses let farmers control all limiting factors — but costs must be balanced against profits.
🎯

Learning objectives

  • How factors affecting photosynthesis interact

Why greenhouses increase yield

In theory: give plants plenty of light, CO₂, and warmth → they photosynthesize as fast as possible → grow as fast as possible. In the open field it is almost impossible to control any of these factors. A greenhouse makes the environment much more controllable.

  • Atmosphere is warmer inside than outside — speeds up photosynthesis
  • Growers can add CO₂ to the air artificially — removes a key limiting factor
  • Growers can add artificial lighting — extends the growing day
  • Growers can control mineral content of water for hydroponics

In the UK, greenhouses are used to grow fruit like peaches, lemons, and oranges that don't grow well outside.

📊 Figure 1 data: American research showed that the crop yield for tomatoes was nearly doubled in a greenhouse — outdoor yield ≈ 6.75 kg/plant; indoor ≈ 11.3 kg/plant.

Hydroponics

In hydroponics, plants are grown in water with a perfect balance of minerals instead of soil — so nothing slows their growth. Large commercial greenhouses combine hydroponics with full environmental control (light, CO₂, temperature, minerals — all controlled by computer software).

  • Crops are clean and soil-free
  • No need to plough or prepare land
  • Can be grown where the land is poor
  • Fast turnover — shorter time from seed to harvest
  • For plants grown hydroponically, limiting factors are a thing of the past

Interacting limiting factors — four key scenarios

ScenarioMost likely limiting factorWhy
i) Wheat field — first thing in the morning Light intensity AND temperature Early morning: low light (sun just rising) AND low temperature (night cool) — both are likely limiting. As light and temperature increase together, CO₂ may then become limiting.
ii) Same field — later in the day CO₂ concentration By mid-day: light is plentiful, temperature is warm — so light and temperature are no longer limiting. Plants have been photosynthesising all morning, using up the CO₂ around them → CO₂ becomes the limiting factor.
iii) Woodland floor — winter Light intensity Winter: trees have no leaves, but light levels are very low due to short days and low sun angle. Temperature also low. Both light and temperature are limiting — light is usually the primary one.
iv) Woodland floor — summer Light intensity Summer: trees are in full leaf, creating dense shade on the woodland floor. Even though temperature is warm and CO₂ is available, very little light penetrates → light is the main limiting factor.
⚠️ Why it's impossible to be certain: There is always a constant interaction between factors — changing one changes which factor is limiting. In the field, growers cannot isolate individual variables as in a lab. Multiple factors may be equally or nearly equally limiting at any one time.

Greenhouse economics — why 20°C not 30°C?

From Figure 3: with high CO₂ and 30°C, the rate of photosynthesis (and therefore yield) is higher than at 20°C. But many growers choose 20°C over 30°C, and are even investing in insulation that can cool the greenhouse electronically.

Why?

  • Heating a large greenhouse to 30°C costs much more in gas/electricity than heating to 20°C
  • The extra crop produced at 30°C may not offset the higher energy costs — the profit margin falls
  • At 20°C with high CO₂, two crops per year are possible — three at 30°C, but the extra heating cost may wipe out the extra profit
  • Insulation reduces heating costs — keeping the greenhouse warm cheaply, and sometimes electronic cooling prevents overheating in summer
💡 Key principle: The decision isn't just about maximum photosynthesis rate — it's about maximising profit. The cost of providing conditions must always be weighed against the value of the increased yield.

Pros and cons of greenhouse growing

✅

Benefits

  • Control of all limiting factors — maximise photosynthesis rate
  • Grow crops out of season or in unsuitable climates
  • Faster growth, larger and cleaner crops
  • No soil preparation or ploughing needed (hydroponics)
  • Can be grown on poor land
  • Shorter time from seed to harvest — faster turnover
❌

Costs and limitations

  • Very expensive to set up (glass/plastic, heating, lighting)
  • Electricity and gas for lighting, heating, CO₂ levels
  • Expensive monitoring equipment and computers
  • Alarms needed — things going wrong can destroy an entire crop
  • Cost of providing very high yield conditions (30°C vs 20°C) may wipe out the extra profit
