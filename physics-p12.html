P12.1 · textbook pages 146–147

The electromagnetic spectrum

Electromagnetic (EM) waves are transverse waves that transfer energy without transferring matter. All travel at the same speed in a vacuum: 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. Order matters: radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-rays → gamma rays. Moving towards gamma means shorter wavelength and higher frequency.

Wave equation: v = fλ. The wavelength λ is measured in metres, the frequency f in hertz.

Visible light: approximately 400–700 nm. Violet has a shorter wavelength than red.

Exam habit: 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m; use standard form carefully.

P12.2 · textbook pages 148–149

Light, infrared, microwaves and radio waves

Visible light is the narrow band of EM radiation detected by our eyes. White light contains all the colours of the visible spectrum. All objects emit infrared radiation; hotter objects emit more infrared radiation per second. Radio and microwave frequencies are used for communications and microwaves can transfer energy to food.

Light: lenses focus visible light to form images in cameras and the eye.

Infrared: thermal imaging and heaters. Absorption and emission depend on temperature and surface characteristics.

Microwaves: used in satellite communication and cooking. Heating occurs when microwave energy is absorbed by food; ordinary microwave oven walls reflect the waves.

Radio: broadcasting and communications. Uses vary by frequency, range and bandwidth.

P12.3 · textbook pages 150–151

Communications

Radio waves, microwaves, infrared and visible light can carry information. A transmitter encodes information on a carrier wave. A receiver detects and reconstructs that signal. Different bands have different ranges, absorption and available bandwidth.

Satellites: microwave links can travel through the atmosphere and communicate with satellites.

Radio masts: transmit signals that travel to receivers. Higher frequencies can carry more information but coverage depends on obstacles, power and propagation.

Optical fibres: transmit light pulses along glass fibres by total internal reflection, providing high data capacity and relatively secure communication.

Higher focus: modulation changes a property of a carrier wave (for example amplitude or frequency) to convey information.

P12.4 · textbook pages 152–153

Ultraviolet waves, X-rays and gamma rays

UV, X-rays and gamma rays have shorter wavelengths and higher frequencies than visible light. Ultraviolet can cause skin damage and eye damage. X-rays and gamma rays are ionising radiation: they can remove electrons from atoms and damage cells and DNA.

UV uses: security marking and fluorescence. Excess exposure can cause sunburn, skin ageing and increased skin cancer risk.

X-rays: produced when high-speed electrons are rapidly slowed or interact with a target in an X-ray tube. X-rays can image bone and metal.

Gamma: emitted by unstable nuclei. Used in sterilisation and radiotherapy; absorbed dose and exposure must be controlled.

Safe practice: reduce time near sources, increase distance and use appropriate shielding.

P12.5 · textbook pages 154–155

X-rays in medicine

Medical X-ray images form because different tissues absorb X-rays differently. Bones absorb more X-rays than soft tissues, so fewer X-rays reach the detector behind bone. In radiotherapy, higher-energy X-rays can be directed at tumours to damage cancer cells.

Image formation: many X-rays pass through soft tissue; fewer pass through bone. A detector measures the radiation reaching it, producing contrast.

Contrast media: substances such as barium sulfate strongly absorb X-rays and help outline the digestive tract in relevant investigations.

Protection: shields and collimation reduce unnecessary exposure; medical staff minimise dose and justify examinations.

Risk–benefit: ionising radiation can damage cells; clinicians choose exposure settings to obtain useful images at the lowest appropriate dose.
