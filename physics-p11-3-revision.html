Big Ideas
🔵
Reflection

The reflected wavefront leaves the barrier at the same angle as the incident wavefront — speed and wavelength don't change.

🔴
Refraction

Waves change speed and direction at a boundary between two different materials — except at normal incidence.

🟢
Same Frequency

Reflected and refracted waves have the same frequency as the incident wave, but different speeds give different wavelengths.

🟠
At a Boundary

Waves can be reflected, refracted (transmitted) or absorbed — or a combination of all three.

Key Definitions
Reflection
The bouncing back of a wave from a surface or barrier without it passing through.
Refraction
The change in direction of a wave as it crosses a boundary between two media, caused by a change in speed.
Incident wavefront
The wavefront travelling toward a boundary or barrier (before reflection or refraction).
Normal incidence
When a wave hits a boundary at exactly 90° (perpendicular). The wave changes speed but NOT direction.
Wavelet
A tiny section of a wavefront. Many wavelets travelling and combining together form the advancing wavefront.
Transmitted wave
A wave that passes through a substance rather than being reflected or absorbed by it.
Reflection

θᵢ = θᵣ — angle of incidence equals angle of reflection

The reflected wavefront moves away from the barrier at the same angle as the incident wavefront approaches it. The speed and wavelength of the wave do not change on reflection.
⚡ Exam Tip

Angles are always measured between the wavefront and the barrier (or equivalently between the ray and the normal to the barrier). The reflected angle always equals the incident angle.

Refraction

Waves slow down → wavelength decreases → direction changes towards boundary

Normal Incidence (90°)

  • Waves hit boundary perpendicularly
  • Speed changes ✓
  • Wavelength changes ✓
  • Direction does NOT change
  • Frequency stays the same

Non-Zero Angle

  • Waves hit boundary at an angle
  • Speed changes ✓
  • Wavelength changes ✓
  • Direction changes ✓
  • Frequency stays the same
When waves slow down crossing a boundary (e.g. deep → shallow water), the refracted wavefronts are closer together (shorter wavelength) and are at a smaller angle to the boundary than the incident wavefronts.
⚠ Common Mistake

Frequency does NOT change when waves refract. Only speed and wavelength change. Remember: v = fλ, so if speed halves, wavelength halves — frequency stays the same.

⚡ Exam Tip — Explaining Refraction

Use the wavelet model: each tiny section of the wavefront creates a wavelet. When one side of the wavefront slows down before the other (hitting the boundary first), the wavefront bends. This explains the direction change.

What Happens at a Boundary?
↩️
Reflected

Some or all of the wave bounces back from the surface. e.g. microwaves reflected by metal surfaces.

➡️
Transmitted

Wave passes through the substance. e.g. light is mostly transmitted by ordinary glass.

🔥
Absorbed

Wave energy is transferred to the substance, heating it. e.g. food heated in microwave ovens.

📉
Amplitude Decreases

As waves travel through a substance, their amplitude gradually decreases as energy is absorbed.

⚡ Exam Tip

What happens depends on the wavelength of the wave AND the substance. Microwaves pass through paper but are reflected by metal. Darkened glass absorbs almost all light; ordinary glass transmits most of it.

Key Points Summary
Reflection — same angle
Plane waves in a ripple tank are reflected from a straight barrier at the same angle as the incident waves because their speed and wavelength do not change on reflection.
Refraction — boundary + non-zero angle
Plane waves crossing a boundary between two different materials are refracted unless they cross the boundary at normal incidence.
Cause of refraction
Refraction occurs at a boundary between two different materials because the speed and wavelength of the waves change at the boundary.
At a boundary
Waves can be reflected, transmitted, or absorbed — or a combination. Which happens depends on the wavelength and the material.
