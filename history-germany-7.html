Chapter 7 · Economic changes

How Nazi economic policies changed employment, farming and workers’ lives — and how the Second World War transformed everyday life in Germany.

7.1
Employment

Work and bread

More than six million Germans were unemployed when Hitler came to power in 1933. The Nazis promised “work and bread” and introduced schemes intended to put Germans back to work.

National Labour Service (RAD)

All men aged 18–25 had to spend six months in the RAD. They planted forests, mended hedges and dug drainage ditches. They wore uniforms, lived in camps, received free meals and only pocket money. The scheme provided work and helped reduce official unemployment.

Public works

In June 1933 the Nazis ordered a new network of autobahns. This gave work to nearly 100,000 people, and by 1938 around 3,800 km of highway had been built. Schools and hospitals were also built.

Rearmament

Hitler ordered tanks, battleships, fighter planes and guns. This created thousands of jobs and large government contracts for businesses. In 1935 conscription was introduced: men aged 18–25 had to join the armed forces for at least two years. Within five years the army grew from about 100,000 to 1.4 million.

“Invisible” unemployment

Official figures did not tell the whole story. Women who gave up work to have a family were not counted, part-time workers were counted as full-time, and jobs taken from Jews and given to unemployed non-Jews reduced the official total without creating genuinely new jobs.

7.2
The Nazi economy

Did Hitler make Germany self-sufficient?

Hitler wanted a powerful Germany ready for war. That meant controlling the economy, increasing military production and trying to reduce dependence on foreign raw materials.

The economy under Schacht

Hitler appointed banker Hjalmar Schacht as Minister of Economics. Germany lacked raw materials such as iron, steel, rubber and wood, so Schacht made deals with countries in South America and south-east Europe to exchange German manufactured goods for raw materials. Production and employment rose, but Germany remained dependent on foreign supplies.

The Four Year Plan

In 1936 Hermann Goering introduced the Four Year Plan to prepare Germany for war. Military production was prioritised and large quantities of weapons, equipment and uniforms were ordered. Targets were achieved in some industries such as steel and explosives, but not in others such as oil.

Self-sufficiency

The Nazis wanted Germany to rely on its own resources. Scientists developed substitutes: petrol from coal, artificial wool and cotton from pulped wood, make-up from flour and coffee from acorns. Even so, by 1939 Germany still needed raw materials from abroad and was not fully ready for a long war.

Did the Nazis help farmers?

Farmers had been important Nazi supporters. Hitler guaranteed prices and protected farms from being taken away because of debt. Laws also stopped farms being divided between children, keeping farms under one family’s control for generations. Some farmers disliked this because children who did not inherit could be forced to leave for work in towns.

Judgement: Germany became more prepared for war and military production increased, but the textbook makes clear that Germany had not achieved full self-sufficiency by 1939.
7.3
Workers under Nazism

Were Germans better off under the Nazis?

The Nazis created jobs, but trade unions and political parties were banned. Workers received some benefits while losing important rights and freedoms.

German Labour Front (DAF)

The DAF replaced trade unions and claimed to protect workers’ rights and improve conditions. Workers could not quit without government permission and were banned from striking. People could also be forced to work as many hours as the Nazis required.

Beauty of Labour (SDA)

Schönheit der Arbeit tried to improve workplaces with better lighting and safety equipment, new washrooms, low-cost canteens and sports facilities.

Strength through Joy (KDF)

Kraft durch Freude organised leisure activities to encourage hard work. Rewards included cheap holidays, theatre trips and football tickets, although expensive holidays such as cruises or skiing were beyond most working-class Germans.

The Volkswagen promise

Workers could save for a “People’s Car” through a stamp scheme. A car cost 990 marks. Workers saved 5 marks a week, but when war began the money was used to make weapons instead and no saver received a car through the scheme.

Quick judgement

Were workers better off?

7.4
1939–45

The impact of war

Early German victories made the impact of war seem limited at first, but defeat in the USSR, shortages, Total War and Allied bombing increasingly transformed life on the home front.

Rationing and shortages

By November 1939, food and clothing were rationed. People were limited to one egg per week, and soap and toilet paper became scarce. Hot water was rationed to two days per week. The Nazis called on Germans to commit themselves fully to winning the war.

Turning point: the USSR

Germany invaded the USSR in 1941. The advance eventually stalled, and at Stalingrad over 80,000 Germans died and 90,000 surrendered. By early 1944 Germany was clearly heading towards defeat.

Total War and labour shortages

In 1942 Albert Speer became Armaments Minister and organised Germany for Total War: everything focused on weapons and food for soldiers. Many men were fighting, women were drafted to work, and by 1944 around seven million foreign workers had been brought to Germany, many as slave labour.

Bombing and refugees

From 1942, Britain and America heavily bombed German cities. Electricity, water and transport were disrupted, people fled damaged homes, and medical services were stretched. Bombing also caused flooding and gas explosions, and unexploded bombs remained dangerous. Nazi support began to weaken.

Overall: The war changed from early optimism to severe hardship. By the later war years, rationing, long hours, labour mobilisation, bombing, homelessness and fear were part of everyday life.

1 · Describe two ways in which Hitler reduced unemployment in Germany.

4 marks

Tip

  • Give two distinct methods.
  • Add precise supporting detail for each.
  • You could use RAD, public works, rearmament or conscription.
Revision marking points

Possible methods include the RAD, which required 18–25-year-old men to do six months’ labour; public works such as autobahn construction; rearmament, which created jobs in weapons and military industries; and conscription from 1935, which moved large numbers of young men into the armed forces.

2 · Explain why Germany had not become fully self-sufficient by 1939.

8 marks

Tip

  • Explain what self-sufficiency meant.
  • Use Schacht and the Four Year Plan.
  • Explain the continuing problem of imported raw materials.
Revision marking points

Germany increased military production and developed substitutes, but it lacked important raw materials such as oil, rubber and other resources. Schacht therefore made foreign trade deals, showing continued dependence on imports. The Four Year Plan met some production targets but not all, particularly in areas such as oil. By 1939 Germany was still dependent on foreign raw materials and was not prepared for a long war.

3 · Which group was more affected by Nazi economic policies: farmers and agricultural workers or industrial and factory workers?

12 marks

Tip

  • Explain effects on both groups.
  • For farmers: prices, debt protection and inheritance rules.
  • For workers: employment, DAF, loss of rights, SDA/KDF and working conditions.
  • Finish with a clear judgement.
Revision marking points

Farmers benefited from guaranteed prices and protection against losing farms through debt, but inheritance rules restricted how land could be divided and affected younger children. Industrial workers gained jobs and some workplace/leisure benefits through SDA and KDF, but trade unions were abolished, strikes were banned, workers could not freely leave jobs and hours could be increased. A strong answer compares the scale and significance of these changes before reaching a supported judgement.

4 · Explain how the Second World War affected the lives of German people.

8 marks

Tip

  • Use several linked effects: rationing, labour shortages, Total War and bombing.
  • Explain how conditions worsened as the war went on.
  • Use dates or examples where possible.
Revision marking points

Food and clothing were rationed from 1939 and shortages worsened. Total War increased working demands and drew women and foreign labour into factories. Allied bombing from 1942 destroyed homes and infrastructure, created refugees, deaths and injuries, and stretched medical services. As military defeat became clearer, morale and support for the Nazis weakened.
