P9.1 · textbook pages 112–113

Speed and distance–time graphs

Distance is how far an object travels. Speed tells you how much distance is travelled per unit time. A distance–time graph has distance on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. Its gradient is speed.

Learn

What you need to master

  • Be able to calculate speed, distance and time using v = s/t.
  • Explain why the gradient of distance–time is speed.
  • Recognise a flat section as stationary, not slow motion.
  • Convert minutes and hours into seconds when the equation needs seconds.
Exam technique: Write the equation, substitute values with units, calculate, and give the final unit.

Speed equation

speed = distance ÷ time, v = s/t. Rearrange to s = vt and t = s/v. Use metres and seconds for m/s.

Reading a distance–time graph

Horizontal line = stationary; straight rising line = constant speed; steeper line = faster speed. For a curved line, speed changes.

How to find gradient

Pick two points on a straight section. Gradient = change in distance ÷ change in time. Example: 1000 m in 40 s = 25 m/s.

Worked example — a delivery van

A van travels 1800 m in 60 s. Speed = 1800 ÷ 60 = 30 m/s. In the next 300 s at this speed, it covers 30 × 300 = 9000 m.

Distance–time graph: gradient = change in distance ÷ change in time.

P9.2 · textbook pages 114–115

Velocity and acceleration

Velocity is speed in a given direction. Changing speed or direction changes velocity. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Negative acceleration means the acceleration acts in the negative direction (and may be a deceleration).

Learn

What you need to master

  • Know that velocity involves direction.
  • Calculate acceleration and deceleration with correct signs and units.
  • Explain why circular motion at constant speed still involves acceleration.
  • Interpret a straight rising velocity–time graph as constant acceleration.
Exam technique: Write the equation, substitute values with units, calculate, and give the final unit.

Speed versus velocity

Speed is scalar. Velocity is vector. Two cars moving at 30 m/s north and 30 m/s south have equal speed but different velocities.

Acceleration formula

a = (v − u)/t; u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, t = time. Units are m/s².

Deceleration

A car moving forward at 28 m/s that slows to 0 in 8 s has a = (0 − 28) ÷ 8 = −3.5 m/s². Its deceleration magnitude is 3.5 m/s².

Worked example — accelerating car

The velocity rises from 8 m/s to 28 m/s over 16 s in a straight line. a = (28 − 8) ÷ 16 = 1.25 m/s².

a = (v − u) / t    (m/s²)

P9.3 · textbook pages 116–117

More about velocity–time graphs

On a velocity–time graph the gradient is acceleration. A flat line represents constant velocity and therefore zero acceleration. For motion in one direction with positive velocity, the area beneath the line gives distance travelled; in general, signed area gives displacement.

Learn

What you need to master

  • Find acceleration from the gradient of a velocity–time graph.
  • Calculate distance using rectangle and triangle areas.
  • Describe acceleration, steady speed and deceleration.
  • Recognise that negative velocity requires care: signed area is displacement, not always total distance.
Exam technique: Write the equation, substitute values with units, calculate, and give the final unit.

The gradient

acceleration = change in velocity ÷ change in time. Rising line = positive acceleration; falling line = negative acceleration.

The area under the graph

For v–t graphs: rectangle area = base × height; triangle area = ½ × base × height; area units are (seconds × m/s) = metres.

When a vehicle brakes

A car moves at 20 m/s for 10 s then decelerates steadily to rest in 5 s. Distance = (20 × 10) + (½ × 5 × 20) = 200 + 50 = 250 m.

Area method — braking

Do not multiply the full 15 s by 20 m/s! The first 10 s form a rectangle and the last 5 s form a triangle.

Distance = rectangle area + triangle area = 200 + 50 = 250 m.

P9.4 · textbook pages 118–119

Analysing motion graphs

Graph questions often mix methods: on a distance–time graph, speed is the gradient; on a velocity–time graph, acceleration is the gradient and displacement is the signed area. For a curved distance–time graph, draw a tangent to find instantaneous speed.

Learn

What you need to master

  • Select the correct graph formula; do not confuse gradient and area.
  • Explain instantaneous speed using a tangent.
  • Calculate distance from velocity–time area.
  • Use v² − u² = 2as when time is not given (Higher).
Exam technique: Write the equation, substitute values with units, calculate, and give the final unit.

Distance–time, straight line

Find change in distance / change in time for constant speed. If the line is curved, the gradient changes.

Tangent to a curve

Draw a straight tangent at the point. Use two well-separated points on your tangent to calculate instantaneous speed.

Higher-tier equation

For constant acceleration: v² − u² = 2as. This is useful when time is not known. Also s = ½(u+v)t for constant acceleration.

Units and interpretation

Convert km/h to m/s by dividing by 3.6. Label axes and gradients clearly. If v is negative, signed area represents displacement.

Worked example — constant acceleration

An object increases velocity from 4 to 12 m/s in 10 s. Acceleration = (12 − 4)/10 = 0.8 m/s². Displacement = ½(4 + 12) × 10 = 80 m. The same result follows from the area under the velocity–time graph.

v² − u² = 2as    and    s = ½(u + v)t
