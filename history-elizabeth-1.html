1.1
From princess to queen

Who was Elizabeth I?

Elizabeth's childhood was full of uncertainty. Those experiences help explain the cautious, political ruler she later became.

Born into the Tudors

Elizabeth was born in 1533, the daughter of Henry VIII and his second wife, Anne Boleyn.

Not expected to rule

Henry wanted a male heir. After Anne Boleyn's execution in 1536, Elizabeth's parents' marriage was treated as invalid and Elizabeth was not expected to become queen.

Queen at 25

After the reigns of her half-brother Edward VI and half-sister Mary I, Elizabeth succeeded Mary in 1558.

Succession story: Edward VI became king in 1547. Mary I became queen in 1553. Elizabeth succeeded Mary in 1558. Elizabeth's path to the throne was therefore far from straightforward.

Education and court life

Elizabeth received an excellent education in subjects and languages and was prepared for life at the royal court. As a princess she could also be valuable in a political marriage.

Years of danger

Under Mary I, Elizabeth was viewed with suspicion and was imprisoned in the Tower of London in 1554 after being accused of supporting a rebellion. She survived years in which powerful people could quickly fall from favour.

Think like a historian

Experience → possible impact

Elizabeth spent years under suspicion during Mary's reign. Which later behaviour does this most help explain?

Big idea: We cannot know exactly what Elizabeth thought because of each childhood event. In an exam, phrase the connection carefully: her experiences may have contributed to her caution over threats, marriage and powerful rivals.
1.2
Government and influence

Who held the power in Elizabethan England?

Elizabeth was queen, but England was governed through a network of advisers, Parliament and local officials. The key question is not only “what did they do?” but “how much power did they really have?”

At the centre

Elizabeth I

The monarch retained final authority. Access to the queen mattered enormously.

Control through reward

How patronage worked

Elizabeth→titles, offices & rewards→status & access→loyalty and influence

Patronage allowed Elizabeth to reward supporters and encourage loyalty. Because court access and royal favour mattered, losing the queen's favour could be politically damaging.

Key minister

William Cecil (1520–1598)

Elizabeth's most trusted adviser. He served as Secretary of State and was involved in Parliament and the Privy Council. He advised on major political, religious and foreign-policy issues and helped develop the Poor Laws and religious policies.

Key minister

Francis Walsingham (c1532–1590)

Secretary of State and one of Elizabeth's closest advisers from 1573. Famous as her “spymaster”, he gathered intelligence and played an important role in dealing with threats, including the case against Mary, Queen of Scots.

1 · Explain what was important about the Privy Council for how Elizabethan England was ruled.

8 marks

How to build an 8-mark “importance” answer

  • Make a clear point about why the Privy Council mattered.
  • Add precise supporting knowledge: what it did, who served on it, or an example.
  • Explain the consequence: how did that affect government?
  • Develop another distinct reason. Two developed explanations are stronger than a long list.
Useful structure: “The Privy Council was important because… For example… This mattered for the government of England because…”
✗ Description only
The Privy Council advised Elizabeth.
✓ Explanation
Turn the fact into an argument: explain how advice from experienced councillors affected Elizabeth's ability to govern and make decisions.
Revision marking points — not an official AQA mark scheme

A strong answer could develop relevant points such as the Council's day-to-day role, its advice on major issues, the expertise of key councillors such as Cecil, and the relationship between councillors' influence and Elizabeth's final authority. Reward explanation of importance, not a list of jobs.

2 · Explain two ways Elizabeth's experiences before 1558 may have influenced her as queen.

8 marks

Writing tip

  • Choose two different experiences.
  • For each one: identify the event → give accurate detail → explain a plausible effect on Elizabeth's later approach.
  • Avoid claiming you can prove what Elizabeth was thinking. Use careful historical language such as “may have made her…” or “could have contributed to…”.
Revision marking points — original revision question

Relevant evidence could include Anne Boleyn's execution, Elizabeth's uncertain position in the succession, the reigns of Edward and Mary, her imprisonment in 1554, religious tension and years of suspicion. The key is to explain a credible link between the experience and later caution, political judgement or attitudes to threats and marriage.

3 · Write an account of how Elizabeth became queen despite not originally being expected to inherit the throne.

6 marks

For an account question

  • Keep a clear chronological chain.
  • Select events that actually move the story forward.
  • Use links such as “this meant…”, “as a result…” and “therefore…” rather than producing disconnected dates.
Revision marking points — original revision question

A coherent account could connect Elizabeth's birth and uncertain status to Henry VIII's male heir Edward, Edward's accession, Mary's accession in 1553, Elizabeth's survival during Mary's reign, and Mary's death in 1558 leading to Elizabeth's accession.

4 · Give one feature of patronage at Elizabeth's court.

4 marks

Short-answer tip

Name a specific feature, then add enough accurate detail to show you understand it. Don't turn a 4-mark response into an essay.

Revision marking points — original revision question

Relevant knowledge includes Elizabeth granting titles, offices or rewards; the importance of royal favour and access; and patronage being used to encourage loyalty and support.
