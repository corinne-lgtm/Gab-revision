P4 Electric circuits

Five textbook topics, working circuit diagrams, I–V graphs, required practicals and Higher GCSE exam practice.

P4.1 · textbook pages 46–47

Current and charge

Current is the rate of flow of electric charge. A cell provides potential difference, driving charge around a complete circuit. Conventional current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal outside the cell.

Learn

Charge flow

Q = I × t. Charge Q is in coulombs (C), current I in amperes (A), time t in seconds (s).

Circuit instruments

An ammeter measures current and must be placed in series. A voltmeter measures potential difference and must be connected in parallel.

Components

Recognise the circuit symbols for a cell, battery, switch, lamp, resistor, variable resistor, diode, LED, fuse, ammeter and voltmeter.

Interactive circuit symbols and measurement

P4.2 · textbook pages 48–49

Potential difference and resistance

Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge; resistance limits the flow of charge. A fixed resistor at constant temperature has a current directly proportional to potential difference (Ohm’s law).

Learn

Potential difference

V = E ÷ Q. One volt equals one joule transferred per coulomb.

Resistance

R = V ÷ I. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω). Rearrange to V = IR or I = V/R.

Ohmic conductor

At constant temperature, the I–V graph is a straight line through the origin. If V is plotted against I, the gradient is resistance.

Exam tip: {tip}

Ohm’s law explorer

6 V ÷ 10 Ω = 0.60 A

Required practical: wire length

Keep the material and diameter constant, and prevent wire heating. At constant temperature, resistance increases approximately in proportion to length.

50 cm → model resistance 5.0 Ω
Illustrative proportional model: 0.1 Ω/cm, not actual experimental data.
P4.3 · textbook pages 50–51

Component characteristics

Different components have different current–potential difference characteristics: an ohmic resistor, a filament lamp and a diode behave differently. Thermistors and light-dependent resistors are sensors.

Learn

Filament lamp

As current rises, the filament becomes hotter. Its resistance rises, so the I–V graph curves and becomes less steep at high positive voltage.

Diode / LED

A diode allows appreciable current mainly in one direction; in reverse it has very high resistance. An LED emits light when forward biased.

Sensors

For a typical NTC thermistor, temperature ↑ → resistance ↓. For an LDR, light intensity ↑ → resistance ↓.

Exam tip: {tip}

Interactive current–potential difference graphs

Choose a component. Current is on the vertical axis; potential difference is on the horizontal axis. Curves are illustrative, not measured data.

Fixed resistor: a straight line through the origin at constant temperature.

Required practical: use an ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel, change the variable resistor, record (V, I) pairs, reverse polarity for negative readings, and compare curves.

P4.4 · textbook pages 52–53

Series circuits

In a series circuit the same current passes through every component. The supply potential difference is shared, and resistances add together.

Learn

Current rule

I₁ = I₂ = I₃ everywhere in a single-loop circuit.

Potential difference

Vtotal = V₁ + V₂ + … across series components. Cells arranged in the same direction add their potential differences.

Resistance

Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + … . Adding a resistor in series increases total resistance and decreases current for the same supply voltage.

Exam tip: {tip}

Series circuit calculator

One current, shared potential difference, resistances added.

P4.5 · textbook pages 54–55

Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit the potential difference is the same across each branch. The total current is the sum of the branch currents. Adding a parallel resistor lowers the total resistance.

Learn

Potential difference

Vsource = V₁ = V₂ = … across all branches.

Current junction rule

Itotal = I₁ + I₂ + … . Charge is conserved at every junction.

Resistance

In parallel, total resistance is smaller than the smallest individual resistance; at the same voltage, another branch provides an extra path for current.

Exam tip: {tip}

Parallel branch simulator

Every branch has the supply p.d.; currents add at the junction. Adjust the resistances.
