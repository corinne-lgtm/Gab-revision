8.1
Education & youth

How did the Nazis change the lives of young people?

Hitler wanted young people to grow up loyal to him and the Nazi Party. The Nazis therefore tried to control both education and young people’s free time.

The Nazi school system

School subjects were used for Nazi propaganda and indoctrination. History stressed German military victories and the unfairness of Germany’s treatment after the First World War. Geography focused on lands Germany might conquer. Science concentrated on weapons and chemical warfare, while Maths problems could promote Nazi ideas.

Race Studies (Eugenics) taught racist and unscientific ideas about a supposed “master race”, antisemitism and the inferiority of disabled people and other groups.

Teachers, textbooks and elite schools

Textbooks were rewritten to reflect Nazi beliefs. Teachers were expected to join the German Teachers’ League and teach Nazi ideas. Promising pupils could be sent to Napolas or Adolf Hitler Schools, where academic work was combined with intense physical training.

Universities

Between 1933 and 1934, 15% of university lecturers or professors were replaced: a third for racial reasons and half for political reasons. By 1939, over 3,000 had been dismissed. University courses were altered to reflect Nazi beliefs, including antisemitism.

Hitler Youth

Boys progressed through Nazi youth groups and joined the Hitler Youth from 14 to 18. Activities stressed marching, fighting, shooting, fitness, competition and leadership to prepare boys for soldiering. The organisation gained equal status with school and home in 1936, and membership became compulsory in 1939.

German girls

Girls joined the Young Girls and then the League of German Girls. They attended camps and did physical training, but also learned cooking, childcare and household skills to prepare for motherhood. Boys and girls were encouraged to report parents or teachers who criticised Hitler.

Not everyone supported Nazi youth groups

Some young Germans resisted. The Swing Youth listened to American jazz and had Jewish friends. The Edelweiss Pirates went camping, mocked Hitler and sometimes physically attacked Hitler Youth groups.

8.2
Family & gender

Women in Nazi Germany

The Nazis wanted women to focus on family life and motherhood, summed up by the idea of Kinder, Kirche und Küche — children, church and cooking.

The place of women

Before 1933 many women worked in professional jobs and had political rights. Nazi ideology was very different. Female doctors, teachers, lawyers and judges were removed or pushed out of work. Women were discouraged from wearing trousers or high heels and from smoking, while motherhood was strongly encouraged.

Encouraging motherhood

Newly married couples could receive loans equivalent to a year’s wages. A quarter of the loan was cancelled for each child. Contraception and abortion were banned, and mothers with many children could receive the Motherhood Medal. The Nazis also established the Lebensborn movement to increase births of children they considered racially desirable.

Not all women were encouraged to have children

The Nazis used eugenic ideas. The Law for the Prevention of Diseased Offspring allowed compulsory sterilisation of women considered to have hereditary illness, disease or “anti-social” behaviour.

Were the policies successful?

Births rose from about 970,000 in 1933 to 1,413,000 in 1939. However, as more men entered the army and war began, women were increasingly needed in factories. In practice, the Nazi ideal of women staying at home conflicted with economic and wartime needs.

8.3
Churches

The Nazis and religion

Christianity was Germany’s largest religion. Some Christians initially supported Hitler, but Nazi attempts to control churches produced growing tension and opposition.

Why did some Christians support the Nazis?

The Nazis publicly praised marriage, family and moral values; Christians feared atheistic communism; and Hitler initially promised to respect the Catholic Church.

The Catholic Church

In 1933 Hitler signed a Concordat with the Pope, promising not to interfere with the Church. He later broke it: Catholic priests were harassed and arrested and youth clubs and schools were closed. In 1937 the Pope condemned Nazi hostility to Christ and the Church. Bishop Galen later criticised Nazi terror, euthanasia and concentration camps.

The Protestant Church

Pro-Nazi German Christians, led by Ludwig Müller, tried to bring Protestantism under Nazi control. Opponents formed the Confessional Church, led by Pastor Martin Niemöller. Around 800 pastors were arrested; Niemöller was sent to a concentration camp and the Confessional Church was banned.

Other Christian groups

Witnesses were sent to concentration camps because they were pacifists and refused military service. The Salvation Army, Christian Scientists and Seventh Day Adventist Church disappeared from Germany.

Key contrast: Nazi beliefs glorified strength, violence and racial hierarchy, whereas Christianity emphasised love, forgiveness, helping the weak and equality before God.
8.4
Racism & persecution

Nazi ideas and racial policy

The Nazis claimed that “Aryan” Germans belonged to a superior master race and persecuted groups they labelled racially inferior or “undesirable”.

Nazi racism

Nazi racial ideology targeted Jews, Roma and Sinti, Slavs and black people, as well as disabled people. The regime claimed that Germany would be stronger if groups it considered “inferior” were removed from society.

Persecution of “undesirables”

Concentration camps held Jewish people, people from groups the Nazis considered racially inferior, gay people, criminals, prostitutes, Jehovah’s Witnesses, political opponents, pacifists and homeless people. Around 400,000 people with hereditary illnesses were sterilised and over 200,000 people with physical or learning disabilities were killed.

8.5
Persecution → mass murder

The journey to the “Final Solution”

Nazi persecution of Jews escalated from discrimination and exclusion to ghettos, mass shootings and the systematic murder of Europe’s Jewish population.

Escalating persecution

After 1933 Jews were used as scapegoats for Germany’s problems. Laws excluded them from government jobs, medicine, teaching and journalism. The Nuremberg Laws intensified legal discrimination. During Kristallnacht in November 1938, Jewish homes, shops and synagogues were attacked and thousands were sent to concentration camps.

The impact of war

German conquest brought millions more Jews under Nazi rule. Many were forced into ghettos or labour camps. Mobile killing units called Einsatzgruppen carried out mass shootings. By the end of 1941 Nazi leaders were planning what they called a “final solution to the Jewish question”.

Wannsee and extermination camps

At the Wannsee Conference in January 1942, Nazi leaders discussed the murder of Europe’s Jews — around 11 million people — through forced labour and killing. Extermination camps included Auschwitz-Birkenau, Belzec, Chelmno, Majdanek, Sobibor and Treblinka. Jews from across occupied Europe were transported to them.

Resistance

Jewish people resisted in different ways. Secret schools, theatres and places of worship operated in some ghettos. In 1943 the Warsaw ghetto rebelled against the Nazis. There were also rebellions in death camps, including Treblinka in 1943.

Scale: Around six million Jews were killed by Nazi Germany and its collaborators. The textbook notes that this mass murder is commonly known as the Holocaust, while some prefer the Hebrew term Shoah.

1 · Describe two main features of the education of children in Nazi Germany.

4 marks

Tip

  • Choose two important features.
  • Add specific detail to each.
  • You could use curriculum changes, racial education, teachers or elite schools.
Revision marking points

Possible features include rewriting the curriculum and textbooks to indoctrinate pupils with Nazi ideas; teaching racist eugenics and antisemitism; forcing teachers to promote Nazi beliefs; and using elite schools such as Napolas and Adolf Hitler Schools to train future Nazi leaders.

2 · In what ways were the lives of women in Germany affected by Nazi social policies?

8 marks

Tip

  • Do not simply list policies — explain their effects.
  • Use employment, marriage loans, motherhood, contraception/abortion and eugenics.
  • Remember that wartime needs changed the reality.
Revision marking points

Women were encouraged to leave professional employment and focus on marriage and motherhood. Marriage loans and medals rewarded larger families, while contraception and abortion were restricted. Nazi eugenics also meant some women were forcibly sterilised. However, labour shortages and war later increased the need for women to work, exposing a gap between Nazi ideology and economic reality.

3 · Describe how the Nazis gained control over German Christians.

4 marks

Tip

  • Give specific examples from different Christian groups.
  • Use the Concordat, German Christians, Confessional Church or arrests.
Revision marking points

The Nazis initially made agreements such as the 1933 Concordat, then interfered with Catholic organisations and arrested priests. Among Protestants they promoted the pro-Nazi German Christians under Ludwig Müller. Opposition through the Confessional Church was suppressed: pastors were arrested, Niemöller was sent to a concentration camp and the Church was banned.

4 · Describe two examples of armed resistance by Jews to the Nazis.

4 marks

Tip

  • Focus on exactly two examples.
  • Identify each event and add precise detail.
Revision marking points

One example was the Warsaw ghetto uprising in 1943, when Jews rebelled against German forces. Another was resistance in extermination camps: at Treblinka in 1943 prisoners seized weapons, attacked guards and around 150 escaped, although many were later recaptured or killed.

5 · Which interpretation of the Hitler Youth is more convincing?

8 marks

Tip

  • Compare the interpretations, not just the topic.
  • Use provenance and content.
  • Test each interpretation against your knowledge of Hitler Youth activities and opposition.
  • Reach a supported judgement.
Revision marking points

A strong answer should recognise that experiences differed. Some young people enjoyed camps, sport and comradeship, while others disliked military drill and loss of freedom. Knowledge of compulsory membership from 1939 and opposition from groups such as the Swing Youth and Edelweiss Pirates can be used to evaluate how convincing each interpretation is.
