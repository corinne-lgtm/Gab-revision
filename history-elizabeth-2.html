2.1
Authority in a male world

Why was it difficult to be a female ruler?

Elizabeth became queen in 1558, aged 25. Her sex did not make successful rule impossible, but it created political expectations and practical problems she had to manage.

👑 Succession

Elizabeth needed to secure the future of the Tudor dynasty. Without a child, the identity of the next monarch remained uncertain.

💍 Marriage

Many expected a queen to marry. But a husband could bring an heir and create a new problem: how much power would he have?

⚖️ Authority

Elizabeth ruled over powerful male nobles, councillors and MPs. She had to make clear that final authority remained with the monarch.

Explore Elizabeth's five connected problems

Select a problem to see why it mattered.

Mary, Queen of Scots

Mary was Elizabeth's Catholic cousin and had a claim to the English throne. For Catholics who rejected Elizabeth's religious settlement, Mary could become an alternative monarch.

Foreign danger

France and Spain were major Catholic powers. Elizabeth needed to think about alliances, religion and security at the same time — marriage could affect all three.

2.2A
Marriage and succession

Why was marriage so important?

Marriage could solve some of Elizabeth's problems, but it could just as easily create new ones. That is why the question remained politically explosive.

Reasons to marry

  • Produce an heir and secure the succession.
  • Create a useful foreign alliance.
  • Fit contemporary expectations about monarchy and family.
  • A husband might provide support in government or war.

Reasons not to marry

  • A foreign husband could pull England into his country's interests or conflicts.
  • An English husband could create faction and jealousy at court.
  • Marriage risked reducing Elizabeth's independence and authority.
  • Childbirth was dangerous; Elizabeth knew her sister Mary had suffered false pregnancies and her stepmother Katherine Parr had died after childbirth.

Suitor selector

Select each possible match. The key question is not “who did Elizabeth love?” but what each marriage could mean politically.

Decision challenge

Claim: “Marriage was the obvious solution to Elizabeth's problems.”

2.2B
Queen versus pressure

Marriage, Parliament and Elizabeth's independence

Marriage was not simply private. Councillors and MPs worried about succession, but Elizabeth resisted attempts to turn that concern into control over her decision.

Parliament's concern

No heir meant uncertainty about the succession. MPs and councillors could argue that marriage was a matter of national security, not just Elizabeth's personal choice.

⇄

Elizabeth's response

Elizabeth guarded the royal prerogative. She could listen to advice while insisting that the decision to marry — and whom to marry — belonged to the queen.

Robert Dudley

An English favourite and close companion. A marriage avoided a foreign husband, but Dudley was controversial and an English match risked faction at court.

Philip II of Spain

A powerful Catholic monarch and Mary's widower. The match offered international weight, but religion, foreign influence and England's independence made it deeply problematic.

François, Duke of Anjou

A French Catholic prince. Negotiations could be diplomatically useful, but the match was unpopular with many English Protestants and Elizabeth never married him.

Exam link: significance, not gossip

For GCSE, the important issue is how marriage connected to succession, foreign policy, religion, Parliament and royal authority. A list of suitors is not enough.

2.3
Catholic challenge

Norfolk's rebellion and the Ridolfi Plot

The Duke of Norfolk was a leading noble with a Catholic family background. His involvement in plots around Mary, Queen of Scots showed how succession, religion and foreign intervention could combine.

First warning: the Northern Rebellion, 1569

Northern Catholic nobles rebelled against Elizabeth. Norfolk did not lead the rebellion itself, but he had been linked to a plan to marry Mary, Queen of Scots. The crisis increased suspicion around him and around Mary's claim.

The Ridolfi Plot, 1571 — follow the chain

1 · PlanRoberto Ridolfi, an Italian banker, acted as a go-between in a Catholic conspiracy.
2 · Foreign helpThe plot looked for Spanish and papal support for an invasion of England.
3 · Replace ElizabethThe conspirators hoped to remove Elizabeth, put Mary on the throne and link her to Norfolk.
4 · DiscoveryElizabeth's government uncovered the conspiracy. Norfolk was convicted of treason and executed in 1572.

Why it mattered

The plot was dangerous because it linked an English noble, Mary's rival claim, Catholic opposition and the possibility of foreign invasion. It also showed the government why intelligence and surveillance mattered.

2.4
A favourite turns rebel

Essex's rebellion, 1601

Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex, rose rapidly at court and became one of Elizabeth's favourites. His fall shows how patronage, rivalry and royal authority had changed by the end of the reign.

Rise

Essex was young, ambitious and favoured by Elizabeth. Court favour gave him status and opportunities.

Conflict

He clashed with rivals around Robert Cecil. In Ireland in 1599 he failed to achieve what Elizabeth expected and returned to England against her orders.

Financial pressure

Elizabeth did not renew his monopoly on sweet wines. With heavy debts, Essex lost an important source of income and patronage.

Essex's rebellion — what actually happened?

1 · February 1601Essex gathered supporters and hoped to challenge his enemies at court.
2 · Into LondonHe left Essex House with supporters, expecting wider backing.
3 · Support collapsesLondon did not rise for him. The government stayed in control and his group dispersed.
4 · PunishmentEssex was arrested, tried for treason and executed. The rebellion had failed quickly.

What does Essex tell us about Elizabeth's authority?

It is tempting to say “a rebellion = Elizabeth was weak”. A stronger judgement is more nuanced.

2.5
Big judgement

Why did rebellions against Elizabeth fail?

The plots were real threats, but none overthrew Elizabeth. Compare the recurring reasons and decide which mattered most.

Norfolk / Ridolfi

Strength: elite connections, Mary, Catholic cause and hopes of foreign backing.

Weakness: secrecy was vulnerable; the government uncovered the conspiracy before a successful invasion or change of regime could happen.

Essex

Strength: a famous noble with followers and court connections.

Weakness: he failed to win broad support in London; the government responded rapidly and his force collapsed.

Rank the recurring factors

Click the factor you think is most important. There is no automatic “correct” choice — the exam skill is to justify the judgement with evidence.

End-of-reign judgement

Essex's rebellion shows tension and faction at court, but its rapid failure also shows that Elizabeth's government still had enough authority, loyalty and administrative control to defeat a challenge in 1601.

1 · Write an account of the difficulties Elizabeth faced as a female ruler.

8 marks

How to build an 8-mark account

1 · Select
Choose 2–3 connected difficulties, not a shopping list.
2 · Develop
Give precise knowledge: succession, marriage, Parliament, Mary, religion or foreign danger.
3 · Link
Explain how one difficulty created or intensified another.
Useful structure: “One difficulty was… This was serious because… This also created the problem of… Therefore…”
Revision marking points — original revision question

A strong answer could develop linked difficulties such as expectations about female authority; pressure to marry; the succession problem if Elizabeth remained childless; the danger that a husband could gain influence; Mary, Queen of Scots as an alternative claimant; religious division; and the foreign-policy consequences of marriage. Reward explanation of connections, not just identification.

2 · Explain what was important about Elizabeth's decision not to marry.

8 marks

For an “importance” question

  • Do not just explain why she did not marry. Explain what difference the decision made.
  • Develop at least two consequences or areas of significance.
  • Use specific context: succession, Parliament, diplomacy, religion and Elizabeth's personal authority.
Paragraph frame: “Elizabeth's decision was important for ___ because… This meant… Therefore its significance was…”
Revision marking points — original revision question

Relevant significance includes continued uncertainty over the succession; repeated parliamentary/councillor concern; Elizabeth retaining personal political independence; the use of marriage negotiations in diplomacy; avoiding some dangers of an English or foreign husband; and the continuing importance of rival claimants such as Mary.

3 · Write an account of how the Ridolfi Plot threatened Elizabeth's rule.

8 marks

Account question = a developed chain

  • Start with the conspiracy and its aims.
  • Explain how Mary, Norfolk and hoped-for foreign support made the threat more serious.
  • Finish with discovery and consequences. Keep the sequence causal.
Avoid: four disconnected facts. Aim for: “Because… this made it possible that… which threatened Elizabeth because…”
Revision marking points — original revision question

Useful knowledge includes Ridolfi's role as a go-between; the plan involving Mary; Norfolk's connection; hoped-for Spanish/papal assistance and invasion; the aim of replacing Elizabeth; discovery by the government; and Norfolk's execution in 1572. The strongest answers explain why these elements together increased the danger.

4 · How convincing is the view that Essex rebelled mainly because Elizabeth had destroyed his position at court?

8 marks

How to answer an interpretation-style 8 marker

  • Make a judgement about the claim, not about who wrote it.
  • Use accurate contextual knowledge to test it.
  • Consider evidence that supports it and evidence that makes the explanation incomplete.
  • Keep returning to the wording: “This makes the view convincing / less convincing because…”
Good evidence: Ireland, Robert Cecil/court rivalry, loss of the sweet-wine monopoly, debts, and Essex's failed attempt to gain wider support.
Revision marking points — original revision question

The claim can be supported by Essex's loss of favour, rivalry with Robert Cecil, the failure in Ireland, his unauthorised return and the non-renewal of the sweet-wine monopoly, which worsened his financial position. A balanced evaluation can also consider Essex's own ambition, poor judgement and the fact that he chose rebellion but failed to attract broad support.

5 · Write an account of why rebellions and plots against Elizabeth failed.

8 marks

Turn factors into explanation

  • Choose 2–3 recurring reasons and attach evidence to each.
  • Compare examples: what can Norfolk/Ridolfi and Essex show?
  • Explain the mechanism: how did intelligence, weak support or government authority cause failure?
Instead of: “The government was strong.” Write: “Government intelligence exposed the Ridolfi conspiracy before its plans could be carried out, so…”
Revision marking points — original revision question

Possible factors include effective intelligence and discovery of plots; failure to secure broad popular support; loyalty to the established government; rapid government action; uncertain or unrealised foreign help; divisions and poor decisions among rebels; and Elizabeth's continuing authority. Evidence should be attached to the factor being explained.
