B10.1

Principles of homeostasis

Learn

What is homeostasis?

Homeostasis is the regulation of internal conditions to maintain optimum conditions for cell and enzyme function. Important examples include body temperature, water content and blood glucose concentration.

Control systems

Receptors detect stimuli. Coordination centres receive and process information. Effectors — muscles or glands — bring about responses.

Working together

Homeostatic control may involve nervous or hormonal responses. The overall pathway is stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response.

A control system detects a change, processes the information and produces a response.

B10.2

The structure and function of the human nervous system

Learn

Nervous coordination

Receptors detect changes in the environment. Electrical impulses travel along neurones. Sensory neurones carry impulses to the CNS; motor neurones carry impulses from the CNS to effectors.

CNS and effectors

The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord. Muscles respond by contracting; glands respond by secreting substances.

Required practical: reaction time

Reaction time can be measured using a ruler-drop test or digital method. Repeat measurements, calculate a mean and control variables such as participant position, hand used and testing conditions.

B10.3

Reflex actions

Learn

Rapid and automatic

Reflexes are rapid, automatic responses that protect the body or control basic functions. They do not require conscious processing before the response occurs.

The reflex arc

A typical pathway is stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector → response.

Synapses

At a synapse, the arriving impulse causes a neurotransmitter to be released. It diffuses across the gap, binds to receptors on the next neurone and triggers a new electrical impulse.

A reflex is coordinated quickly, usually through the spinal cord, before conscious awareness.
