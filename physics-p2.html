P2.1 · textbook pages 24–25

Energy transfer by conduction

Thermal energy moves through a solid from warmer to cooler regions. Metals normally have high thermal conductivity; materials such as wool, fibreglass, wood and trapped air are good insulators.

Learn

Conductors

Copper conducts better than steel; both are generally better conductors than glass or wood.

Insulators

Materials with low thermal conductivity transfer energy more slowly. Trapped air helps reduce the energy transfer rate.

Rate of transfer

The rate depends on the material’s thermal conductivity, the temperature difference and the thickness of the layer.

Learning objectives

  • Explain conduction without saying that energy disappears.
  • Compare identical rods heated at one end and note when wax melts.
  • For fair tests, control rod length, diameter and heating conditions.
Exam tip: Do not confuse conductivity with specific heat capacity.
P2.2 · textbook pages 26–27

Specific heat capacity

Specific heat capacity c is the energy required to increase the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C, without changing state. AQA Required Practical: measure it with an electric heater and an insulated block.

Learn

Equation

ΔE = mcΔθ. Rearrange to c = ΔE/(mΔθ).

Units

Energy ΔE in J, mass m in kg, temperature change Δθ in °C, c in J/(kg °C).

Examples

Water ≈ 4200; aluminium ≈ 900; copper ≈ 385 J/(kg °C). Higher c means more energy needed for the same mass and rise.

Learning objectives

  • Measure the mass of the sample accurately.
  • Measure energy input using a joulemeter, or V × I × t.
  • Record start and end temperature; insulate to reduce losses.
  • Explain why measured values can be inaccurate.
Exam tip: A temperature change of 1°C is the same size as a change of 1 K.
P2.3 · textbook pages 28–29

Heating and insulating buildings

Homes lose energy to the surroundings. Insulation slows this transfer and can lower heating costs. Always link the feature of an insulating material to the transfer pathway it reduces.

Learn

Loft insulation

Fibreglass traps air. The thicker the insulation and the lower its thermal conductivity, the lower the heat transfer rate.

Cavity walls and windows

Foam or fibre insulation traps air pockets inside cavities; double glazing separates panes with air, inert gas or a vacuum.

Other methods

Reflective foil behind radiators reduces infrared radiation into the wall. Thicker low-conductivity walls and plastic window frames also help.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how conduction and convection are reduced.
  • Evaluate insulation options using cost, savings and payback period.
  • Distinguish photovoltaic solar panels from solar thermal water-heating panels.
Exam tip: Simple payback = installation cost ÷ annual saving; it ignores interest and changing energy prices.
