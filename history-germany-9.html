Control and resistance

How did the Nazis control Germany — and how far did Germans resist?

9.1
Fear & terror

Repression and the police state

Nazi Germany was a police state. Fear of the SS, Gestapo, informers, courts and concentration camps made open opposition dangerous.

Himmler & the SS

Heinrich Himmler controlled all police forces and headed the SS. The SS had grown from Hitler’s bodyguard into a feared organisation.

  • SD: security service; could arrest, search and seize property.
  • Waffen SS: elite military unit.
  • Death’s Head Units: ran concentration camps and later death camps.

The Gestapo

The secret police wore no uniform. They could arrest, imprison without trial and torture. They depended heavily on denunciations and encouraged children to report parents or teachers who criticised the Nazis.

Courts & camps

Concentration camps held “enemies of the state”, including Jews, Roma and Sinti, political opponents and critics. Courts and judges were brought under Nazi control and new laws criminalised acts such as listening to foreign radio.

Exam brain: Nazi control did not require a Gestapo officer on every street. The belief that anyone might inform on you made fear itself a method of control.
9.2
Winning people over

Propaganda and censorship

The Nazis combined persuasion with control of information. Joseph Goebbels aimed to make Nazi ideas appear everywhere while restricting competing ideas.

Propaganda

Joseph Goebbels, Minister of Enlightenment and Propaganda, used repeated messages, powerful images and mass spectacle. Themes included blaming Jews, attacking Versailles and presenting Hitler and Germany as strong.

Repeat the message→Control what people see→Make Nazi ideas feel normal

Censorship

The regime controlled what Germans could hear, read and see. Material judged harmful to Nazism or Hitler was banned or restricted.

  • Newspapers: only approved stories printed.
  • Films: plots checked before production.
  • Radio: Nazi-controlled stations; cheap radios widened access.
  • Books, theatre, music: writers and performers were pressured to support Nazi ideas.
Key distinction: propaganda tries to persuade; censorship removes or limits alternative messages. The Nazis used both together.
9.3
Culture under control

How were the arts and culture affected by Nazi rule?

The Nazis tried to make culture carry the same political message: Nazi beliefs were correct and Hitler’s ideas served Germany.

Chamber of Culture

Run by Goebbels. Artists, musicians, writers and actors had to belong to it to work. Jews and others could be excluded.

Cinema & literature

Films were checked and expected to carry pro-Nazi messages. Books by Jewish authors and other banned writers were removed; public book burnings took place in 1933.

Music & theatre

German and classical music was promoted. Jewish composers were banned and jazz was rejected. Theatre and cabaret were restricted when their work did not fit Nazi ideas.

Art & design

Hitler rejected modern or abstract art as “degenerate”. Nazi-approved art was meant to be simple, realistic and heroic: healthy “pure” Germans, soldiers, workers, farmers and family scenes. The Bauhaus was closed in 1933.

Sport & leisure

The Nazis used sport to promote physical fitness and national prestige. The 1936 Berlin Olympics were used to show the world an impressive Germany, although antisemitic posters were temporarily removed. Sport could also undermine Nazi racial claims when athletes such as Jesse Owens succeeded.

9.4
Opposition

Resistance and opposition to Hitler

Not all Germans supported the Nazis. Opposition ranged from private criticism and passive resistance to organised youth groups and attempts to assassinate Hitler.

A spectrum of resistance

PrivateMoaning, grumbling and anti-Hitler jokes.
PassiveRefusing the Hitler salute or refusing to give to Nazi collections.
OpenSwing Youth, White Rose, Edelweiss Pirates and religious criticism.
Armed / assassinationArmy and elite plots against Hitler.

Why resist?

Motives varied: horror at concentration camps and Gestapo methods, treatment of Jews and disabled people, restrictions on daily life, dislike of Nazi youth control, religious objections, and opposition to the war or Hitler’s leadership.

Why so difficult? There were no free elections, public criticism risked arrest or punishment, and the police state encouraged fear and suspicion.

White Rose

University students including Hans and Sophie Scholl distributed anti-Nazi leaflets, posters and graffiti. They were caught in 1943 and beheaded.

Youth resistance

Swing Youth listened to jazz and rejected Nazi expectations. Edelweiss Pirates resisted Nazi youth control; some helped army deserters and carried out sabotage. In November 1944, a group were publicly hanged.

July Bomb Plot, 1944

Claus von Stauffenberg placed a bomb at a meeting with Hitler. The bomb exploded, but Hitler survived. Stauffenberg and other plotters were executed, and about 5,000 others were executed in revenge.

Judgement: opposition existed in many forms, but Nazi surveillance, fear and severe punishment made coordinated resistance extremely difficult and generally ineffective.

1 · In what ways would the behaviour of German people be affected by the police state?

8 marks

Tip

  • Focus on effects on ordinary people, not just a list of organisations.
  • Use fear of the SS/Gestapo, informers, concentration camps and controlled courts.
  • Explain how these could discourage criticism and opposition.
Revision marking points

People could become cautious about speaking openly because criticism might be reported by informers. Fear of arrest by the Gestapo or SS, imprisonment without trial, concentration camps and Nazi-controlled courts discouraged opposition. The system encouraged self-censorship and suspicion because people could not know who might report them.

2 · In what ways were art and culture used by the Nazis?

8 marks

Tip

  • Use two or three different areas of culture.
  • Explain both what the Nazis promoted and what they rejected.
  • Possible areas: art, cinema, literature, music, theatre, architecture or sport.
Revision marking points

The Chamber of Culture controlled who could work and helped make culture reflect Nazi beliefs. Cinema carried pro-Nazi messages; banned books were removed and burned; Jewish composers and jazz were rejected; modern art was labelled “degenerate” while realistic heroic German images were promoted. Sport, especially the 1936 Olympics, was also used to present Nazi Germany positively.

3 · Describe two examples of opposition to the Nazis.

4 marks

Tip

  • Choose exactly two examples.
  • Name each example and add precise supporting detail.
  • You could use White Rose, Edelweiss Pirates, Swing Youth or an assassination attempt.
Revision marking points

Examples include the White Rose group, which distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and whose members Hans and Sophie Scholl were executed in 1943; the Edelweiss Pirates, who resisted Nazi youth control and sometimes helped deserters; and the July Bomb Plot of 1944, when Stauffenberg attempted to kill Hitler with a bomb.

4 · Which was the more important reason why resistance and opposition to the Nazis was not effective: the strength of the Nazi police state or the weaknesses of the protesters?

12 marks

Tip

  • Explain both reasons.
  • Police state: Gestapo, SS, informers, camps, courts and fear.
  • Protesters: separate groups, different motives, limited numbers and failed assassination attempts.
  • Finish with a supported judgement about which mattered more.
Revision marking points

The police state made opposition exceptionally dangerous through surveillance, denunciations, arrest, concentration camps and harsh punishment. This restricted communication and organisation. Protesters were also divided: youth groups, church critics, students and army officers had different aims and did not form one coordinated movement. Attempts to kill Hitler failed. A judgement should weigh these factors rather than simply listing them.

5 · How successful were propaganda and censorship in controlling German people?

8 marks

Tip

  • Use evidence of wide Nazi control of radio, newspapers, film and culture.
  • Then test success against evidence of continued opposition and resistance.
  • Reach a balanced judgement.
Revision marking points

Goebbels controlled a very wide range of media and culture, while censorship restricted competing ideas, so Nazi messages were difficult to avoid. However, propaganda did not win over everyone: private grumbling, youth opposition, White Rose activity, religious criticism and assassination plots show that control was never complete. A strong judgement distinguishes exposure to propaganda from genuine belief.
