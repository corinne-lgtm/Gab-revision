P6.1 · textbook pages 70–71

Density

Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V. Use kg/m³, or g/cm³, and convert carefully: 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. Find mass using a balance and volume by dimensions for a regular solid, displacement for an irregular solid, or a measuring cylinder for a liquid. An object less dense than water usually floats.

Equation

ρ = m ÷ V; mass = density × volume. For a rectangular solid V = length × width × height.

Irregular object

Measure mass with a balance. Submerge fully in a measuring cylinder: volume = final reading − initial reading.

Liquid

Measure the mass of a known volume, subtracting the mass of the empty container.

P6.2 · textbook pages 72–73

States of matter

In solids, particles vibrate around fixed positions; in liquids particles remain close together but can move past each other; in gases particles move randomly and are far apart. Changes of state are physical changes. Mass is conserved in a closed system.

Solid

Regular, fixed arrangement; particles vibrate. A solid keeps its shape and volume.

Liquid

Particles are close but can change positions; fixed volume, takes the container’s shape.

Gas

Particles are far apart and move rapidly at random; easily compressed, fills its container.

P6.3 · textbook pages 74–75

Changes of state

At a pure substance’s melting point or boiling point the temperature stays constant during the change of state, even though energy continues to be transferred. Melting/freezing and boiling/condensation occur at characteristic temperatures. Evaporation can happen below boiling point at the surface.

Heating curve

Sloping sections: temperature rises. Flat sections: energy changes state without raising temperature.

Boiling vs evaporation

Boiling occurs throughout the liquid at its boiling point; evaporation occurs at the surface at a range of temperatures.

Cooling

During freezing or condensation energy is transferred from the substance; temperature remains constant during the change of state.

P6.4 · textbook pages 76–77

Internal energy

Internal energy is the sum of the kinetic energies and potential energies of particles in a system. Heating can increase the particles’ kinetic energy (raising temperature) or their potential energy (changing state). Gas pressure comes from particle collisions with surfaces.

Kinetic energy

Particles move or vibrate more vigorously at higher temperatures.

Potential energy

Energy associated with attractions and the separation of particles changes during a phase transition.

Exam distinction

Internal energy is not simply the kinetic energy of the object moving as a whole.

P6.5 · textbook pages 78–79

Specific latent heat

Specific latent heat is energy required per kilogram to change state without changing temperature. E = mL; L is in J/kg. Latent heat of fusion applies to melting/freezing and latent heat of vaporisation to boiling/condensation. A practical can use an immersion heater, a joulemeter and measurements of mass melted or evaporated.

Equation

E = m L, so L = E/m. Convert grams to kilograms before dividing.

Fusion

Energy is absorbed on melting and released on freezing, without a temperature change during the transition.

Vaporisation

Energy is absorbed on boiling and released on condensing; use mass boiled away in kg.

P6.6 · textbook pages 80–81

Gas pressure and temperature

Gas particles move randomly and collide with the container’s walls, exerting forces that create pressure. In a sealed, rigid container with the same amount of gas, raising temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the particles and raises pressure. Brownian motion provides evidence for moving molecules.

Collision model

Pressure is caused by many impacts of gas molecules on a surface.

Heating sealed gas

Faster particles collide more frequently and with greater momentum changes, increasing pressure at fixed volume.

Brownian motion

Smoke particles move irregularly because air molecules hit them unevenly from different directions.

Required Practical — Measuring density

Regular solid: measure mass on a balance and dimensions with a ruler or calipers; calculate volume, then ρ = m/V.

Irregular solid: measure mass and find volume displaced in a measuring cylinder (fully submerge and avoid trapped air).

Liquid: subtract the mass of an empty container from the mass of the container with the liquid; measure the liquid's volume.

Exam technique: read the measuring cylinder at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus, convert units consistently and repeat measurements where possible.

Why use a balance? To measure mass. Why use the displacement method? To measure the volume of an irregular object that cannot be calculated from simple dimensions.
