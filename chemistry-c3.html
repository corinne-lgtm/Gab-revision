C3.1

States of matter

Solid

Fixed shape + fixed volume.

Particles are closely packed in fixed positions and vibrate.

Liquid

No fixed shape + fixed volume.

Particles remain close but can slide past one another.

Gas

No fixed shape or volume.

Particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly. Gases can be compressed.

Heating curve

During melting, added energy is used to overcome forces between particles, so the temperature does not rise until melting is complete.

Higher — limitation of the particle model: simple diagrams treat particles as solid spheres and often omit forces. Real atoms, molecules and ions differ in size and shape, interact with neighbours, and atoms are mostly empty space.
GCSE trap: a change of state does not create a new substance. The particles themselves remain the same.

C3.6

Structure of simple molecules

Inside each molecule

Strong covalent bonds hold atoms together.

Between molecules

Weak intermolecular forces. These are overcome when a simple molecular substance melts or boils.

What breaks when chlorine boils?

The intermolecular forces are overcome. The Cl—Cl covalent bonds are not broken.

Low melting/boiling points

Only weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome.

No electrical conduction

Simple molecules have no overall charge and no mobile charged particles.

Larger molecules

Intermolecular forces increase with molecular size, so melting/boiling points generally rise.

C3.7

Giant covalent structures

Huge networks of atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds throughout the structure. This gives very high melting and boiling points.

Diamond

Each carbon forms 4 covalent bonds. Rigid 3D lattice → extremely hard. No delocalised electrons → does not conduct electricity.

Graphite

Each carbon forms 3 covalent bonds in hexagonal layers. One electron per carbon is delocalised → conducts. Weak forces between layers → layers slide.

Graphite: slide the layers

There are no covalent bonds between layers, so the weak forces allow layers to move over each other.

C3.8

Fullerenes and graphene

Fullerenes

Carbon molecules shaped as hollow cages or tubes, based mainly on hexagonal rings but sometimes pentagons/heptagons.

Carbon nanotubes

Very high tensile strength, high electrical and thermal conductivity. Useful for reinforcing composites and electronics.

Graphene

A single layer of graphite — one atom thick. Excellent conductor, low density and extremely strong for its mass.

Recognise the carbon structures
Uses follow properties: fullerene cages can carry molecules; nanoparticles have large surface area; nanotubes are strong; graphene combines strength with excellent conductivity.

C3.9

Bonding in metals

Metals have a giant metallic lattice: positively charged metal ions surrounded by a “sea” of delocalised electrons. Strong electrostatic attraction between them is metallic bonding.

Metallic bonding model
+++++++++++++++++++++
e⁻e⁻e⁻e⁻e⁻

The electrons are not linked to one particular ion: they can move through the giant structure.

Metallic bonding is the strong attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons — not simply “metal atoms stuck together”.

C3.10

Giant metallic structures

Malleable + ductile

Regular layers can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bonding.

Conduct electricity

Delocalised electrons move through the lattice and transfer charge.

Conduct thermal energy

Mobile delocalised electrons transfer energy rapidly through the metal.

High melting points

Strong electrostatic attractions extend throughout the giant structure.
