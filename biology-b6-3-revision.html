Drugs from plants

🌿

Digitalis / Digoxin

Extracted from foxgloves. Used since the 18th century to strengthen the heartbeat. Several drugs including digoxin are still used for older patients with heart problems. Large amounts can act as poisons.

🌳

Aspirin

Originated from a compound in the bark of willow trees. Anti-inflammatory and pain-relieving properties first recorded in 400BC. In 1897 Felix Hoffmann synthesised acetyl salicylic acid (aspirin) — relieves pain and inflammation better than willow bark with fewer side effects. Still widely used today.

🍋

Noni fruit

Used in traditional medicine in Costa Rica and many other countries to treat infections and non-communicable diseases. Recent research shows it has antibiotic properties. May be the source of new antibiotics or other medicines.

How penicillin was discovered

1928
Alexander Fleming was growing bacteria on culture plates. He was careless and often left the lids off. After one holiday, he noticed mould (Penicillium) growing on his plates — and a clear ring where bacteria had been killed around the mould. He realised something in the mould was killing bacteria and called it 'penicillin'.
1928–38
Fleming tried unsuccessfully for several years to extract an active penicillin juice from the mould. Eventually gave up and moved on.
~1938
Ernst Chain and Howard Florey set about extracting penicillin — and succeeded. They gave some to a man dying of a blood infection → he recovered almost miraculously — until the penicillin ran out. He died, but they had proven penicillin could cure bacterial infections in people.
WWII
Working with the company Pfizer in the USA, Florey and Chain produced penicillin on an industrial scale — enough to supply the demands of World War II. It is still used today.

Medicines for the future — synthetic drugs

Most drugs are now synthesised by research chemists using chemical banks and computer models — much faster and cheaper than extracting from organisms. However, the starting point may still be a chemical from a plant or microorganism — compounds can be modified to produce more powerful, easily synthesised molecules.

Scientists are also collecting soil samples globally — searching for new antibiotics from soil microorganisms. Only ~1% of soil microorganisms can be cultured in the lab, but a special unit now grows them in controlled ways. In 2015 a new antibiotic from soil bacteria destroyed all bacteria in tests — including MRSA and other antibiotic-resistant pathogens. It worked in mice — but will it work in humans?

Advantages and disadvantages of looking for new antibiotics in living organisms

✅

Advantages

  • Living organisms have evolved chemicals that kill other organisms — they are natural and proven starting points
  • Traditional medicines have been used safely for centuries — provides a safety record
  • Compounds from organisms can be synthesised and modified to be more effective
  • Soil microorganisms represent an untapped source of novel antibiotics
❌

Disadvantages

  • Difficult to find — only ~1% of soil microorganisms can be cultured; many traditional plants haven't been tested
  • Long, expensive development and clinical trial process before use in humans
  • Extracting directly from organisms is not easily scalable — small amounts only
  • Compounds may work in mice but fail in human trials
