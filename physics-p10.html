P10.1 · textbook pages 122–123

Forces and acceleration

The resultant force acting on an object is equal to mass × acceleration. Newton’s second law states that acceleration is proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to mass.

Learn

What you need to master

  • F = m × a. F in newtons, m in kg, a in m/s². Rearrange a = F/m or m = F/a.
  • If the resultant force doubles with the same mass, acceleration doubles. If the mass doubles with the same force, acceleration halves.
  • Inertia is the tendency to resist a change in velocity. Inertial mass = resultant force ÷ acceleration, and is measured in kg.
Worked example: Example: A 6.0 kg object accelerates at 3.0 m/s². F = 6.0 × 3.0 = 18 N.

Equation & units

F = m × a. F in newtons, m in kg, a in m/s². Rearrange a = F/m or m = F/a.

How it works

If the resultant force doubles with the same mass, acceleration doubles. If the mass doubles with the same force, acceleration halves.

Exam distinction

Inertia is the tendency to resist a change in velocity. Inertial mass = resultant force ÷ acceleration, and is measured in kg.

Investigation and exam technique

Required practical: vary force while keeping total mass constant, then vary mass while keeping force constant; use a trolley, newton-meter, motion sensor and a track. On a velocity–time graph, gradient = acceleration.

See Newton’s second law in action

Question: What changes when the same force pushes a heavier trolley?

Same trolley: mass = 2 kg

Push with 4 N → acceleration = 2 m/s².

Double the push to 8 N → acceleration = 4 m/s².

4 N → 2 kg   a = 2 m/s²
8 N ⟶ 2 kg   a = 4 m/s²
Same push: force = 8 N

2 kg trolley → acceleration = 4 m/s².

4 kg trolley → acceleration = 2 m/s².

8 N ⟶ 2 kg   a = 4 m/s²
8 N ⟶ 4 kg   a = 2 m/s²

Why? F = ma, so a = F ÷ m. More force causes more acceleration; more mass means less acceleration for the same resultant force.

P10.2 · textbook pages 124–125

Weight and terminal velocity

The weight of an object is the force acting on it due to gravity. Mass does not depend on location, but weight depends on gravitational field strength.

Learn

What you need to master

  • W = m × g. Weight W in N, mass m in kg and gravitational field strength g in N/kg. On Earth g ≈ 9.8 N/kg.
  • A falling object released from rest initially has a downward resultant force. Drag increases with speed until it balances weight; then resultant force and acceleration are zero.
  • Terminal velocity is constant velocity in a fluid when the resistive force equals the downward weight (for simple falling-object examples). It does not mean the object stops.
Worked example: Example: A 55 kg person has weight 55 × 9.8 = 539 N ≈ 540 N. On the Moon the weight is much lower for the same mass.

Equation & units

W = m × g. Weight W in N, mass m in kg and gravitational field strength g in N/kg. On Earth g ≈ 9.8 N/kg.

How it works

A falling object released from rest initially has a downward resultant force. Drag increases with speed until it balances weight; then resultant force and acceleration are zero.

Exam distinction

Terminal velocity is constant velocity in a fluid when the resistive force equals the downward weight (for simple falling-object examples). It does not mean the object stops.

Investigation and exam technique

Skydiver sequence: initially accelerate → air resistance increases → acceleration decreases → terminal velocity. A parachute increases drag, causing slowing down until a lower terminal velocity is reached.

Why does a falling object reach terminal velocity?

1. Just released

Weight = 100 N down; drag is small (e.g. 10 N up).

↑ 10 N drag●↓ 100 N weight

Resultant = 90 N down. It accelerates.

2. Moving faster

Drag increases as speed increases. Eventually drag = weight.

↑ 100 N drag●↓ 100 N weight

Resultant = 0 N. Speed becomes constant: terminal velocity.

Important: terminal velocity does NOT mean the object has stopped. It is still falling, but no longer accelerating.

P10.3 · textbook pages 126–127

Forces and braking

When a car moves at constant velocity, driving and resistive forces balance. To slow down the car, braking must create a resultant force opposite the direction of motion.

Learn

What you need to master

  • Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Thinking distance = speed × reaction time.
  • Thinking distance increases with speed and reaction time; tiredness, alcohol, drugs and distractions delay reaction. Braking distance depends on initial speed, road/weather conditions, tyre/brake condition and mass.
  • For constant braking deceleration, use v² − u² = 2as. If v = 0, the magnitude of deceleration is u²/(2s). Force magnitude = ma.
Worked example: Example: A 1500 kg car stops from 30 m/s in 75 m. |a| = 900/150 = 6.0 m/s²; braking resultant ≈ 9000 N.

Equation & units

Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Thinking distance = speed × reaction time.

How it works

Thinking distance increases with speed and reaction time; tiredness, alcohol, drugs and distractions delay reaction. Braking distance depends on initial speed, road/weather conditions, tyre/brake condition and mass.

Exam distinction

For constant braking deceleration, use v² − u² = 2as. If v = 0, the magnitude of deceleration is u²/(2s). Force magnitude = ma.

Investigation and exam technique

At constant deceleration, braking distance is proportional to speed squared. Doubling speed therefore quadruples braking distance; thinking distance doubles if reaction time is unchanged.

Stopping distance: what actually happens?

A driver sees a hazard at 20 m/s (about 45 mph). Reaction time = 0.75 s. Assume braking deceleration = 5 m/s² on a suitable dry road.

1. Thinking distanceThe car keeps moving at 20 m/s before the driver brakes.20 × 0.75 = 15 m
2. Braking distanceThe brakes slow the car from 20 m/s to rest.v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 400 − 10s → 40 m
15 m thinking40 m braking

Total stopping distance = 15 + 40 = 55 m. A tired driver increases thinking distance; a wet road may increase braking distance. The values here are illustrative.

P10.4 · textbook pages 128–129

Momentum

Momentum is a vector: the momentum of a moving object equals its mass multiplied by its velocity. In a closed system without external resultant force, total momentum is conserved.

Higher tier

What you need to master

  • p = m × v. Unit: kg m/s. Set a positive direction, include negative signs for movement the other way.
  • For an inelastic collision where two objects stick together: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v. Total momentum before = total momentum after.
  • For an explosion or skaters pushing apart from rest, total initial momentum is zero; their final momenta must have equal magnitude and opposite direction.
Worked example: Example: Two trolleys, 1.0 kg moving at 4 m/s and 1.0 kg at rest, stick together. Final speed = 4/2 = 2.0 m/s.

Equation & units

p = m × v. Unit: kg m/s. Set a positive direction, include negative signs for movement the other way.

How it works

For an inelastic collision where two objects stick together: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v. Total momentum before = total momentum after.

Exam distinction

For an explosion or skaters pushing apart from rest, total initial momentum is zero; their final momenta must have equal magnitude and opposite direction.

Investigation and exam technique

Momentum conservation applies only if external resultant force is zero (or negligible during the interaction). The lighter skater moves faster if both receive equal and opposite momentum.

Collision walkthrough: where does the momentum go?

Two trolleys collide and stick together. Assume negligible external force during the collision.

Before collision
A: 1 kg4 m/s →B: 1 kg0 m/s

Momentum = (1 × 4) + (1 × 0) = 4 kg m/s to the right.

After collision
A + B: 2 kg2 m/s →

Momentum = 2 × 2 = 4 kg m/s to the right.

Why 2 m/s? Total momentum stays at 4 kg m/s, but the moving mass is now 2 kg. So velocity = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 m/s. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy need not be.

P10.5 · textbook pages 130–131

Forces and elasticity

An elastic object returns to its original shape after the force deforming it is removed. Extension is the increase in length relative to the original length.

Learn

What you need to master

  • F = k × e (Hooke’s law, within the limit of proportionality). F in N, spring constant k in N/m, extension e in metres.
  • Before the limit of proportionality, force is directly proportional to extension; a force–extension graph is a straight line through the origin. Its gradient equals k (if force is on the y-axis).
  • Beyond the limit of proportionality, the force–extension relation is no longer linear. Passing the elastic limit may cause permanent deformation; these are related but not identical concepts.
Worked example: Example: A spring extends 0.080 m with a 4.0 N load. k = 4.0/0.080 = 50 N/m.

Equation & units

F = k × e (Hooke’s law, within the limit of proportionality). F in N, spring constant k in N/m, extension e in metres.

How it works

Before the limit of proportionality, force is directly proportional to extension; a force–extension graph is a straight line through the origin. Its gradient equals k (if force is on the y-axis).

Exam distinction

Beyond the limit of proportionality, the force–extension relation is no longer linear. Passing the elastic limit may cause permanent deformation; these are related but not identical concepts.

Investigation and exam technique

Required practical: clamp a spring, measure its original length, add known weights, measure extension from the same reference, plot force against extension, and identify the straight-line section. Take care with falling masses.

Hooke’s law: connect the graph to a real spring

Start with a spring of original length 10 cm and spring constant 50 N/m.

Apply 1 N

Extension = F/k = 1/50 = 0.02 m = 2 cm.

Fixed support〰〰〰1 N

New length = 12 cm.

Apply 2 N

Extension = 2/50 = 0.04 m = 4 cm.

Fixed support〰〰〰〰〰2 N

New length = 14 cm.

Key difference: extension is the increase in length (not the full length). Doubling force doubles extension only while the straight-line relationship holds. Beyond the limit of proportionality, the graph curves.
