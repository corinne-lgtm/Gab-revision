⚡ The Big Ideas
🔄

Concentric Circles

A current-carrying wire creates a magnetic field in concentric circles centred on the wire, in a plane perpendicular to it.

🔩

Corkscrew Rule

The direction of the field matches the rotation of a corkscrew moving in the direction of the current.

🌀

Solenoid

A long coil of wire. The field inside is strong, uniform, and parallel — just like a bar magnet's field.

🔌

Electromagnet

A solenoid with an iron core. The iron is magnetised by the wire's field and multiplies its strength.

Field Around a Straight Wire

Field Pattern — Cross-section View

Field lines are circles. Circles closest to the wire = strongest field. Reversing current reverses direction.

Direction Use the corkscrew rule: point thumb in direction of current → fingers curl in direction of field.
Reversing the current The plotting compass reverses direction — the magnetic field lines reverse.
Distance from wire The further from the wire, the weaker the field — the circles spread out.
Increasing current The magnetic field becomes stronger everywhere around the wire.
🌀 Solenoids
A solenoid is a long coil of insulated wire. It is used whenever a strong magnetic field needs to be produced. The field is produced in and around the solenoid when a current flows.

Solenoid Field Pattern

🔵 Inside the Solenoid

  • Field lines are parallel to the axis
  • All in the same direction
  • Field is strong and uniform
  • A uniform field = field lines are parallel

🟣 Outside the Solenoid

  • Field lines bend around from end to end
  • Like the field of a bar magnet
  • Each line is a complete loop
  • Passes back through the inside of the solenoid
Increasing current → The solenoid's magnetic field becomes stronger.
Reversing current → The field direction reverses — N and S poles swap.
Finding polarity Look at the end: current flows anticlockwise = N pole; clockwise = S pole.
🔌 Electromagnets
An electromagnet is a solenoid in which the insulated wire is wrapped around an iron bar (the core). When a current passes through the wire, a magnetic field forms — this magnetises the iron bar, making the overall field much stronger. When the current is switched off, the iron bar loses most of its magnetism.
Why iron, not steel? Iron is a soft magnetic material — it gains and loses magnetism easily. Steel retains its magnetism (it becomes a permanent magnet).
How to make it stronger Increase the current, increase the number of coils, or use a soft iron core.
What the iron core does The wire's field magnetises the iron, which then adds its own field — the combined effect is much stronger.
In exam questions about electromagnets, always explain two things: (1) the current in the wire creates a field, and (2) that field magnetises the iron core — this is why the electromagnet is so much stronger than a plain solenoid.
The iron core is NOT a permanent magnet. It only acts as a magnet while the current is on. Steel would stay magnetised — which is why iron is used for electromagnets and steel for permanent magnets.
