2.1
War, hardship and revolution

Germany and the First World War

The war began with enthusiasm in 1914. By 1918, shortages, military defeat and political unrest had pushed Germany towards collapse.

Shortages at home

The British navy restricted supplies reaching Germany. Food, medicine and clothing became scarce. By 1918 many people survived on turnips and bread, and a deadly flu epidemic hit an already weakened population.

War weariness

Long queues, hunger and news of battlefield losses damaged morale. In 1915, 500 women demonstrated outside parliament; a year later around 10,000 workers protested in Berlin.

Defeat by 1918

Germany was close to collapse. General Ludendorff told politicians they could not win and advised the Kaiser to make Germany more democratic in the hope of gaining better peace terms.

Interactive timeline

From war to revolution

Mutiny and revolution

On 28 October 1918, sailors at Kiel refused orders for a final attack on British ships. The mutiny spread. Workers joined sailors and created councils in towns across Germany.

The Kaiser abdicates

Wilhelm II lost control and the army generals would not support him. On 9 November 1918 he abdicated and left Germany. SPD leader Friedrich Ebert took his place temporarily. Germany surrendered on 11 November.

Impact by 1918: Germany was virtually bankrupt, society was more divided, and the country had become politically unstable. Around 600,000 war widows and two million children had lost fathers; factories were exhausted by war production.
2.2A
Revolution and a new republic

What was the Weimar Republic?

The Kaiser had gone, but Germany's new leaders immediately faced revolution, violence and deep political divisions.

Ebert's first steps

Ebert signed the armistice, improved working conditions, helped the unemployed, improved housing and food supplies, guaranteed freedoms, and arranged elections for a new parliament.

A democratic republic

Germany would have no Kaiser. People would elect their leaders. In January 1919, Ebert's SPD won the most votes and he became President.

Why “Weimar”?

Because of recent violence in Berlin, the new politicians met in the town of Weimar on 11 February 1919 to decide how Germany should be governed. This began the Weimar Republic.

Opposing forces

Spartacists vs Free Corps

Spartacus League

A left-wing communist movement. It wanted Germany run by councils of soldiers and workers rather than a large parliament. On 6 January 1919, Spartacists attempted to take over Berlin.

Free Corps

About 2,000 tough ex-soldiers were sent by Ebert against the Spartacists. After brutal fighting they crushed the revolt; leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were arrested and murdered.

Communism: the chapter describes a system aiming at equality, little or no private property, and collective control of farms and businesses. After the Russian Revolution of 1917, many wealthier Germans feared a similar communist takeover.
2.2B
Democracy with weaknesses

The Weimar Constitution

The 1919 constitution created one of the most democratic systems in the world, but some of its features could make stable government difficult.

1919 Constitution

Weimar Republic

A much more democratic system than the Kaiser's Germany.

Proportional representation

Parties received roughly the same percentage of Reichstag seats as their percentage of votes. This gave many parties representation, but no party won more than half the votes between 1919 and 1933, so coalitions were needed and law-making could be slow.

Article 48

In a crisis, the President could rule without Reichstag support by issuing special emergency decrees. This gave the President exceptional powers within an otherwise democratic constitution.

2.3
Punishment and resentment

Why did Germans hate the Treaty of Versailles?

The Allies announced the peace terms in 1919. Germans regarded them as harsh, humiliating and imposed without negotiation.

War guilt & reparations

Germany had to accept responsibility for the war and pay reparations. The chapter gives the later figure as £6.6 billion, with payments intended to last until 1988.

Military restrictions

The army was limited to 100,000 soldiers; Germany could have only six battleships and no submarines, tanks or air force. German soldiers were forbidden from entering the Rhineland.

Territory and empire

Germany lost its overseas colonies and large areas of land. It was also forbidden from uniting with Austria.

Why the anger?

Three reasons Germans hated Versailles

Harsh terms+“Diktat”+“November Criminals”

Harsh terms: land, people, factories, farms and mines were lost, alongside reparations. Diktat: Germany had not been invited to negotiate and was ordered to accept the treaty. November Criminals: some Germans claimed politicians had betrayed an undefeated army by ending the war.

Interpretations matter: the textbook contrasts strongly anti-Treaty views with historian Sally Marks' argument that the Treaty was severe but not exceptionally so. GCSE interpretation questions ask you to compare what interpretations say and use contextual knowledge to evaluate them.
2.4A
1923 crisis

The Weimar Republic in trouble

Failure to meet reparations payments led to the occupation of the Ruhr. The government's response helped turn an economic problem into hyperinflation.

Germany fails to pay

Germany was ordered to pay 132 billion gold marks. In 1922 it said it could not afford the next payment. In January 1923, around 60,000 French and Belgian soldiers entered the Ruhr and took control of factories, mines and railways.

Passive resistance

The government ordered Ruhr workers not to fight, but to strike and refuse to help the occupiers. The government continued paying them even though the Ruhr was no longer producing goods Germany could sell abroad.

Cause → consequence

How the Ruhr crisis fed hyperinflation

Germany misses payment→Ruhr occupied→Passive resistance→Government prints money→Prices rise faster→Hyperinflation
Remember the mechanism: more money was printed to pay striking workers and help people buy goods. Shops responded by raising prices. The government printed still more money, and the cycle accelerated.
2.4B
Winners and losers

The impact of hyperinflation

By 1923 German money had become almost worthless. The effects were dramatic — but they were not identical for everyone.

Big losers: savers

People who had saved money in banks saw its value disappear. The chapter gives the example of someone who had saved 1,000 marks finding that it would not buy even a loaf of bread by 1923.

Fixed incomes & businesses

Elderly people on fixed pensions could no longer afford necessities. Many small businesses collapsed because prices changed so rapidly that normal trade became extremely difficult.

Winners: borrowers

People who owed money could repay old debts with now-worthless currency. Someone who had borrowed 10,000 marks in 1920 could later clear the debt with a single banknote.

The price of bread

Watch the mark collapse

Political impact: many Germans blamed the government for the crisis, especially its decisions over the Ruhr and money printing. The Weimar politicians lost support as people looked for someone to blame.

Revision marking points

Relevant material includes the British blockade and shortages, turnips and bread, poor health and the flu epidemic, battlefield losses, war widows and children without fathers, women's factory work, restrictions on wages, demonstrations and growing war weariness.

2 · Explain two weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution.

8 marks

Writing tip

  • Choose two genuinely different constitutional features.
  • Describe each accurately.
  • Then explain why each could weaken stable democratic government.
Revision marking points — original revision question

Relevant points include proportional representation producing many parties and making majority government difficult, coalition bargaining slowing decisions, Article 48 allowing emergency rule without Reichstag support, and hostility to the new system among influential groups.

3 · How does Interpretation C differ from Interpretation B about the terms of the Treaty of Versailles?

4 marks

Interpretation question tip

  • Identify the overall view of each interpretation.
  • Make a direct comparison: “C suggests…, whereas B suggests…”
  • Focus on the difference in their views of the Treaty, not everything you know about Versailles.
Revision marking points

Interpretation B, from Hitler's Mein Kampf, presents the Treaty as a source of lasting shame and humiliation. Interpretation C, historian Sally Marks, argues that although the Treaty was severe, it was not exceptionally severe and that Germany's actual losses were relatively limited.

4 · Explain how the French and Belgian occupation of the Ruhr helped cause hyperinflation in 1923.

8 marks

Cause-chain tip

  • Begin with the occupation and the government's response.
  • Follow the chain in chronological order.
  • At each step explain how it led to the next one.
  • End by linking money printing to rapidly rising prices.
Revision marking points — original revision question

A strong chain links Germany's failure to pay reparations → French and Belgian occupation → passive resistance → Ruhr production falling while striking workers were still paid → government printing more money → shops raising prices → further money printing → hyperinflation.

5 · Which had the greater impact on the German people: the Treaty of Versailles or the hyperinflation of 1923?

12 marks

12-mark judgement tip

  • You must discuss both factors.
  • Use specific evidence for the impact of each.
  • Compare scale, severity and who was affected.
  • Finish with a supported judgement about which had the greater impact.
Don't reveal your judgement too cheaply: build the evidence first, then decide which impact was greater and why.
Revision marking points

For Versailles, relevant material includes reparations, territorial and military losses, humiliation, the diktat and resentment against the November Criminals. For hyperinflation, consider savers, pensioners, small businesses, borrowers, rapidly changing prices and loss of confidence in Weimar. The strongest response compares their effects and reaches a reasoned judgement.
