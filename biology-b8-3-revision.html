B8.3 How plants use glucose

How plants use glucose, the extra materials needed to produce proteins, and practical tests for starch, sugars, and proteins.

📖 Revision ✏️ Questions
Plants use glucose for respiration, cellulose, starch, amino acids/proteins, and lipids — glucose + nitrate ions = everything a plant needs to grow.
🎯

Learning objectives

  • How plants use glucose
  • Extra materials needed to produce proteins

Six uses of glucose in plants

⚡

1. Respiration

Glucose is broken down by aerobic respiration to release energy for all cell processes. Plants respire 24 hours a day — not just in the dark.

🏗️

2. Cellulose

Glucose is built up into cellulose — a complex carbohydrate used to strengthen cell walls.

🥔

3. Starch (storage)

Glucose → insoluble starch for storage. Starch is insoluble so it doesn't affect osmosis. Found in leaves (short-term) and storage organs: tubers, bulbs (e.g. potatoes, onions).

🧬

4. Amino acids → Proteins

Glucose + nitrate ions (from soil) → amino acids → proteins (including enzymes). Nitrate ions must be absorbed from the soil — they cannot be made from glucose alone.

🛢️

5. Lipids (fats and oils)

Glucose + energy from respiration → fats and oils. Used as energy stores in seeds. Seeds are often very rich in oils — considered as potential biofuels.

🪟

Why store starch not glucose?

Glucose is soluble in water — storing lots of it in cells would affect water movement by osmosis, disrupting the cell's water balance. Starch is insoluble — no osmotic effect.

Nitrates, proteins, and carnivorous plants

Plants need nitrate ions absorbed from the soil (or water for algae) to combine with glucose to make amino acids. These are then built into proteins used as enzymes and for growth.

In nitrate-poor soil (e.g. bogs, peaty soil) plants cannot get enough nitrates. Carnivorous plants (pitcher plants, Venus flytraps, sundews) solve this by catching and digesting insects — using the digested proteins to get the nitrates and other minerals they need. After digesting an insect, the trap reopens, ready to catch another.

Testing for starch in a leaf (practical)

1
Take a leaf from a plant kept in the light and one kept in the dark for 24+ hours
2
Boil the leaves in water (to kill cells and break down membranes)
3
Place leaves in boiling ethanol using a hot water bath — removes chlorophyll (decolourises the leaf) — safety: ethanol is volatile and flammable, no naked flames
4
Rinse in hot water to soften the leaves
5
Add iodine solution to both leaves
✅ Result: Light leaf turns blue-black (starch present — photosynthesis happened). Dark leaf stays orange-brown/yellow (no starch — no photosynthesis in the dark). Why remove chlorophyll? The green colour would mask the colour change.
