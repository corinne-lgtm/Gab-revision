B6.4 Developing drugs

The stages involved in testing and trialling new drugs, and why testing is so important.

📖 Revision ✏️ Questions
Before a drug can be prescribed, it must be proven to be effective, safe, and the right dosage — through a series of stages taking up to 12 years.

What makes a good medicine?

💊

Effective

Must prevent or cure a disease, or at least make you feel better.

⚠️

Safe

Must not be too toxic (poisonous) or have unacceptable side effects.

🏺

Stable

Must be usable under normal conditions and can be stored for some time.

🎯

Absorbable

Must reach its target in the body and be cleared from your system once it has done its work.

Stage details

Preclinical testing (stages 1–2):
  • Tested in the laboratory on cells, tissues, and live animals
  • Finds out if chemicals are toxic and if they have any efficacy
  • Also gives information on possible doses and side effects
  • Animals used as models to predict how the drug may behave in humans
  • Many chemicals fail at this stage
Clinical trials (stages 3–5):
  • Phase 1: Very low doses given to healthy volunteers — checks for safety and side effects
  • Phase 2: Tested on a small number of patients — checks if it treats the disease
  • Phase 3: Larger-scale trials — finds optimum dose; safety and efficacy confirmed on larger population

Double blind trials

🎭

In human trials, a double blind trial is used to measure effectiveness accurately:

  • Patients randomly allocated to receive either the real drug or a placebo
  • Neither the patient nor the doctor knows who has the real drug until the trial is complete
  • The placebo often contains a different drug already used to treat the disease — so patients are not deprived of treatment
  • This removes bias — results are based on the drug's effect, not on psychological expectation
💡 Results are published in peer-reviewed journals — other scientists can check the results to prevent false claims. National bodies like NICE decide if a drug gives good value for money and should be prescribed by the NHS.

Why testing matters — the thalidomide case

Thalidomide was used in the 1950s–60s as a sedative and anti-nausea drug for pregnant women — without adequate testing for safety in pregnancy. It caused limb deformities in developing foetuses. Thousands of children were born with severely shortened or absent limbs.

Florey and Chain had tested penicillin directly on a human volunteer — this would no longer be acceptable. The thalidomide disaster showed the need for full, rigorous pre-clinical and clinical trialling of all drugs before use in patients.

Today, drug development takes up to 12 years and costs ~£1700 million. This long process exists to prevent similar disasters.
