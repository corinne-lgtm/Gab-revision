2.1 Germany and the First World War

How WWI devastated Germany — economically, politically, and socially — and led to revolution by 1918.

📖 Revision ✏️ Questions ✅ Answers ✏️ Quiz rapide
WWI left Germany bankrupt, divided, and politically unstable — setting the stage for revolution.

Key timeline

Aug 1914
War begins
Germany joins WWI — initially popular with young patriotic Germans.
1915–17
Home front
Food shortages, disease, protests. 500 women march in 1915; 10,000 workers in 1916.
Oct 1918
Mutiny
German navy ordered to attack Britain — sailors at Kiel refuse and mutiny spreads.
9 Nov 1918
Abdication
Kaiser Wilhelm abdicates and secretly leaves Germany. Ebert takes temporary power.
11 Nov 1918
War ends
Germany surrenders. The First World War is over.

Germany at war — conditions at home

🍞

Food shortages

British navy blocked supply ships. Germans survived on turnips and bread. A deadly flu epidemic spread.

💸

Economic crisis

Germany borrowed money abroad (e.g. from the USA) and lent money to allies — with no guarantee of getting it back.

🏭

Factory exhaustion

Factories had been making weapons, not goods to sell. Some owners grew rich while workers had wage restrictions.

👨‍👩‍👧

Social damage

600,000 widows and 2 million fatherless children. War pensions would cost the government a fortune.

Impact of the war on Germany by 1918

🏦 Virtually bankrupt

Germany had borrowed and spent enormous sums. Factories were exhausted and had nothing left to sell abroad.

⚡ Politically unstable

Before the war Germany was stable. By 1918 there was mutiny and revolution in the streets.

✂️ Divided society

Some factory owners made fortunes; workers had wage limits. Women worked in factories, which some felt damaged family values.

😡 Betrayal myth

Many ex-soldiers felt Germany could have won. They blamed politicians ('November Criminals') who ended the war and refused to support them.

Key detail: In just six days, workers' and soldiers' councils were governing cities all over Germany. The Kaiser had lost control — his generals refused to support him.

The end of the war & abdication

Why the Kaiser abdicated

  • He could not control the mutinies and revolution.
  • General Ludendorff said Germany could never win the war.
  • He was advised that Britain, France and the USA might treat Germany better if it became more democratic.
  • His army generals refused to support him.

Friedrich Ebert takes over

  • Leader of the SPD (Germany's largest party).
  • Took the Kaiser's place as temporary leader.
  • Promised to hold elections soon.
  • Gave the people the chance to vote for him if they wished.

Interpretation A

Words of a Berlin resident, adapted from Germany by Robert Gibson and Jon Nicol (1985):

"A general war weariness became apparent. The queues at the food shops grew longer every day. In the queues were thin faces, weak bodies and hungry eyes. What had once been a popular movement now seemed to be destroyed. Nobody read the posters and the reports from the front anymore. Grumbling spread. Demonstrations for food turned into ones for peace. Victory or defeat, heroism, battles, the Kaiser's speeches — it suddenly became unimportant. Hunger was a ghost, invisible but to be felt everywhere."

The author's view: By 1918, Germans were exhausted and demoralised — hunger mattered far more than patriotism or victory.
