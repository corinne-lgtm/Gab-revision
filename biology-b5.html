B5.1

Health and disease

Learn

Health and illness

Health is a state of physical and mental well-being, not simply the absence of disease. Communicable diseases are caused by pathogens and can spread between organisms. Non-communicable diseases cannot be transmitted between people. Diet, stress and life circumstances can affect health.

Interactions between diseases

A disease can increase the risk of another. HIV damages the immune system, making other infections more likely. Some viruses can trigger cancers, including HPV and cervical cancer. Immune reactions may trigger allergies. Physical illness and mental health can affect each other.

Interpreting evidence

A correlation is an association between variables; it does not prove that one causes the other. Consider sample size, confounding variables, the reliability of measurements and whether the results have been repeated. Avoid assuming that every person in a group has the same risk.

Physical health

Physical health describes how well the body and its organ systems are functioning.

B5.2

Pathogens and disease

Learn

The four pathogen groups

Bacteria are single-celled organisms; some cause disease by multiplying and producing toxins. Viruses reproduce inside living cells and can damage them. Fungi and protists can also cause disease. Not all microorganisms are harmful.

How infection spreads

Pathogens can spread through air or droplets, direct contact, contaminated water or food, and vectors such as mosquitoes. The route depends on the pathogen. A vector carries a pathogen between organisms.

Bacteria versus viruses

Bacteria are cells and reproduce by binary fission. Viruses are much smaller, are not cells and require host cells to reproduce. Antibiotics target bacteria, not viruses.

Bacteria

Bacteria are single-celled organisms. Pathogenic bacteria may reproduce rapidly and produce toxins.

B5.3

Preventing infections

Learn

Breaking the chain of infection

Good hygiene reduces the transfer of pathogens. Isolation reduces contact with infected individuals. Destroying or controlling vectors reduces transmission. Vaccination prepares the immune system to respond to a particular pathogen.

Hygiene and food safety

Wash hands with soap and water, especially after using the toilet and before preparing food. Keep raw meat separate from ready-to-eat food, cook food thoroughly and clean contaminated surfaces. Follow appropriate infection-control guidance.

Semmelweis and evidence

Ignaz Semmelweis observed that handwashing with chlorinated lime solution reduced deaths from childbed fever in a maternity clinic. His work illustrates how evidence can challenge established medical practice. Pasteur's germ theory and Lister's antiseptic methods helped establish modern infection control.

Hygiene

Handwashing, cleaning surfaces and safe food handling reduce the transfer of pathogens between sources and new hosts.

B5.4

Viral diseases

Learn

Measles

Measles causes fever and a red skin rash and can lead to serious complications. It spreads through the air and is prevented by vaccination. There is no specific cure; treatment is supportive. The claim in older textbooks that measles is no longer a UK concern is outdated.

HIV and AIDS

HIV is transmitted through certain body fluids, including blood and sexual fluids. It attacks immune cells. Early infection may cause a flu-like illness; untreated infection can eventually lead to AIDS, when the immune system is severely damaged. Antiretroviral therapy suppresses viral replication and can prevent progression to AIDS. Effective treatment can allow a near-normal life expectancy. Prevention includes condoms, sterile needles, testing and appropriate preventive medication.

Tobacco mosaic virus

TMV infects plants and causes a mosaic pattern of discolouration on leaves. It reduces photosynthesis and can stunt growth. It spreads through contact with infected plant material and contaminated tools. Hygiene and resistant varieties help control it.

Measles

Measles spreads through the air. It causes fever and a red rash and can cause serious complications. Vaccination greatly reduces risk.

B5.5

Bacterial diseases

Learn

Salmonella food poisoning

Salmonella bacteria can be acquired from contaminated food, particularly undercooked poultry and eggs. Symptoms include fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea. Prevention includes thorough cooking, handwashing, avoiding cross-contamination and vaccination of poultry in the UK.

Gonorrhoea

Gonorrhoea is a sexually transmitted bacterial infection. Symptoms may include pain when urinating and a thick yellow or green discharge, but infection can be asymptomatic. It is treated with appropriate antibiotics, although resistance is a growing problem. Condoms, testing and treatment of sexual partners help prevent spread.

Antibiotic resistance

Random mutations can produce resistant bacteria. Antibiotic use selects for resistant strains, which survive and reproduce. Resistance can spread between bacteria. Use antibiotics only when clinically appropriate and follow the prescribed course; antibiotics do not treat viral infections.

1 · Variation

A bacterial population contains genetic variation. A random mutation may make one bacterium resistant to an antibiotic.

B5.6

Diseases caused by fungi and protists

Learn

Rose black spot

Rose black spot is a fungal disease that causes purple or black spots on leaves. Leaves may turn yellow and drop early, reducing photosynthesis and growth. Spores spread through wind and water. Remove affected leaves, use suitable fungicides where appropriate and grow resistant varieties.

Malaria

Malaria is caused by Plasmodium protists and is transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes. The parasites infect liver cells and red blood cells, causing recurrent fever and other symptoms. Prevention includes insecticide-treated nets, vector control and appropriate preventive medicines. Diagnosis and antimalarial treatment are important; resistance can complicate treatment.

Vectors and control

A mosquito is a vector, not the pathogen itself. Reducing mosquito breeding sites, using nets and controlling adult mosquitoes can interrupt transmission. Evaluate effectiveness, cost, environmental effects and the possibility of resistance.

1 · Mosquito bite

An infected female Anopheles mosquito can transmit Plasmodium parasites when it feeds on human blood.

B5.7

Human defence responses

Learn

Physical and chemical barriers

Intact skin forms a barrier and produces antimicrobial secretions. Nose hairs and mucus trap particles and pathogens. Cilia move mucus away from the lungs towards the throat. Stomach hydrochloric acid kills many swallowed pathogens.

White blood cells

Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens. Lymphocytes produce specific antibodies that bind to antigens. Antitoxins neutralise toxins produced by bacteria. These are different mechanisms and should not be confused.

Immunity and vaccination

A vaccine exposes the immune system to harmless pathogen antigens or instructions to produce them. This stimulates an immune response and memory cells. On later exposure, a faster response can prevent illness or reduce its severity. Vaccines are specific and their effectiveness varies.

1 · Barriers

Skin, mucus, cilia and stomach acid act before pathogens establish an infection.
