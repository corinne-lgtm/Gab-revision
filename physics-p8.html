P8.1 · textbook pages 98–99

Vectors and scalars

A scalar has magnitude only. A vector has magnitude and direction. Displacement is distance in a given direction. Vectors can be shown as arrows: arrow length = magnitude, arrow direction = direction.

Common scalars

Distance, speed, time, mass, energy, temperature, power.

Common vectors

Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum.

Scale diagrams

Choose a scale first, then draw arrows in the correct directions and with lengths in proportion to the sizes.

P8.2 · textbook pages 100–101

Forces between objects

A force is a push or a pull caused by an interaction. Contact forces need touching: friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force. Non-contact forces act at a distance: gravity, electrostatic force, magnetic force. When two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other.

Action–reaction pair

If skater A pulls skater B, skater B pulls skater A with an equal and opposite force.

Driving force

The tyre pushes the road backwards, and the road pushes the tyre forwards.

Braking

When brakes act, the resultant force is opposite the motion, so the vehicle slows down.

GCSE diagram · Forces between objects

Exam technique

Do not say: “the bigger object exerts the bigger force”. The pair of forces are equal in size.
P8.3 · textbook pages 102–103

Resultant forces

Same direction → add the forces. Opposite directions → subtract the smaller from the larger. The resultant force acts in the direction of the larger force. If the resultant force is zero, the object is at rest or continues at constant speed in a straight line.

Balanced forces

Resultant force = 0. No change in velocity.

Unbalanced forces

Resultant force ≠ 0. The object accelerates.

Free-body diagram

Show only the forces acting on one object, usually as arrows from the object.

GCSE diagram · Resultant forces

Exam technique

Common mistake: zero resultant does not always mean stationary.
P8.4 · textbook pages 104–105

Centre of mass

The centre of mass is the point where the mass of an object can be thought of as being concentrated. For a uniform ruler, it is at the midpoint. For a freely suspended object, the centre of mass comes to rest directly below the point of suspension. For a symmetrical object, the centre of mass lies on the axis of symmetry.

Stable or unstable?

An object is more stable when its centre of mass is low and remains above its base.

Finding centre of mass

Suspend the shape from two points and draw vertical lines with a plumb line. Where the lines cross is the centre of mass.

Why it matters

Designers want racing cars, chairs, and cranes to remain stable and not topple easily.

The parallelogram of forces

Use it when two forces act on an object at an angle . Draw the two force vectors to scale from a common point. Complete the parallelogram. The diagonal from the common point is the resultant force .

P8.6 · textbook pages 108–109

Resolution of forces

Resolving a force means splitting it into two perpendicular components. On a slope, one component acts parallel to the slope and one acts perpendicular (normal) to the slope. For weight W on a slope angle θ to the horizontal:

Parallel component

This tries to pull the object down the slope.

Perpendicular component

This presses the object into the slope.

Equilibrium on a slope

Friction or another force must balance the component parallel to the slope.
