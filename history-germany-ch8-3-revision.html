8.3 The Nazis and religion

Revision notes designed to make sense without needing the textbook.

The Nazis wanted to control religion because Hitler wanted Nazi loyalty to be stronger than loyalty to churches or religious beliefs.
🎯

Objectives

  • Describe how the Nazis controlled churches and religion.
  • Explain why some Christians supported or opposed the Nazis.
  • Assess the impact of Nazi policies on Christians and Jews.

1. Germany’s main religion

Christian country

Christianity was the biggest religion in Germany. Around one third of Germans were Catholic and around two thirds were Protestant.

Why Hitler had to be careful

Religion was important to many people, so Hitler could not simply ban Christianity immediately. He first tried to control it.

Nazism vs Christianity

Nazi ideas praised strength, violence, racial superiority and Hitler as a near god-like leader. Christianity taught love, forgiveness, equality before God and loyalty to Jesus Christ.

2. Why did some Christians support the Nazis?

Family values

The Nazis spoke about marriage, family and moral values, which appealed to some Christians.

Fear of communism

Many Christians feared communism because it was anti-religious. Hitler promised to destroy communism.

Promise to respect churches

Hitler promised to respect the Catholic Church and signed an agreement with the Pope in 1933.

3. The Nazis and the Catholic Church

Concordat, 1933

Hitler signed a Concordat with the Pope, promising not to interfere with the Catholic Church if the Church stayed out of politics.

Broken promises

Hitler broke the agreement. Nazis harassed Catholics, arrested priests, and closed Catholic youth clubs and schools.

Protest from Galen

In 1941, Catholic Archbishop Galen criticised Nazi terror tactics, euthanasia and concentration camps. He was put under house arrest until the end of the war.

4. The Nazis and the Protestant Church

German Christians

Some Protestants supported Hitler. They called themselves German Christians, wore Nazi uniforms and used slogans such as “the swastika on our chests and the Cross in our hearts”.

Confessional Church

Other Protestants opposed Nazi control of religion. Pastor Martin Niemöller helped form the Confessional Church. The Nazis struck back: around 800 pastors were arrested and the Church was banned.

5. Other Christian groups and opposition

Persecution

About one third of Jehovah’s Witnesses were killed in concentration camps because they were pacifists and refused to serve in the army.

Groups that disappeared

The Salvation Army, Christian Scientists and the Seventh Day Adventist Church all disappeared from Germany under Nazi rule.

Niemöller’s warning

Martin Niemöller’s famous poem warned that if people do not speak out when others are persecuted, eventually there may be nobody left to defend them.

6. The Nazis and Judaism

Small but targeted community

Jewish people made up less than one per cent of Germany’s population, but the Nazis made them a main target of persecution.

Long history in Germany

Jewish people had lived in German lands for around 1600 years and many were fully integrated into German society.

Persecution

Jewish people faced relentless persecution under the Nazis. By 1941, nearly 80 per cent had fled to other countries as refugees.
