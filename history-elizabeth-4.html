Poverty

Why did poverty become a serious problem, why did attitudes begin to change, and how did Elizabeth's government respond?

4.1
A growing problem

Why was there so much poverty?

Poverty was not caused by one thing. Population growth, inflation, bad harvests, unemployment and changes in farming combined to make life less secure.

Big picture: England's population rose from roughly 2.8 million in the mid-sixteenth century to about 4 million by 1603. More people meant greater demand for food, housing and work.

How causes worked together

Population rises→More demand for food→Prices rise→Bad harvest makes it worse→Wages buy less→More poverty

GCSE skill: explaining links between causes is stronger than listing them.

Failures in agriculture

Bad harvests in the 1590s caused food shortages. Some landowners also changed how land was used, including enclosure, which could reduce work or access to land for poorer families.

Inflation

Prices rose faster than many wages. Debasement of coinage before Elizabeth's reign contributed to inflation, and rapid population growth increased demand. Even people in work could struggle.

4.2
Fear, sympathy and responsibility

How did Elizabethans respond to poverty?

Elizabethans did not see all poor people in the same way. Fear of disorder existed alongside a growing recognition that some people were poor through no fault of their own.

Fear and suspicion

Vagrants and travelling beggars could be portrayed as lazy, criminal or dangerous. Writers such as Thomas Harman helped spread the idea that some beggars were tricksters.

Sympathy and charity

Others recognised that sickness, age, disability, unemployment and high prices could cause genuine hardship. Charity and organised poor relief therefore became increasingly important.

4.3
From punishment to organised relief

Government treatment of the poor

Government policy gradually became more systematic. It still punished people considered idle, but increasingly accepted responsibility for helping those thought unable to support themselves.

Build the timeline

Select a date to see what changed.

Local experiments

Towns including York, Norwich and Ipswich tried organised schemes: identifying poor people, collecting money and arranging work or relief. Government could learn from local practice.

Houses of Correction

From 1576, counties were expected to provide places where able-bodied people who refused work could be disciplined and made to work.

1601 Poor Law

Each parish was expected to organise relief. Overseers collected a compulsory poor rate, supported the helpless poor and arranged work for those able to work.

Why did government act?

Scale
The number of poor people was visibly increasing.
Order
Officials feared vagrancy, crime and unrest.
Economy
Inflation, harvest failure and unemployment made private charity insufficient.
Changing attitudes
There was greater recognition that not all poverty was self-inflicted.

1 · Write an account of the ways in which the lives of poor people changed in Elizabethan England.

8 marks

How to write the account

  • Organise the answer as change over time, not a list of laws.
  • Start with harsher attitudes and punishment, then show the move towards organised relief.
  • Use precise turning points such as 1576 and 1601.
  • Explain what each change actually meant for poor people.
Useful move: “Earlier in the period… However, by… This changed life for the poor because…”
Revision marking points

Useful knowledge includes punishment of vagrancy, changing distinctions between categories of poor, local schemes, the 1576 Act and Houses of Correction, organised work and relief, compulsory poor rates and parish responsibility under the 1601 Poor Law.

2 · Explain why poverty increased during Elizabeth's reign.

8 marks

Explain causes — don't just name them

  • Choose two or three connected causes.
  • For each one, explain the mechanism: how did it make a family poorer?
  • Link population, food prices, wages and harvests where possible.
Strong chain: population growth → demand → higher prices → wages buy less → poverty.
Revision marking points — original revision question

Relevant causes include rapid population growth, inflation, bad harvests and food shortages, unemployment, changes in agriculture/enclosure and the reduced ability of older charitable systems to meet growing need.

3 · How convincing is the view that fear of disorder was the main reason the government acted on poverty?

8 marks

Test the claim

  • Give evidence that supports fear of vagrancy, crime and unrest.
  • Then test other reasons: scale of poverty, economic hardship and changing attitudes.
  • Finish by judging whether fear was the main reason, not merely one reason.
Revision marking points — original revision question

Fear of disorder mattered, but government also faced rising numbers in poverty, high food prices and unemployment, while policy increasingly recognised that some people needed support through no fault of their own.

4 · Explain the importance of the 1601 Poor Law.

8 marks

Importance = consequence and significance

  • State what the law did precisely.
  • Explain why parish organisation and a compulsory poor rate mattered.
  • Compare it with earlier, less systematic responses.
  • Remember that punishment did not disappear.
Revision marking points — original revision question

The law made poor relief a local legal responsibility, funded through a compulsory poor rate and administered by parish overseers. It distinguished between help for those unable to work and work or punishment for others, making provision more systematic.
