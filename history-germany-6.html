Chapter 6 · The establishment of Hitler’s dictatorship

How Hitler moved from Chancellor in January 1933 to dictator: the Reichstag Fire, emergency powers, the Enabling Act, destruction of political opposition and the Night of the Long Knives.

6.1
January 1933–August 1934

How did Hitler begin to eliminate his opposition?

When Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933, he was not yet in complete control. Within 18 months he had transformed Germany from a democracy into a Nazi dictatorship.

The road to dictatorship

Key dates

Feb 1933 · New election→27 Feb · Reichstag Fire→28 Feb · Protection Law→5 Mar · Election→23 Mar · Enabling Act→7 Apr · Nazi control
2 May · Trade unions banned→14 July · One-party state→2 Aug 1934 · Hitler becomes Führer

1 · February 1933: a new election

Hitler arranged an election for March 1933, hoping for the Reichstag majority he wanted. As Chancellor he had greater influence over newspapers and radio stations and controlled the police, who could intimidate voters and attack opponents.

2 · 27 February: Reichstag Fire

A week before voting, the Reichstag burned down. The evidence was unclear, but a young communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested and blamed. Hitler presented the fire as part of a communist plot to take over Germany.

3 · 28 February: emergency decree

Hitler persuaded Hindenburg to agree to the Protection Law, giving Hitler powers to deal with Germany’s problems. Leading Communists were banned from campaigning, thousands were imprisoned and Communist newspapers were shut down.

4 · 5 March: election

The Nazis gained more votes than ever before, but Hitler still did not obtain the majority he wanted.

5 · 23 March: Enabling Act

Hitler used fear of communism to persuade politicians in other parties to support the Nazis. He forced the Reichstag to pass the Enabling Act, allowing him to make laws without asking the Reichstag. He no longer had to worry about what Hindenburg or the Reichstag thought of him.

6 · 7 April: Nazi control expands

Nazis took control of local government, councils and the police. The Gestapo (secret police) was formed. The first concentration camp for political prisoners opened at Dachau in southern Germany.

7 · 2 May: trade unions banned

Hitler banned all trade unions, seized their money and imprisoned their leaders. Workers had lost an organised way of complaining about pay and conditions.

8 · 14 July: one-party state

Hitler banned every political party except the Nazis. The Law Against the Formation of New Parties made Germany a one-party state.

9 · 2 August 1934: Der Führer

After President Hindenburg died, Hitler took over the President’s job while remaining Chancellor. He made the army swear an oath of loyalty to him personally, not to Germany, and gave himself the title Der Führer — “the leader”.

Key idea: Hitler’s dictatorship was built step by step. Emergency powers weakened civil liberties, the Enabling Act weakened parliament, opponents and trade unions were removed, other parties were banned, and Hitler finally combined the offices of President and Chancellor.
6.2
30 June 1934

The Night of the Long Knives

Hitler still felt threatened by the power of both the SA and the army. On 30 June 1934 he used the SS to destroy potential rivals and strengthen his dictatorship.

Why was the SA a problem?

The SA had been useful to Hitler, but its leader Ernst Röhm wanted to merge his roughly 400,000 Stormtroopers with the much smaller German army of about 100,000 men and take command. Army leaders were alarmed. Hitler needed their military expertise and feared rumours that Röhm might seize power.

What happened?

Shortly before dawn on 30 June 1934, Hitler and his SS bodyguards stormed a hotel where SA leaders were staying. Röhm and other SA leaders were arrested and later killed. Over the following days around 400 people were executed, including former Chancellor Kurt von Schleicher.

Impact

Why did it matter?

Potential rivals killed→SS replaces SA as key security force→Police state strengthened→Army reassured→Hitler’s power increases

The SS and the police state

The SS, led by Heinrich Himmler, became responsible for Hitler’s security rather than the SA. Together with the Gestapo, the SS formed the basis of the Nazi police state. The SA continued to exist but was never again a major force.

The army and Hitler

Hitler had shown he could kill opponents and escape punishment. When Hindenburg died soon afterwards, Hitler became Führer. The army swore a personal oath of loyalty to him and agreed to stay out of politics; in return Hitler promised major spending to rebuild German military power.

2 · Explain how Hitler was able to eliminate political opposition between February and July 1933.

8 marks

Tip

  • Use a clear sequence: Reichstag Fire → emergency decree → Enabling Act → trade unions → political parties.
  • Explain how each step increased Hitler’s power.
  • Use precise dates where you can.
Revision marking points

After the Reichstag Fire, Hitler blamed a communist plot and gained emergency powers from Hindenburg. Communists were restricted, imprisoned and prevented from campaigning. On 23 March the Enabling Act allowed Hitler to make laws without the Reichstag. Nazis then took control of local government and police, the Gestapo was formed and Dachau opened. Trade unions were banned on 2 May and all other political parties were banned on 14 July, creating a one-party state.

3 · Which was more important in establishing Hitler’s dictatorship: the Enabling Act or the Night of the Long Knives?

12 marks

Tip

  • Explain the importance of both.
  • Compare political/legal power with the removal of threats and support of the army.
  • Finish with a judgement about which did more to secure dictatorship.
Revision marking points

The Enabling Act was crucial because it allowed Hitler to make laws without Reichstag approval and provided the legal basis for dismantling democracy. The Night of the Long Knives removed rivals, weakened the SA, strengthened the SS and reassured the army. A strong judgement should decide whether legal control or removal of physical/political threats was more decisive and explain why.
