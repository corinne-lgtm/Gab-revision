Question 1 — Forces and Free-Body Diagrams
4 marks

A skydiver jumps from a plane. Initially they accelerate downwards. After some time, they reach terminal velocity.

(a) Describe two features of the forces acting on the skydiver when they are accelerating downwards before reaching terminal velocity. [2 marks]

💡 How to Answer — "Describe two features" State each feature clearly as a separate point. For forces questions, think: direction of resultant force, which force is larger, and what this means for motion. Use words like "greater than", "resultant", "downward direction".

(b) Describe two features of the forces acting on the skydiver at terminal velocity. [2 marks]

(a) Any two from:

  • Weight / gravitational force acts downwards ✓
  • Air resistance acts upwards ✓
  • Weight is greater than air resistance ✓
  • There is a resultant (net) force acting downwards ✓

(b) Any two from:

  • Weight equals air resistance ✓
  • Resultant force is zero ✓
  • Forces are balanced / in equilibrium ✓
  • Constant velocity / no acceleration ✓

Question 3 — Velocity–Time Graph Analysis
6 marks

A car travels along a straight road. The velocity–time graph shows its motion.

The car accelerates from rest to 24 m/s in 8 seconds, travels at constant 24 m/s for 12 seconds, then decelerates to rest in 4 seconds.

(a) Calculate the acceleration during the first 8 seconds. [2 marks]

a = (v − u) ÷ t
💡 How to Answer — Graph Calculations For acceleration: gradient = (change in velocity) ÷ (change in time). For distance: area under the graph (use triangles and rectangles). Always show your working with units. A horizontal line = zero acceleration.

(b) Calculate the total distance travelled. [3 marks]

Distance = area under v–t graph

(c) State the deceleration during the final 4 seconds. [1 mark]

(a)

  • a = (24 − 0) ÷ 8 ✓
  • = 3 m/s² ✓

(b) Area = three sections:

  • Triangle (0–8 s): ½ × 8 × 24 = 96 m ✓
  • Rectangle (8–20 s): 12 × 24 = 288 m ✓
  • Triangle (20–24 s): ½ × 4 × 24 = 48 m
  • Total = 96 + 288 + 48 = 432 m ✓

(c) deceleration = (24 − 0) ÷ 4 = 6 m/s² ✓ (accept −6 m/s²)

Question 4 — Newton's Second Law
6 marks

A student investigates how the acceleration of a trolley depends on the applied force (Required Practical).

(a) Explain how the student should set up the experiment to ensure a fair test when investigating the effect of force on acceleration. [3 marks]

💡 How to Answer — Required Practical "Explain" Questions Mention: (1) what is the independent variable (what you change), (2) what is the dependent variable (what you measure), (3) what variables must be controlled (kept the same). For force/acceleration practicals: control mass, compensate for friction.

(b) The trolley has a mass of 0.8 kg. The student applies a force of 2.4 N. Calculate the expected acceleration. [2 marks]

F = ma  →  a = F ÷ m

(c) The student finds the actual acceleration is lower than calculated. Suggest one reason for this. [1 mark]

(a) Any three from:

  • Keep the total mass of trolley constant when changing the force ✓
  • Move masses from the trolley to the hanging weights to keep total mass constant ✓
  • Compensate for friction by raising one end of the runway ✓
  • Use light gates / ticker tape to measure acceleration accurately ✓
  • Repeat and average to reduce random error ✓

(b)

  • a = F ÷ m = 2.4 ÷ 0.8 ✓
  • = 3 m/s² ✓

(c) Any one from:

  • Friction / air resistance not fully compensated for ✓
  • Mass of string/pulley not accounted for in total mass ✓
Question 5 — Stopping Distance
6 marks

A car travels at 20 m/s. The driver has a reaction time of 0.6 s. The braking deceleration is 8 m/s².

(a) Calculate the thinking distance. [2 marks]

distance = speed × time
💡 How to Answer — Stopping Distance Questions Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Thinking distance = speed × reaction time. For braking distance, use v² = u² + 2as (with v = 0). Common mistake: confusing thinking distance with braking distance. List factors affecting each separately.

(b) Calculate the braking distance. [3 marks]

v² = u² + 2as  (v = 0 when stopped)

(c) State one factor that would increase the braking distance (not initial speed). [1 mark]

(a)

  • Thinking distance = 20 × 0.6 ✓
  • = 12 m ✓

(b)

  • 0 = 20² + 2 × (−8) × s ✓
  • 0 = 400 − 16s → s = 400 ÷ 16 ✓
  • s = 25 m ✓

(c) Any one from: wet/icy road ✓, worn tyres ✓, poor brakes ✓, heavier vehicle ✓

Question 6 — Conservation of Momentum
6 marks

Explain, using the law of conservation of momentum, what happens when a moving trolley of mass 2 kg travelling at 4 m/s collides with and sticks to a stationary trolley of mass 2 kg.

💡 How to Answer — 6-Mark Explain Questions Structure: (1) State the law/principle. (2) Apply it to the specific situation with numbers. (3) Calculate the answer. (4) Interpret the result. Use: "Before the collision...", "The total momentum is conserved, therefore...", "After the collision...". Show all working for calculation marks.
  • State law: total momentum before = total momentum after (in a closed system) ✓
  • Before: p = mv = 2 × 4 = 8 kg m/s; stationary trolley has p = 0 ✓
  • Total momentum before = 8 kg m/s ✓
  • After: combined mass = 2 + 2 = 4 kg ✓
  • 8 = 4 × v → v = 2 m/s ✓
  • The joined trolleys move in the same direction as the original trolley at 2 m/s ✓
Question 7 — Work Done and Energy Transfer
6 marks

A person pushes a 60 kg box along a rough floor with a horizontal force of 150 N for a distance of 8 m. The force of friction is 90 N throughout.

Explain how energy is transferred in this situation and calculate the net work done on the box.

💡 How to Answer — Energy Transfer Questions Identify: (1) energy input (work done by pushing force), (2) energy lost to friction (thermal/heat), (3) net work done = resultant force × distance. Common mistake: using pushing force alone without subtracting friction. Net work done = increase in kinetic energy.
W = Fd  |  Net force = applied force − friction
  • Work done by pushing force = 150 × 8 = 1200 J ✓
  • Work done against friction = 90 × 8 = 720 J ✓
  • This energy is transferred to the thermal store of the floor/box ✓
  • Net (resultant) force = 150 − 90 = 60 N ✓
  • Net work done = 60 × 8 = 480 J ✓
  • This equals the increase in the kinetic energy of the box ✓
Question 8 — Safety Features and Force
6 marks

Explain how crumple zones and seat belts reduce injury in a car crash. Refer to momentum, force and time in your answer.

💡 How to Answer — Explain Safety Features Link: change in momentum (impulse) = force × time. Both devices INCREASE the time of impact → REDUCE force. Use equation F = Δp/Δt. Address each safety feature separately. Examiners like to see cause → mechanism → effect.
F = Δp ÷ Δt  (Force = change in momentum ÷ time)

Award marks for any 6 of:

  • In a crash, the car (and occupants) undergo a change in momentum from their initial momentum to zero ✓
  • Force = change in momentum ÷ time (F = Δp/Δt) ✓
  • Crumple zones collapse and deform in a crash ✓
  • This increases the time over which the car decelerates ✓
  • For the same change in momentum, a longer time → smaller force on occupants ✓
  • Seat belts stretch slightly, increasing the time over which the person decelerates ✓
  • This reduces the force on the person's body ✓
  • Without a seat belt, person continues at original speed, hitting windscreen — very short time, huge force ✓
Question 9 — Newton's Laws and Equilibrium
6 marks

A 70 kg person stands in a lift. The lift accelerates upwards at 2 m/s². (g = 10 N/kg)

Explain, using Newton's laws, why the person feels heavier than normal. Calculate the normal reaction force from the floor on the person.

💡 How to Answer — Lift/Acceleration Questions Draw a free-body diagram mentally. Weight acts down (W = mg). Normal reaction (N) acts up. Resultant force = N − W (upward positive). Use F = ma for resultant. Common error: forgetting to add weight and resultant; remember N = W + ma for upward acceleration.
W = mg  |  F = ma  |  N − W = ma
  • Weight of person = mg = 70 × 10 = 700 N (downward) ✓
  • The lift accelerates upward, so resultant force must be upward (Newton's 2nd Law) ✓
  • Resultant force = ma = 70 × 2 = 140 N (upward) ✓
  • N − W = ma → N = W + ma ✓
  • N = 700 + 140 = 840 N ✓
  • The floor pushes upward with 840 N, greater than the person's weight of 700 N → person feels heavier (perceives greater normal force) ✓

Question 10 — Forces in Balance: Moments
6 marks

A see-saw is 4 m long with a pivot at its midpoint. A child of weight 300 N sits 1.5 m from the pivot on one side. An adult wants to balance the see-saw.

Calculate where the adult (weight 600 N) must sit to balance the see-saw. Explain your method using the principle of moments.

💡 How to Answer — Moments Calculations Principle of moments: sum of clockwise moments = sum of anticlockwise moments (for equilibrium). Moment = F × d (perpendicular distance from pivot). Always state the principle first, then substitute. Units: N m (newton-metres).
Moment = F × d  |  Clockwise moments = Anticlockwise moments
  • State principle of moments: for equilibrium, clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments ✓
  • Child's moment = 300 × 1.5 = 450 N m ✓
  • Adult's moment must also = 450 N m ✓
  • d = moment ÷ force = 450 ÷ 600 ✓
  • d = 0.75 m from the pivot ✓
  • The adult must sit on the opposite side to the child, 0.75 m from the pivot ✓
Question 11 — Vector Resolution
6 marks

A ball is kicked at 20 m/s at an angle of 30° to the horizontal.

Explain the concept of resolving vectors. Calculate the horizontal and vertical components of the ball's initial velocity. (sin 30° = 0.5, cos 30° = 0.87)

💡 How to Answer — Vector Resolution Explain what resolving means (splitting into perpendicular components). Horizontal = v × cos θ, Vertical = v × sin θ. Draw a right-angled triangle. Common mistake: swapping sin and cos — remember horizontal uses cos (adjacent side).
Horizontal: vₓ = v cos θ  |  Vertical: vᵧ = v sin θ
  • Resolving a vector means splitting it into two perpendicular components (horizontal and vertical) ✓
  • This allows each component to be analysed independently ✓
  • Horizontal component = v cos θ = 20 × cos 30° ✓
  • = 20 × 0.87 = 17.4 m/s ✓
  • Vertical component = v sin θ = 20 × sin 30° ✓
  • = 20 × 0.5 = 10 m/s ✓
Question 12 — SUVAT and Projectile Motion
6 marks

A ball is dropped from rest from a height of 20 m. (g = 10 m/s², air resistance negligible)

Calculate the time taken to reach the ground and the velocity on impact. Show all working.

💡 How to Answer — SUVAT Questions List what you know: u (initial velocity), v (final velocity), a (acceleration), s (displacement), t (time). Choose the equation that uses the three known quantities. For falling objects: u = 0, a = g = 10 m/s². Always show: identify, formula, substitute, calculate, units.
s = ut + ½at²  |  v² = u² + 2as  |  v = u + at

Time:

  • Known: s = 20 m, u = 0, a = 10 m/s² ✓
  • s = ut + ½at² → 20 = 0 + ½ × 10 × t² ✓
  • 20 = 5t² → t² = 4 → t = 2 s ✓

Velocity:

  • v² = u² + 2as = 0 + 2 × 10 × 20 = 400 ✓
  • v = √400 = 20 m/s ✓
  • OR: v = u + at = 0 + 10 × 2 = 20 m/s ✓
Question 13 — Terminal Velocity and Drag
6 marks

Describe and explain the motion of a skydiver from the moment of jumping until they open their parachute and reach a new, lower terminal velocity. In your answer, refer to forces, acceleration and velocity at each stage.

💡 How to Answer — Extended Narrative Questions Structure your answer in clear stages: (1) Initial fall — weight > drag, accelerating. (2) Increasing drag — as speed increases. (3) First terminal velocity — weight = drag. (4) Parachute opens — drag > weight, decelerating. (5) New lower terminal velocity. Use correct scientific language at each stage.

Award any 6 from:

  • Initially: weight acts downward, air resistance is small (low speed), resultant force downward → accelerates ✓
  • As speed increases, air resistance increases ✓
  • Resultant force decreases, so acceleration decreases (still speeds up, but more slowly) ✓
  • First terminal velocity reached: weight = air resistance, resultant force = 0, constant velocity ✓
  • Parachute opens: much larger surface area → large sudden increase in air resistance ✓
  • Air resistance now greater than weight → resultant force upward → decelerates (slows down) ✓
  • As speed decreases, air resistance decreases until it again equals weight ✓
  • New (lower) terminal velocity reached — skydiver descends at safe landing speed ✓
Question 14 — Gravitational Potential Energy and Kinetic Energy
6 marks

A rollercoaster car of mass 500 kg starts from rest at the top of a hill 30 m high and reaches the bottom. (g = 10 N/kg, ignore friction)

Calculate the loss in gravitational PE, the gain in KE, and the speed at the bottom of the hill. Explain the energy transfers involved.

💡 How to Answer — Energy Transfer Calculations GPE = mgh (joules). KE = ½mv². If no energy lost to friction: GPE lost = KE gained. Then rearrange KE formula to find v. Always state what transfers to what. Units: joules (J) for energy, m/s for velocity.
GPE = mgh  |  KE = ½mv²  |  GPE lost = KE gained
  • GPE = mgh = 500 × 10 × 30 = 150,000 J ✓
  • Energy transfers from gravitational potential store to kinetic store ✓
  • KE gained = GPE lost = 150,000 J ✓
  • KE = ½mv² → 150,000 = ½ × 500 × v² ✓
  • v² = 150,000 × 2 ÷ 500 = 600 ✓
  • v = √600 ≈ 24.5 m/s ✓
Question 15 — Elasticity and Energy Storage
6 marks

A catapult spring has a spring constant of 400 N/m. It is compressed by 0.15 m.

Calculate the elastic potential energy stored. A 0.05 kg ball is launched. Calculate the maximum speed of the ball (assuming all EPE converts to KE). Explain any assumption you are making.

💡 How to Answer — Elastic PE and Energy Conversion EPE = ½ke². Set EPE = KE = ½mv² and solve for v. State assumptions: no energy lost to friction/heat/sound, all EPE converts to KE. This question always requires stating and justifying assumptions.
EPE = ½ke²  |  KE = ½mv²  |  EPE = KE (if no losses)
  • EPE = ½ke² = ½ × 400 × (0.15)² ✓
  • = ½ × 400 × 0.0225 = 4.5 J ✓
  • Assumption: all elastic PE converts to KE of ball / no energy lost to friction or sound ✓
  • KE = EPE → ½mv² = 4.5 → ½ × 0.05 × v² = 4.5 ✓
  • v² = 4.5 × 2 ÷ 0.05 = 180 ✓
  • v = √180 ≈ 13.4 m/s ✓
Question 16 — Newton's Laws Synthesis
6 marks

A rocket of total mass 5000 kg (including fuel) produces a thrust of 80,000 N at launch. The weight of the rocket is 50,000 N (g = 10 N/kg).

Calculate the initial acceleration. Explain why the acceleration increases as the fuel burns, even if the thrust stays constant.

💡 How to Answer — Multi-Stage Newton's Law Questions Step 1: Find the resultant force (Thrust − Weight). Step 2: Use F = ma to find acceleration. Step 3: Explain what changes as fuel burns: mass decreases → same F, smaller m → greater a (F = ma, so a = F/m). Examiners expect you to link the physics principle to the changing variables.
Resultant F = Thrust − Weight  |  a = F ÷ m
  • Resultant force = Thrust − Weight = 80,000 − 50,000 = 30,000 N ✓
  • a = F ÷ m = 30,000 ÷ 5000 ✓
  • a = 6 m/s² ✓
  • As fuel burns, the mass of the rocket decreases ✓
  • If thrust stays constant, the resultant force also increases (less weight) ✓
  • From F = ma: if F stays same or increases and m decreases, acceleration a = F/m increases ✓
