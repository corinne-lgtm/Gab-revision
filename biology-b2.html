B2.1

Cell division

Chromosomes, DNA and genes; the cell cycle; mitosis and genetically identical daughter cells.

DNA

The molecule that carries genetic information.

Gene

A section of DNA that codes for a particular protein.

Chromosome

A long DNA molecule, packaged with proteins, containing many genes.

Most human body cells contain 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs. Gametes contain 23 chromosomes. A chromosome is not the same thing as a gene: many genes are located on each chromosome.

Explore the cell cycle

Why mitosis matters

It produces genetically identical cells for growth, development, repair and replacement of worn-out cells. It also enables asexual reproduction in some organisms.

Exam precision

DNA is replicated before mitosis. During mitosis, one set of chromosomes is pulled to each end and the nucleus divides. Finally, the cytoplasm and cell membrane divide to form two daughter cells with the same chromosome number.

The three overall stages are required for Trilogy. Names of the individual mitotic phases are not required.
B2.2

Growth and differentiation

How animal and plant cells specialise, meristems, growth and plant cloning.

Growth can occur through cell division, cell enlargement, or both. Differentiation is the process by which a cell becomes specialised for a particular function. Different genes are switched on and off, leading to different structures and proteins.

Animal cells

Most differentiation happens early in development. In mature animals, cell division is mainly for repair and replacement. Some specialised cells, such as mature red blood cells, cannot divide; some tissues retain adult stem cells.

Plant cells

Many plant cells retain the ability to differentiate throughout life. Meristems at root and shoot tips contain cells that divide, then elongate and differentiate into specialised tissues.

Explore a growing root

Cloning plants

Plant tissue culture can produce many genetically identical plants from a small piece of tissue under suitable sterile conditions. Cells divide and differentiate to form new plants.

Why use it?

Rapidly produce plants with desirable characteristics, preserve rare species and create genetically identical specimens for research. Clones share genes, but their environment can affect their appearance.

Cloning does not mean every plant will look exactly the same in every environment. It means the plants are genetically identical.
B2.3

Stem cells

Embryonic, adult and plant stem cells, their potential and their current uses.

Embryonic

Cells from an early embryo can differentiate into most types of body cell.

Adult

Found in some mature tissues. They usually form a more limited range of cell types; bone marrow stem cells produce blood cells.

Plant meristems

Can differentiate into any type of plant cell throughout the plant's life.

A stem cell is an undifferentiated cell that can divide to produce more stem cells and can differentiate into specialised cells. The ability to make specialised cells is what makes stem cells useful for research and potential treatments.

From stem cell to specialised cell

Established use

Blood-forming stem cells from bone marrow or blood are used to treat certain blood cancers and blood disorders. They can replace damaged blood-forming cells.

Research and potential

Scientists investigate stem cells for conditions such as diabetes, spinal cord injury and retinal disease. Some treatments are experimental; the possibility of growing replacement tissues does not mean that all these conditions can currently be cured.

Plant stem cells can be used to clone rare plants for conservation and to produce crops with desirable characteristics, such as disease resistance.
B2.4

Stem cell dilemmas

Benefits, risks, ethical arguments and therapeutic cloning.

Stem-cell research offers potential medical benefits, but it also raises scientific, practical and ethical questions. A balanced GCSE answer should explain the reasoning behind different viewpoints rather than simply listing opinions.

Potential benefits

Replacement of damaged cells, research into disease, testing treatments and the possibility of patient-matched tissues. Adult stem cells can avoid some objections associated with embryos.

Risks and limitations

Uncontrolled cell division may lead to tumours; cells may not differentiate as intended; infection or immune rejection may occur. Treatments can be costly and require extensive testing.

Therapeutic cloning: follow the process

In therapeutic cloning, the nucleus of a donated egg cell is removed and replaced with the nucleus of a body cell from the patient. The egg is stimulated to divide into an early embryo. Stem cells can then be extracted, cultured and encouraged to differentiate. The embryo is destroyed when the stem cells are removed.

The resulting cells have the same nuclear genes as the patient, so the risk of immune rejection may be reduced. This is not a guarantee of no rejection, and the technique is not a routine method for growing replacement organs.

Arguments in favour

Potential to reduce suffering, treat serious disease, improve understanding of development and make use of embryos that would otherwise be discarded.

Arguments against

Some people believe embryos have moral status or a right to life. Others object to creating embryos for research, question consent, or believe resources should be directed towards alternative treatments.

The detailed nuclear-transfer procedure is textbook enrichment. For the AQA specification, focus on the potential uses, benefits, risks and ethical issues of stem cells and therapeutic cloning.
