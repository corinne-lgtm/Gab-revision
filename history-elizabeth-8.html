8.1

Why was there conflict between England and Spain?

The textbook shows a relationship that deteriorated gradually. Religion mattered, but so did the Netherlands, trade, privateering and Elizabeth’s growing support for Spain’s enemies.

From possible ally to enemy

When Elizabeth became queen, Philip II of Spain was not automatically her enemy. He had been married to her half-sister Mary I and even considered marriage with Elizabeth. But England was Protestant, Spain was Catholic, and their interests increasingly clashed.

Choose a factor to see how it pushed the two countries towards war.

Why did the Netherlands matter?

Why Elizabeth wanted to help

Religion: Dutch Protestant rebels were resisting Catholic Spanish rule.

Security: a powerful Spanish presence directly across the Channel worried England.

Trade: English merchants had strong commercial links with the Netherlands, especially the cloth trade.

Why she hesitated

Open support for rebels against another monarch could encourage rebellion against Elizabeth herself and risk direct war with Spain. She therefore moved cautiously before committing troops.

Think causally: there was no single cause. Religious rivalry made the relationship fragile, while events in the Netherlands, trade disputes and English attacks on Spanish wealth turned tension into war.

Source thinking: propaganda and point of view

The textbook includes a Dutch image mocking Philip II and the Duke of Anjou. Use it as evidence of hostile Protestant attitudes, not as a neutral photograph of what happened. Ask who produced it, what message it was trying to send and why.

8.2

The importance of naval warfare

England’s navy benefited from changing ship design, better navigation and tactics that favoured manoeuvrability and long-range gunnery.

Faster ships

More manoeuvrable

Newer English ships were generally lower, faster and easier to turn than many Spanish vessels. This helped English captains choose when and where to fight.

More powerful weapons

Long-range gunnery

English ships aimed to damage Spanish ships from a distance rather than rely mainly on boarding and close combat.

Navigation

Better control at sea

Improved navigation helped sailors plan routes and work out position more accurately, although weather remained a huge danger.

Piracy or good tactics?

The textbook stresses that sixteenth-century sea warfare was not just formal fleet battles. English privateers attacked Spanish shipping and treasure. Drake’s 1587 raid on Cádiz destroyed or disrupted Spanish preparations and delayed the Armada.

Philip’s plan — and its weakness

Philip planned for the Armada to sail through the English Channel, link with the Duke of Parma’s army in the Netherlands, and protect the invasion force crossing to England. The plan depended on coordination between fleet and army. That proved extremely difficult.

Why was the Armada defeated?

English strengths

Spanish problems + circumstances

Best judgement: avoid saying “the weather defeated the Armada” on its own. The Armada had already been disrupted and forced north after Gravelines. Weather then made the return journey much more destructive.

What happened?

May 1588

The Armada left Lisbon, but bad weather forced it back before it sailed again.

July 1588

The Armada entered the English Channel in its defensive crescent formation. English ships attacked from a distance.

Calais

The Spanish anchored while waiting for contact with Parma. English fire ships caused the formation to scatter.

8 August · Gravelines

The English attacked the disorganised Spanish fleet. The Armada could no longer carry out the invasion plan.

Return

The Armada sailed north around Scotland and Ireland. Storms, damage, hunger and disease caused further heavy losses.
