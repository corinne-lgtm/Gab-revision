B11.1

Principles of hormonal control

Learn

Hormones

Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands into the bloodstream. They travel to target organs where cells with the correct receptors respond.

Main endocrine glands

Pituitary, thyroid, pancreas, adrenal glands, ovaries and testes are key endocrine organs.

Hormonal vs nervous control

Hormonal responses are generally slower but longer lasting than nervous responses.

B11.2

The control of blood glucose levels

Learn

Insulin

When blood glucose rises, the pancreas releases insulin. Cells take up more glucose and glucose is converted to glycogen in liver and muscle.

Glucagon

When blood glucose falls, glucagon causes glycogen to be converted back to glucose and released into the blood.

Negative feedback

Insulin and glucagon act in opposite directions to keep blood glucose within a narrow range.

B11.3

Treating diabetes

Learn

Type 1

The pancreas produces little or no insulin. Treatment usually involves insulin injections plus careful monitoring of carbohydrate intake and exercise.

Type 2

Cells become less responsive to insulin. Weight loss, exercise, diet and medicines can improve control; some people eventually need insulin.

Future treatments

Pancreas or insulin-producing cell transplants and stem-cell approaches are being investigated.

B11.4 · Higher tier

The role of negative feedback

Learn

Negative feedback

If a variable rises above its normal level, responses act to lower it. If it falls below normal, responses act to raise it.

Thyroxine

Thyroxine from the thyroid controls basal metabolic rate and contributes to growth and development. TSH from the pituitary stimulates its release.

Adrenaline

Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight by increasing heart rate and breathing rate and making more glucose available.

B11.5

Human reproduction

Learn

Puberty

Sex hormones cause secondary sexual characteristics to develop. Oestrogen is produced mainly by the ovaries; testosterone mainly by the testes.

Female hormones

FSH matures follicles, LH triggers ovulation, and oestrogen and progesterone control the uterine lining.

Male hormones

Testosterone drives male secondary sexual characteristics and supports sperm production.

B11.6 · Higher tier

Hormones and the menstrual cycle

Learn

FSH

FSH stimulates egg maturation and oestrogen production.

Oestrogen and LH

Oestrogen rebuilds the uterine lining and, at high levels, stimulates the LH surge that triggers ovulation.

Progesterone

Progesterone rises after ovulation, maintains the uterine lining and inhibits FSH and LH.

B11.7

The artificial control of fertility

Learn

Hormonal methods

Pills, implants, injections and patches use oestrogen and/or progesterone to prevent ovulation or change the uterine lining and cervical mucus.

Non-hormonal methods

Condoms, diaphragms, copper IUDs, spermicides, abstinence and surgical sterilisation work in different ways.

Choosing a method

Effectiveness, side effects, reversibility, STI protection and convenience all matter.

B11.8 · Higher tier

Infertility treatments

Learn

Fertility drugs

FSH can stimulate several eggs to mature and LH can trigger ovulation.

IVF

Eggs are collected, fertilised with sperm in the laboratory, allowed to develop into embryos and one or more embryos are transferred to the uterus.

Advantages and disadvantages

IVF can help people conceive, but success is not guaranteed and treatment can be costly, stressful and associated with multiple pregnancy.
