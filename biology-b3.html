B3.1

Tissues and organs

How specialised cells work together to build a functioning organism.

Multicellular organisms contain specialised cells organised into tissues, organs and organ systems. Each level works together to carry out a function.

Build an organism: explore each level

1 · Specialised cells

A specialised cell is adapted to carry out a particular job.

What is a tissue?

A group of cells with a similar structure and function working together. Examples include muscle, glandular and epithelial tissue.

What is an organ?

A collection of different tissues working together to perform a particular function.

The stomach is an organ, not just a muscle

  • Muscular tissue contracts to churn and mix food with digestive juices.
  • Glandular tissue produces digestive juices containing enzymes and acid.
  • Epithelial tissue covers and protects the stomach; mucus helps protect its lining.

The pancreas contains glandular tissue that produces digestive enzymes and hormones, including insulin. The digestive system is an organ system: several organs cooperate to digest and absorb food.

B3.2

The human digestive system

Follow food through the body and learn what each organ actually does.

Digestion breaks large, insoluble food molecules into smaller, soluble molecules that can be absorbed into the blood. The digestive system is a muscular tube with associated glands and organs.

Follow a meal through the digestive system

Mouth

Food is chewed and mixed with saliva. Salivary amylase begins starch digestion.

Associated organs

  • Salivary glands produce saliva containing amylase.
  • The liver produces bile.
  • The gall bladder stores bile.
  • The pancreas produces digestive enzymes that enter the small intestine.

Absorption is not digestion

Digestion makes small soluble molecules. Absorption moves them across the intestinal wall into the blood. Villi and microvilli increase surface area; a thin wall and good blood supply help rapid absorption.

The route matters

Food does not travel through the liver or pancreas. Their secretions enter the digestive tract. The small intestine is the main site of digestion and absorption; the large intestine absorbs water from undigested material.

B3.3

The chemistry of food

The building blocks of carbohydrates, proteins and lipids—and how to identify them.

Carbohydrates

Made from sugar units. Glucose is a simple sugar; starch is a large molecule made from many glucose units. Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen and provide energy.

Proteins

Long chains of amino acids folded into specific shapes. Proteins contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. They are used for growth, repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies.

Lipids

Fats and oils are made from glycerol and three fatty acids. They store energy, form part of cell membranes and provide insulation. They are insoluble in water.

Molecules and digestion

Large food molecules must be broken down into smaller soluble products: starch to sugars, proteins to amino acids, and lipids to fatty acids and glycerol.

Food-test laboratory: choose a test

Starch · Iodine solution

Method: Add a few drops of iodine solution to the food sample. No heating is required.
Positive result: Orange-brown → blue-black

Required practical 3: test foods for carbohydrates, lipids and proteins. Use appropriate eye protection and follow school safety procedures; Benedict’s test requires heating in a water bath.
B3.4

Catalysts and enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts: their shape makes reactions possible and specific.

A catalyst increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up. Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts, controlling reactions in living organisms.

Watch an enzyme catalyse a reaction

1 · Substrate approaches

The active site has a specific shape. Only a suitable substrate can bind effectively.

The active site

The substrate has a complementary shape to the enzyme’s active site. An enzyme–substrate complex forms, the reaction occurs, and products leave. The enzyme can be used again.

Metabolism

The sum of all chemical reactions in a cell or organism. Enzymes catalyse building larger molecules, breaking down molecules, respiration and other reactions.

Examples of metabolic reactions

  • Building proteins from amino acids.
  • Making starch, glycogen and cellulose from glucose.
  • Respiration and the breakdown of excess amino acids to form urea.
  • Converting glucose into other carbohydrates and producing lipids.
Enzymes lower activation energy. For GCSE, focus on the active site, specificity, reaction rate and the fact that enzymes are not used up.
B3.5

Factors affecting enzyme action

Investigate temperature and pH, and explain why enzyme activity changes.

Temperature and pH affect enzyme activity. Each enzyme has an optimum at which it works most efficiently. Different enzymes have different optima.

Temperature: explore enzyme activity

0°C · 2% relative activity

At low temperature, molecules have little kinetic energy and successful collisions are infrequent. The curve is an illustrative model, not experimental data.

pH: compare enzyme optima

Pepsin · optimum around pH 2

Pepsin works efficiently in the acidic stomach. Different enzymes have different optimum pH values. This is an illustrative curve.

Below the optimum

Increasing temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy. They move faster and collide more frequently, increasing the rate of successful enzyme–substrate collisions.

Above the optimum

High temperature disrupts bonds holding the protein’s shape. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured and activity falls.

What does pH do?

Changes in pH can alter the bonds maintaining the active site. At unsuitable pH values the enzyme may become denatured. Pepsin works best in acidic conditions, while pancreatic enzymes generally work best in alkaline conditions.

Graph skills

Plot the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis. Label both axes and units, use an appropriate scale and draw a suitable line of best fit. A rate can be calculated as change in quantity divided by time.

B3.6

How the digestive system works

Learn the three digestive enzymes and investigate amylase in a practical.

Carbohydrases

Break carbohydrates into simple sugars. Amylase breaks starch into sugars. It is produced by salivary glands, the pancreas and the small intestine.

Proteases

Break proteins into amino acids. They are produced by the stomach, pancreas and small intestine.

Lipases

Break lipids into fatty acids and glycerol. They are produced by the pancreas and small intestine.

Why digestion is necessary

Large insoluble molecules cannot be absorbed efficiently. Enzymes produce small soluble molecules that can pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.

Required practical 4: investigate amylase and pH

1 · Prepare

Place starch and buffer solutions at the chosen pH in a water bath. Keep the temperature constant. Prepare a spotting tile with iodine drops.

Variables and reliability

  • Independent variable: pH, changed using buffer solutions.
  • Dependent variable: time until starch is no longer detected, or rate calculated as 1 ÷ time.
  • Control temperature, volumes and concentrations of starch and amylase.
  • Repeat measurements, identify anomalies and calculate a mean.
  • Use a water bath and check that the reaction mixture reaches the intended temperature.

Calculate reaction rate

Enter the time taken for starch to disappear. The rate is estimated as 1 ÷ time.

B3.7

Making digestion efficient

How stomach acid, bile and emulsification make digestion more efficient.

Hydrochloric acid

The stomach produces hydrochloric acid, creating a low pH suitable for the protease pepsin. Acid also helps kill many bacteria in food.

Protective mucus

The stomach lining produces mucus that protects it from acid and digestive enzymes. Damage to this protection can contribute to ulcers.

Bile

Produced by the liver, stored in the gall bladder and released into the small intestine. Bile is alkaline and neutralises acid arriving from the stomach.

Emulsification

Bile breaks large fat globules into many smaller droplets. This increases the surface area available to lipase, increasing the rate of lipid digestion. Bile is not an enzyme.

Bile: see why smaller droplets digest faster

Before emulsification

One large globule has a relatively small surface area compared with its volume. Lipase can act only at the surface.

Link the whole process

The stomach provides acidic conditions for pepsin. In the small intestine, bile neutralises the acid so pancreatic enzymes can work in alkaline conditions. Bile also emulsifies fats, increasing the surface area available to lipase.
