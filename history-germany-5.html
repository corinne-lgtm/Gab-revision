5.1
1930–30 January 1933

How did Hitler become Chancellor?

Weimar governments rarely had a majority. Coalition governments were unstable, and President Hindenburg could use Article 48 and appoint new Chancellors in emergencies.

Key timeline

From electoral breakthrough to the Chancellorship

Sept 1930 · Nazis win 107 seats→July 1932 · Nazis win 230 seats→Nov 1932 · Nazi vote falls slightly→von Papen → von Schleicher→30 Jan 1933 · Hitler appointed Chancellor

The 1930 Reichstag election

By September 1930 unemployment had risen to around 3 million. Heinrich Brüning became Chancellor but was unpopular because he reduced unemployment pay and increased taxes.

The Nazis made a major breakthrough, rising from 12 seats to 107 and becoming the second-largest party in the Reichstag.

Support for the Nazis

After the 1930 success, the Nazis worked hard to spread their message. They used millions of posters and flags, and Hitler spoke at huge mass rallies across Germany. His popularity was clearly growing.

Violence on the streets

The SA, Hitler's private army, attacked Communists and disrupted meetings, making it difficult for opponents to campaign freely. Other groups also had their own forces. Street fighting, bomb plots and murders made Germany appear chaotic and strengthened the impression that Weimar government was failing.

Democracy under pressure

Some Germans argued that democracy had already failed because President Hindenburg was increasingly using Article 48 emergency decrees to make decisions without the consent of the democratically elected Reichstag.

By July 1932 the Nazis were the largest political party in the Reichstag.

Hitler still refused the Chancellorship

Hitler demanded the job of Chancellor, but Hindenburg refused because he regarded the Nazis as a disruptive and violent party. Hindenburg instead used emergency powers to appoint Franz von Papen.

November 1932

Von Papen called another election. Nazi votes fell slightly, although the Nazis remained the largest party. Von Papen's Centre Party also lost seats, so he resigned.

Another new Chancellor

Hindenburg appointed his old friend Kurt von Schleicher. He also failed to gain enough support to pass laws and resigned. Germany had now had two Chancellors come and go in less than a year.

30 January 1933

Hindenburg finally appointed Adolf Hitler as Chancellor. He tried to limit Hitler's power by making von Papen Vice Chancellor and allowing Hitler only two fellow Nazis in the cabinet. Hindenburg and his advisers believed they would be able to control him.

Key idea: Hitler did not simply “win an election and become Chancellor”. The Nazis became the largest party, but Hitler's appointment also depended on the failure of successive governments and on Hindenburg's decision to appoint him.

Revision marking points

Hitler did not have complete control: Hindenburg remained President; von Papen was Vice Chancellor; only two other Nazis were allowed in the cabinet; and the Nazis did not hold a majority of Reichstag seats. Political opponents, including the SPD and KPD, still had substantial support.

Coalition government was unstable; Brüning was unpopular and increasingly depended on Article 48; the Nazis became the second-largest party in 1930 and largest in July 1932; von Papen lacked support and resigned; von Schleicher also could not pass laws and resigned.
