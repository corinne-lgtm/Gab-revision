4.1
Formation and Covenant · pp 28–29

Why was the League of Nations created?

Wilson wanted collective security after the First World War, but the new organisation faced contradictions from its birth.

Why establish a League?

The First World War devastated Europe. President Woodrow Wilson proposed a worldwide organisation in his Fourteen Points. It would encourage countries to negotiate rather than resort to war. The Covenant set out its rules and was incorporated into the post-war peace settlement.

Four central aims

Keep peace through cooperation and collective security; encourage disarmament; improve living and working conditions; and fight disease. The League combined political peacekeeping with humanitarian work.

Geneva and membership

Its headquarters were in Geneva, Switzerland, viewed as neutral. There were 42 initial members; by 1934 membership had reached 58. Britain, France, Italy and Japan initially held permanent Council seats. Germany joined in 1926 and left in 1933.

Explore who joined — and who stayed outside

Collective security · the idea

Members would act together against threats to peace. A dispute could first be discussed; members could then apply moral condemnation or economic sanctions. Armed force was a last resort, but the League had no standing army and relied on member countries.

Britain and France · mixed motives

Britain hoped for a forum for discussion without excessive costs; it also gained mandates over former enemy territories. France saw the League as possible protection against further German aggression. Yet neither wanted an organisation powerful enough to override national interests.

Key problem: protecting peace sometimes required costs or risks that powerful members would not accept.

GCSE Exam Tip: Distinguish aims (what the League hoped to do) from powers (how it could act) and limitations (why it sometimes failed). Use membership examples with dates.

Organisation and powers · pp 30–31

The structure of the League

The Assembly, Council, Secretariat, Court and commissions gave the League an ambitious structure — but not equal power to enforce decisions.

Strengths

A large membership, shared Covenant, a forum for dialogue, moral pressure, economic sanctions and expert commissions provided ways to solve disputes without immediately fighting.

Structural weaknesses

The USA never joined; Germany and the USSR were initially absent; the League had no army; unanimity caused delays and the most powerful nations could put their own interests first.

Grade 8–9: A structure was not automatically ineffective: unanimity encouraged agreement, but created a veto problem in a crisis. Explain both sides before judging.

4.3
Social work and agencies · pp 32–33

Did the League of Nations help people?

Beyond peacekeeping, the League’s technical and humanitarian organisations changed lives in practical ways.

Peace was not the only purpose. The League’s agencies helped refugees, workers, public health and the fight against slavery, but successes were uneven.

4.4A
Vilna, Åland, Upper Silesia · pp 34–35

How successful was the League in the 1920s? · Part A

Early crises show the League both preventing conflict and failing against stronger national interests.

Case-study timeline · early 1920s

Vilna, 1920 · failure

Poland seized Vilna, Lithuania’s capital. The League requested withdrawal but Poland refused. France considered Poland an ally against Germany; Britain would not act alone. Outcome: Poland kept control: a strong state could ignore the League.

Åland Islands, 1921 · success

Sweden and Finland both claimed the islands. After investigation the League awarded them to Finland with protections limiting their militarisation. Sweden accepted. Outcome: peaceful settlement prevented armed conflict.

Upper Silesia, 1921–25 · qualified success

A plebiscite found a German majority, but the industrial districts were contested. The League divided the region, leaving most rural areas to Germany and important industrial areas to Poland, arranging cross-border transport and resources. Outcome: war avoided, but both sides complained and relations worsened.

Exam Tip · 8 marks: Chronology + mechanism + outcome. For each crisis explain why the League could or could not act, then compare the results.

4.4B
Corfu, Bulgaria and the Depression · pp 36–37

How successful was the League in the 1920s? · Part B

These cases expose the difference between successes with smaller nations and the organisation’s vulnerability to powerful states.

Case-study timeline · 1923 to 1929

Corfu, 1923 · failure

An Italian general, Tellini, and his team were murdered while surveying the Greek–Albanian frontier. Mussolini blamed Greece and occupied Corfu. The League condemned the aggression, but Mussolini turned to the Conference of Ambassadors. Greece paid compensation and Italian troops withdrew. Significance: Italy, a powerful member, bypassed League authority.

Bulgaria, 1925 · success

After Greek soldiers were killed at the Bulgarian border, Greece invaded. Bulgaria appealed to the League, which ordered Greek withdrawal and compensation. Greece complied. Significance: the League could restrain smaller states more readily than powerful ones.

Wall Street Crash, 1929 · growing weakness

Financial collapse in the USA helped trigger a worldwide Depression. Economic insecurity, job losses and political extremism made governments more inward-looking and less willing to enforce sanctions or act collectively.

Why results were mixed

League expertise and diplomacy could solve disputes where governments accepted mediation. When a major power refused, the League depended on Britain and France, whose national interests often outweighed collective security.

Grade 9 challenge

“The League was more successful in disputes involving smaller countries.” How convincing is this claim? Use Bulgaria, Corfu, Vilna and Åland, then qualify it with Upper Silesia. A strong response explains the pattern, not just the examples.
