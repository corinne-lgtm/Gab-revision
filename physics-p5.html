P5.1 · textbook pages 58–59

Alternating current

Direct current (d.c.) flows in one direction; alternating current (a.c.) repeatedly reverses direction. UK mains is approximately 230 V rms, 50 Hz. The live wire alternates in potential difference relative to earth; neutral is close to 0 V. The National Grid uses step-up transformers to reduce current and energy waste in transmission cables.

Learn

Alternating current

The direction of current reverses periodically. One full cycle takes 0.02 s at 50 Hz.

D.C. vs A.C.

Cells and batteries supply d.c. Domestic mains supplies a.c.

National Grid

Step-up transformers increase p.d. for long-distance transmission. Step-down transformers reduce p.d. for safe use by consumers.

Exam focus: UK 50 Hz mains; distinguish peak voltage from the 230 V rms mains value.

Interactive oscilloscope: a.c. vs d.c.

Toggle the supply; change the frequency and amplitude. A.C. reverses direction, but d.c. stays at one level.

National Grid: why high voltage?

For the same transmitted power, a higher voltage means a smaller current and therefore lower resistive heating losses (I²R).

P5.2 · textbook pages 60–61

Cables and plugs

In a UK three-pin plug, live is brown, neutral is blue and earth is green-and-yellow. The live wire is dangerous because it carries an alternating potential. The earth wire protects users if a fault makes a metal case live; the fuse breaks the circuit when the current becomes excessive. Double-insulated appliances usually have plastic cases and do not need an earth connection.

Learn

Live — brown

Carries the alternating potential difference from the mains. Connected through the fuse.

Neutral — blue

Completes the circuit; normally close to 0 V relative to earth.

Earth — green/yellow

Safety connection to exposed metal cases; carries current in a fault, allowing the fuse to disconnect the supply.

Exam focus: Know plug wire colours and explain protection from electric shock.

Safety challenge

An appliance has a metal case. A fault makes the case live. Why is earthing important?

Fault current flows through the low-resistance earth path, producing a large enough current to disconnect the supply (e.g. via a fuse). This reduces the risk of electric shock. Never handle mains wiring yourself.

P5.3 · textbook pages 62–63

Electrical power and potential difference

Power is the rate of energy transfer. For electrical devices P = IV; for a resistor P = I²R. The correct fuse rating is just above the normal operating current. A fuse protects against excessive current, not by limiting it during normal operation.

Learn

Electrical power

P = IV. For example, 230 V × 2 A = 460 W.

Resistive heating

P = I²R; at fixed resistance, doubling current increases power by a factor of four.

Fuse selection

Find normal current using I = P/V, then choose the smallest standard fuse rating above that current.

Exam focus: Check seconds, watts and fuse rating reasoning.

P5.4 · textbook pages 64–65

Electrical currents and energy transfer

Charge transferred is Q = It. Electrical energy transferred to a component is E = QV, equivalent to E = VIt when current and voltage are constant. In a complete circuit, energy supplied by the battery equals energy transferred by all components and their surroundings.

Learn

Charge

Q = It, measured in coulombs (C). 1 A = 1 C/s.

Energy transfer

E = QV, measured in joules. Each coulomb transfers V joules across a component.

Conservation

The battery transfers energy to devices and the surroundings. The sum of transfers equals the energy supplied.

Exam focus: Show Q = It and E = QV with units.

P5.5 · textbook pages 66–67

Appliances and efficiency

An appliance transfers E = Pt joules when used for t seconds at power P watts. Its electrical input power is P = IV. Efficiency = useful power output ÷ total power input, multiplied by 100 for a percentage. Energy wasted per second equals input power minus useful power.

Learn

Appliance energy

E = Pt. A 1000 W heater used for 30 min transfers 1.8 MJ.

Efficiency

Useful power ÷ input power × 100%. For 60% efficiency, useful power is 0.60 × input power.

Power waste

If input power is 2760 W and useful output is 1656 W, power wasted = 1104 W.

Exam focus: Use E = Pt and convert percentage efficiency to a fraction.
