The big picture

Ethical, legal and environmental issues

Three different questions

Ethical: Is it fair, responsible or morally acceptable?

Legal: Does it comply with laws and rules?

Environmental: How does it affect energy use, materials, waste and pollution?

Privacy: Who collects personal information, how is it used and who can access it?

The GCSE skill: explain both sides

Almost every technology creates benefits and risks. A balanced response considers different groups and reaches a reasoned judgement.

Example — wearable health monitor: It may detect a health problem early, but continuous tracking may expose sensitive personal information if data is shared without consent.

Exam tip: Do not write “it is bad for privacy” alone. Explain what data is collected, who might access it and the consequence.

Privacy and security

Who should be allowed to see data?

Privacy versus public safety

Citizens' view: private messages, locations and medical details should not be accessed unnecessarily. Excessive monitoring can undermine trust and freedom.

Government/security-services view: limited access to information can help investigate serious crime or prevent attacks.

Judgement: distinguish targeted, proportionate access with oversight from unrestricted surveillance.

Cyber security and hacking

Benefit: stronger security protects financial details, medical records and school files.

Risk: unauthorised hacking may steal confidential data, change records or disrupt services.

Legal point: unauthorised access to computer material may be an offence under UK computer misuse law; permitted security testing is different.

Mobile and wireless technology

Connected anywhere — but at what cost?

Mobile phones and wireless networks

Benefits: communication, access to learning, remote work, navigation and emergency support.

Risks: constant tracking, exposure of location data, distraction, digital exclusion and insecure connections.

Ethical debate: should apps track a user's movements by default?

Wearable technology

Fitness trackers and smartwatches collect activity, location and sometimes health-related information.

Benefit: useful personal feedback and emergency alerts.

Risk: employers, insurers or advertisers might infer sensitive information if data is accessed or shared inappropriately.

Worked exam paragraph

“A smartwatch can help a user monitor their activity because it gathers regular measurements. However, those records may reveal sensitive routines or health information. Therefore, users should be able to control what is collected and shared.”

Cloud storage

Convenience versus control

Why use cloud storage?

Files can be accessed from several devices and shared quickly. A provider may offer resilience and backup facilities.

Ethical / privacy risk: sensitive files are held by a third party. Users may not know exactly who has access or where their data is processed.

Legal issue: organisations processing personal data must follow applicable data protection obligations.

Environmental impact

Data centres consume electricity for servers and cooling. Their impact depends partly on energy source, efficiency and equipment life.

However, shared infrastructure may be more efficient than many poorly utilised individual servers; avoid claiming that cloud storage is always better or worse for the environment.

Exam tip: Name a real consequence: loss of access during an outage, unauthorised disclosure, or extra energy demand.

Implants and autonomous vehicles

When computers affect human bodies and lives

Computer-based implants

Examples include digitally controlled implanted medical devices.

Benefits: monitor or support important bodily functions and potentially improve quality of life.

Risks: malfunction, software faults, attacks or sensitive data being exposed.

Ethics: informed consent and careful testing matter when software affects health.

Autonomous vehicles

Benefits: potentially fewer collisions caused by human error, increased independence for some users and more efficient journeys.

Risks: sensor/software failures, difficult decisions in emergencies, security vulnerabilities and questions about responsibility after a collision.

Judgement: potential advantages must be balanced against evidence of safety and accountability.

Environmental impacts

The full life cycle of digital devices

Negative effects

Manufacture: mining raw materials and producing electronics require energy and resources.

Use: devices, wireless networks and data centres use electricity.

Disposal: electronic waste can contain harmful materials; poor recycling wastes valuable resources.

Possible benefits and solutions

Video meetings can reduce some travel; digital systems can optimise energy use and transport.

Repairing, reusing and responsibly recycling devices extends their life. Efficient software, devices and data centres can reduce electricity use.

Balanced point: benefits depend on how technology is manufactured and used.

Strong answer: “A longer device lifespan can reduce e-waste because fewer replacement devices need manufacturing.”

Extended answers

How to earn marks in evaluation questions

Four-step answer structure

1. State a specific benefit.

2. Explain why it occurs and who benefits.

3. Explain a specific risk or disadvantage, again with a consequence.

4. Give a balanced judgement linked to the scenario.

Weak versus strong

Weak: “Cloud is good, but it can get hacked.”

Strong: “Cloud storage lets staff access files from different locations, improving collaboration. However, an account breach may expose confidential files to unauthorised people. Strong authentication and access permissions reduce this risk, so the benefits depend on suitable safeguards.”

Remember the stakeholders

Users • businesses • employees • vulnerable people • governments • the wider public • the environment.

Ask: Who gains? Who might be harmed? How likely or serious is the impact? What safeguards change the balance?
