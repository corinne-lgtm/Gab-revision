Section A · Bioenergetics · B8–B9
1
Describe two features of… · 4 marks
Describe two ways in which a leaf is adapted to maximise the rate of photosynthesis.
4 marks

Feature + explanation of HOW it helps photosynthesis happen faster. Think about: light absorption, CO₂ entry, chlorophyll location, surface area, stomata.

Any two (1 mark adaptation + 1 mark explanation):

  • Large, flat surface area → maximises light absorption ✓
  • Thin → short diffusion distance for CO₂ to reach mesophyll cells ✓
  • Stomata on the underside → allow CO₂ to enter and O₂ to leave by diffusion ✓
  • Palisade cells packed with chloroplasts near the top → near the light source; chlorophyll absorbs maximum light ✓
  • Transparent epidermis → allows light to pass through to photosynthetic cells ✓
  • Vascular bundles (xylem/phloem) → supply water for photosynthesis and remove glucose ✓
2
Analyse and explain · 6 marks
A student measures the rate of oxygen production by a pond plant at different light intensities. The results are shown below. Describe and explain the pattern shown in the graph.
6 marks
Described Graph Data

From light intensity 0 to 4 (arbitrary units), the rate of O₂ production increases steeply from 0 to 16 mm³/min. From intensity 4 to 8, the rate continues to increase but more slowly, reaching 22 mm³/min. Above intensity 8, the rate levels off and remains at 22 mm³/min even as light intensity continues to increase.

Describe what happens at each section of the graph (use data values). Then explain why using the concept of limiting factors. The flat section is key — what is limiting the rate when more light doesn't help?

Describe (up to 3):

  • From 0–4 units, rate increases steeply from 0 to 16 mm³/min ✓
  • From 4–8 units, rate continues to increase but more slowly ✓
  • Above 8 units, rate levels off at ~22 mm³/min / rate is constant ✓

Explain (up to 3):

  • At low intensities, light is the limiting factor → more light = faster rate ✓
  • As light increases, another factor (CO₂ or temperature) becomes limiting ✓
  • Above intensity 8, light is no longer limiting — CO₂ concentration or temperature is the limiting factor, so increasing light has no further effect ✓
  • The plant cannot photosynthesise any faster regardless of light because another factor is at a limiting level ✓
3
Compare and explain · 8 marks
Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration. In your answer, include the reactants, products, location in the cell, and the relative amount of energy released by each process.
8 marks

A comparison question — go through each criterion systematically. The examiner wants to see you explicitly compare (not just describe each one separately). Use phrases like "whereas", "in contrast", "both… but…". Include the word equations for both.

  • Aerobic: glucose + oxygen → CO₂ + water + LOTS of ATP (in mitochondria)
  • Anaerobic (humans): glucose → lactic acid + small amount of ATP (in cytoplasm)
  • Anaerobic (yeast/plants): glucose → ethanol + CO₂ + small amount of ATP
LevelMarksDescription
Level 37–8Full comparison of all four criteria with accurate word equations for both. Clear, structured response. Correct use of terminology throughout.
Level 24–6Compares at least 3 criteria accurately. May have minor errors. Some structure.
Level 11–3Describes one or two aspects of one process. Limited comparison. Basic or missing word equations.

Key points:

  • Reactants: Both use glucose. Aerobic also requires oxygen; anaerobic does NOT require oxygen ✓
  • Products (aerobic): Carbon dioxide + water ✓
  • Products (anaerobic — humans): Lactic acid ✓
  • Products (anaerobic — yeast): Ethanol + carbon dioxide ✓
  • Location: Aerobic mainly in mitochondria; anaerobic in cytoplasm ✓
  • Energy: Aerobic releases much more ATP (~38 per glucose); anaerobic releases much less (~2 ATP) ✓
  • When used: Aerobic = normal conditions; anaerobic = when oxygen is insufficient (e.g. intense exercise) ✓
  • Oxygen debt: Lactic acid must be broken down after exercise using extra oxygen ✓
Section B · Non-communicable Disease and Bioenergetics · B7–B8
4
Explain · 6 marks
Explain the difference between a benign and a malignant tumour. Describe two factors that increase the risk of developing cancer.
6 marks

Two parts: (1) explain benign vs malignant clearly — the key difference is whether it spreads (metastasis). (2) Give two risk factors with a brief explanation of how/why each increases cancer risk.

Benign vs Malignant (up to 3):

  • Benign: tumour stays in one place / does not spread / contained by a membrane ✓
  • Malignant: tumour invades and destroys surrounding tissue ✓
  • Malignant tumour cells can break off and spread through blood/lymph to form secondary tumours elsewhere (metastasis) ✓
  • Benign tumours are generally less dangerous; malignant tumours = cancer ✓

Risk factors (1 mark each factor + 1 mark explanation):

  • Smoking → carcinogens in tobacco smoke cause mutations in DNA that can trigger uncontrolled cell division ✓
  • UV radiation → damages DNA in skin cells, increasing risk of melanoma ✓
  • Viral infections → some viruses (e.g. HPV) can insert genetic material that disrupts the cell cycle ✓
  • Inherited gene mutations → BRCA1/2 mutations increase breast cancer risk ✓
  • Ionising radiation → X-rays/gamma rays can mutate DNA ✓
Section C · Extended Response · 46 marks

Extended response · 46 marks (6+6+6+6+6+8+8)
This section contains seven questions. Answer all of them.
46 marks total

5a. Explain the role of the liver in the body's response to exercise and how it relates to respiration. [6 marks]

Focus on lactic acid → glucose (gluconeogenesis), oxygen debt, and also glycogen storage/release. Link each point to respiration clearly.

  • During intense exercise, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid in the muscles ✓
  • Lactic acid builds up and causes muscle fatigue / cramp ✓
  • After exercise, the liver converts lactic acid back into glucose (gluconeogenesis) ✓
  • This process requires oxygen → hence "oxygen debt" must be repaid by continued deep breathing ✓
  • The liver also stores glucose as glycogen; during exercise it breaks glycogen down to release glucose for aerobic respiration ✓
  • The liver maintains blood glucose levels to ensure muscles have a steady supply for respiration ✓

5b. Describe how scientists use greenhouse conditions to maximise crop yield, linking your answer to the factors that affect photosynthesis. [6 marks]

For each factor (light, CO₂, temperature), explain how it affects photosynthesis AND how a greenhouse controls it. Mention the economic balance too — adding CO₂ or heating costs money.

  • CO₂: pumping CO₂ into greenhouses increases its concentration → removes CO₂ as limiting factor → higher rate of photosynthesis → more glucose for growth ✓
  • Light: artificial lighting extends growing hours beyond daylight → more photosynthesis ✓
  • Temperature: heating the greenhouse to optimum temperature speeds up enzyme reactions involved in photosynthesis ✓
  • All three factors can act as limiting factors — greenhouse controls them simultaneously ✓
  • Cost-benefit analysis: extra CO₂ / heating costs money; farmers must ensure the increase in yield is worth the extra expense ✓
  • Higher yield = more glucose produced → more biomass → larger, faster-growing crops ✓

5c. Explain how malnutrition and an unbalanced diet can lead to non-communicable diseases. [6 marks]

Give specific examples — too much saturated fat → CVD, too much sugar → obesity/type 2 diabetes, too little vitamin C → scurvy. For each, explain the biological mechanism, not just the association.

  • Too much saturated fat → raises LDL cholesterol → atherosclerosis → cardiovascular disease ✓
  • Excessive sugar/carbohydrate intake → obesity → increases risk of type 2 diabetes (insulin resistance) ✓
  • Too little vitamin C → scurvy (deficiency disease); vitamin C needed for collagen synthesis ✓
  • Too little iron → anaemia; iron needed for haemoglobin production → fewer red blood cells → less oxygen transport ✓
  • Obesity (excess calorie intake) increases risk of cancer, CVD, and type 2 diabetes ✓
  • Malnutrition weakens immune system → increased susceptibility to communicable diseases too ✓

5d. A student carries out an experiment to investigate the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis using pondweed. Describe a suitable method and explain how you would ensure the results are valid. [6 marks]

Required practical style. Method: lamp distance, count bubbles/measure O₂, controlled variables. Validity: control variables (CO₂, temperature, same piece of pondweed, same time interval). This is a classic question — be specific about what you measure and what you control.

Method (any 3):

  • Place pondweed in a beaker of water with sodium hydrogen carbonate (to supply CO₂) ✓
  • Place a lamp at different distances from the pondweed (e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 cm) ✓
  • Count the number of oxygen bubbles produced per minute at each distance ✓
  • Allow the pondweed to equilibrate at each distance before counting ✓

Validity/controls (any 3):

  • Keep temperature constant (e.g. water bath) so it is not a confounding variable ✓
  • Keep CO₂ concentration constant (same amount of sodium hydrogen carbonate) ✓
  • Use the same piece of pondweed throughout ✓
  • Count bubbles for the same time period at each distance ✓
  • Repeat each distance and calculate a mean to improve reliability ✓
  • Use a black card/shield to block background light ✓

5e. Explain how plants use glucose produced in photosynthesis. Give at least four different uses. [6 marks]

Name the use AND explain why the plant needs to do it. Don't just list: "converted to starch for storage because starch is insoluble and does not affect osmosis." Aim for 4 uses with brief explanations each.

  • Respiration — glucose is broken down to release energy (ATP) for cellular processes ✓
  • Starch storage — converted to insoluble starch in leaves/roots; doesn't affect osmosis; released when needed ✓
  • Cellulose — used to make cell walls, providing structural support ✓
  • Sucrose — transported in phloem to growing regions and storage organs ✓
  • Lipids — converted to fats/oils for energy storage in seeds ✓
  • Amino acids — combined with nitrate ions from soil to make amino acids → proteins for growth and enzymes ✓

5f. "Lifestyle factors are the main cause of non-communicable diseases in the UK." Evaluate this statement. [8 marks]

"Evaluate" = give both sides AND reach a conclusion. Use data/examples you know to support and challenge the statement. Don't forget: genetics, age, sex are also factors — and some non-communicable diseases have little to do with lifestyle (e.g. genetic conditions).

  • FOR: smoking → lung cancer; diet → obesity/CVD; alcohol → liver disease
  • AGAINST: genetic factors, age, environmental carcinogens not lifestyle-related
  • Conclusion: must weigh which is the MAIN cause overall

Level 37–8Detailed evidence both for and against the statement with specific named diseases and risk factors. Reaches a clear, justified conclusion. Well organised.
Level 24–6Some evidence on both sides. At least 3 specific examples. Some attempt at conclusion.
Level 11–3Mostly one-sided. Few specific examples. Little or no conclusion.

FOR the statement:

  • Smoking → lung cancer, COPD, cardiovascular disease; a clear avoidable lifestyle choice ✓
  • Obesity (excess calories/sedentary lifestyle) → type 2 diabetes, CVD, certain cancers ✓
  • Alcohol → liver cirrhosis, liver cancer, pancreatitis ✓
  • Poor diet (high saturated fat) → atherosclerosis, heart disease ✓
  • Lack of exercise → increases cardiovascular disease risk, obesity ✓

AGAINST the statement:

  • Genetic factors cause conditions like cystic fibrosis, Huntington's, some breast cancers (BRCA mutations) — nothing to do with lifestyle ✓
  • Age is a major non-modifiable risk factor for most cancers ✓
  • Environmental carcinogens (UV, radon gas, industrial chemicals) not directly lifestyle choices ✓
  • Some people develop CVD with no lifestyle risk factors due to genetics ✓

Conclusion:

  • A balanced conclusion — e.g. "Lifestyle factors ARE the main cause of the most common non-communicable diseases in the UK (CVD, type 2 diabetes, lung cancer), but genetic and environmental factors also play a significant role in some conditions" ✓✓

5g. A patient has type 2 diabetes. Explain what type 2 diabetes is, what causes it, and discuss possible treatments and lifestyle changes that could help. [8 marks]

Three parts: (1) what it is — cells become resistant to insulin; blood glucose stays high. (2) Causes — obesity, poor diet, genetics. (3) Treatments/lifestyle — diet, exercise, medication, weight loss. For full marks, explain the biology behind each treatment, not just name it.

LevelMarksDescription
Level 37–8Clear explanation of what type 2 diabetes is (insulin resistance), its causes, and at least 3 treatments/lifestyle changes with biological explanation. Well-structured.
Level 24–6Describes what it is and gives 2 treatments. Some biological explanation. Some structure.
Level 11–3Basic description only. May confuse type 1 and type 2. Limited treatment suggestions.

What it is (up to 2):

  • The body's cells become resistant to insulin / do not respond to insulin normally ✓
  • Blood glucose cannot be absorbed into cells → blood glucose remains abnormally high ✓
  • (NB: in type 2 the pancreas may still produce some insulin, unlike type 1) ✓

Causes (up to 2):

  • Obesity / being overweight is the main risk factor ✓
  • Poor diet (high sugar, high calorie, low fibre) over a long period ✓
  • Genetic predisposition / family history ✓

Treatments/lifestyle changes (up to 4):

  • Reduced sugar/carbohydrate diet → lowers blood glucose spikes ✓
  • Increased exercise → muscles use more glucose; improves insulin sensitivity ✓
  • Weight loss → reduces insulin resistance; can reverse type 2 diabetes in early stages ✓
  • Medication (e.g. metformin) → reduces glucose production in liver and improves insulin sensitivity ✓
  • In severe cases, insulin injections may eventually be needed ✓
