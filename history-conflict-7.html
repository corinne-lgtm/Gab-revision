7.1
Foreign-policy aims · pp 54–55

What did Hitler want?

Hitler's aims challenged the post-1919 settlement. Understand the difference between a policy, its motivation and the threat it posed to international peace.

Six aims — explore the threat

Choose an aim to see what it meant, why Hitler wanted it and why other states might see danger.

From the Depression to power

Hitler became German Chancellor in January 1933. The Nazi Party exploited high unemployment, resentment of Versailles and promises to restore German power.

Hitler had already developed his ideology in Mein Kampf, written after the failed Munich Putsch of 1923. His goals were not simply to reverse one treaty clause: they imagined a fundamentally different Europe.

Vocabulary that earns marks

Lebensraum = ‘living space’, especially to expand German control eastwards.

Volksdeutsche = ethnic Germans living outside Germany.

Anschluss = union between Germany and Austria.

Rearmament = rebuilding armed forces restricted by Versailles.

Bolshevism = revolutionary communism, which Hitler opposed.

How to analyse Hitler's rhetoric

In Source B, Hitler speaks of overturning the postwar leadership and restoring a ‘Germany of power and greatness’. This reveals his claimed aims and persuasive language, but it does not prove that every promise would be implemented or that Germans universally agreed.

Contextual link: the Nazi message exploited bitterness about defeat, the Treaty of Versailles and economic insecurity.

Interactive cause → conflict challenge

Why could several of Hitler's aims lead to war?

Grade 8–9 technique: do not just list aims. Make a chain: aim → action required → why that breaches an agreement or threatens another state → likely international response. Also distinguish Hitler's ideology from the causes of another world war: other states' decisions mattered.
7.2
International responses · pp 56–57

How did other countries react to Hitler’s foreign policies?

Governments did not respond in the same way. Their domestic pressures, security concerns and attitudes to war influenced their decisions.

Britain — appeasement

Neville Chamberlain, Prime Minister from 1937, sought to avoid another devastating war by making concessions he hoped would satisfy some German demands. Appeasement means conceding limited demands to avoid a wider conflict.

Reasons included memories of the First World War, fear of a new war, views that parts of Versailles were unfair, economic pressures and concerns about communism. Critics including Winston Churchill argued that concessions risked encouraging Hitler.

In 1935 Britain, France and Italy formed the Stresa Front to oppose German breaches of Versailles; the alliance soon weakened.

France — exposed but constrained

France had a direct security interest in limiting Germany, but suffered political instability, the Depression and fears about military action without Britain's support.

France signed a mutual-assistance treaty with the USSR in 1935, but mistrust and practical limits weakened the possibility of a unified response.

Evaluation: a country could fear Hitler and still lack political agreement or confidence to act.

USSR — threatened and suspicious

Stalin viewed Nazi anti-communism and expansion eastwards as serious threats. The USSR joined the League in 1934 and reached a mutual-assistance arrangement with France in 1935.

However, mistrust between communist Soviet leaders and Western capitalist democracies complicated collective resistance.

USA — isolationism

Many Americans wanted to avoid becoming involved in another European war. Franklin D. Roosevelt focused heavily on tackling the effects of the Great Depression.

American isolationist opinion and neutrality policy restricted international involvement. The US remained outside the League of Nations, reducing its ability to enforce collective measures.

Why was Hitler not stopped earlier?

Fear of another warThe DepressionDifferent national interestsAnti-communismWeak collective security

Do not treat these as interchangeable: for Britain, fear of war and a willingness to revise Versailles were significant; France faced geographical vulnerability and instability; the USSR feared Nazi expansion; the USA preferred non-intervention.

16-mark judgement: ‘Fear of war’ is a strong reason, but it is not the only reason. Compare it with economic hardship, lack of trust and the belief that some of Hitler's demands were reasonable. Use evidence for each country, then weigh significance.
7.3
1933–35 · pp 58–59

German rearmament and the road to war

Between 1933 and 1935, Hitler tested the peace settlement. Each response shaped his assessment of what he could attempt next.

Interactive 1933–35 timeline

Click each event for its outcome and its significance.

1933: Leaving the disarmament talks

Hitler insisted Germany should enjoy equal armaments or other powers should disarm. France refused the proposed equality. In October 1933, Germany withdrew from the Disarmament Conference and the League of Nations.

Why it mattered: the move weakened cooperation and freed Hitler from having to appear to work within the League's framework.

1934: The Dollfuss Affair

Austrian Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss opposed union with Nazi Germany and banned the Austrian Nazi Party. In July 1934 Austrian Nazis murdered him during a failed coup.

Mussolini, who wanted Austria to remain independent at the time, moved Italian troops towards the Austrian border. Hitler backed down, revealing limits to Germany's power in 1934.

Source B poster: This is mainly a propaganda image supporting the Saar’s return to Germany. The important contextual points are: it was published just before the 1935 plebiscite, it aimed to persuade German-speaking voters, and it helped Hitler present the result as support for uniting Germans.

January 1935: The Saar plebiscite

The Saar's coal-rich territory had been administered by the League for 15 years under the Treaty of Versailles. In the 13 January 1935 vote, over 90% voted to return to Germany.

This was a propaganda success and strengthened German industry. However, the vote was provided for in the treaty: the Saar's return was not itself an illegal breach of Versailles.

March 1935: Rearmament and conscription

Germany openly announced a larger army and the return of compulsory military service in March 1935. Hitler also developed a new air force, the Luftwaffe, contrary to restrictions imposed after 1919.

Other powers protested but took no decisive military action. Hitler learned that open violations might be tolerated.

April 1935: The Stresa Front

Britain, France and Italy agreed to oppose further German violations and uphold the independence of Austria. Yet divergent priorities and the coming Abyssinian crisis made the united front fragile.

June 1935: Anglo-German Naval Agreement

Britain permitted Germany to build a navy up to 35% of the British surface fleet's tonnage, while submarine provisions were more permissive.

This undermined Versailles restrictions and angered France and Italy, who were not consulted. It suggested Britain might negotiate directly with Hitler rather than enforce a united policy.

Was this a Versailles violation?

Choose an event. The distinction is a common examination trap.

8-mark ‘Write an account’: follow chronological order and explain links: 1933 withdrawal → 1934 failed Austrian coup → early 1935 Saar vote → March rearmament → Stresa response → June naval agreement. Separate legal treaty arrangements from breaches.

4. Write an account of how Hitler broke the military terms of the Treaty of Versailles between 1933 and 1935.

Writing guidance (no answer revealed)

Write chronologically with causal links: League/disarmament withdrawal (1933), secret/open rearmament, conscription and Luftwaffe (1935), reactions, Stresa and naval deal.

Indicative marking points — original revision exercise

1933 withdrawal from talks was a diplomatic signal; Germany was rebuilding forces; March 1935 conscription, military expansion and the Luftwaffe openly breached Versailles. Britain, France and Italy responded at Stresa, but Britain’s June naval agreement weakened common action. Saar return was not itself a breach.
