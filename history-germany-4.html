Chapter 4 · The Depression and the rise of the Nazis

How the Wall Street Crash damaged Germany, why Nazi support grew so quickly after 1929, and which groups were attracted to Hitler and the Nazi Party.

4.1
1929–33

How did the Depression affect Germany?

Germany's recovery had depended heavily on American money. The Wall Street Crash turned that dependence into a severe economic and political crisis.

Cause chain

From Wall Street to German unemployment

Wall Street Crash · October 1929→US banks demand loans back→German banks & firms struggle→Factories close / production falls→Millions lose jobs→Hunger, anger & political radicalisation

Why Germany was vulnerable

Since 1924 Germany had borrowed large amounts from the USA. American loans helped build factories and businesses and created jobs. After the crash, Americans bought fewer foreign goods and American banks demanded their money back. German banks then tried to recover loans from German businesses, some of which could not repay and went bankrupt.

Impact on ordinary Germans

Factories producing goods such as cars, electrical products and clothing closed as sales fell. Unemployment rose from roughly 1.5 million in 1929 to almost 6 million by 1932. Many Germans became homeless, hungry, angry and disillusioned with the Weimar system.

Support for extremes

Millions blamed the Weimar government for failing to solve unemployment and poverty. Some listened to radical parties on the left and right that promised dramatic solutions. The textbook interpretations show that desperation could push people toward either Nazism or communism.

Antisemitism

The source notes that Jewish Germans were a small, largely assimilated minority, but antisemitism was widespread in parts of Europe. The Nazis exploited prejudice by falsely blaming Jewish people for Germany's problems.

4.2A
Why Nazi support grew

The growth of the Nazi Party

In 1928 the Nazis were only the eighth most popular political party. By July 1932 they were the largest party in the Reichstag, with nearly 14 million votes.

Factor 1 · The Depression

Economic collapse made old Nazi messages more attractive. The Nazis continued to attack Versailles, blame Germany's problems on enemies and promise national rebirth, jobs and strong government. Ideas that had attracted limited support in the 1920s now reached desperate voters.

Factor 2 · Weimar government weakness

Politicians seemed unable to solve the crisis. Chancellors Müller and Brüning struggled, while Brüning increasingly relied on President Hindenburg's Article 48 emergency decrees. Many voters concluded parliamentary democracy was not working.

Factor 3 · Hitler's appeal

Hitler was a charismatic speaker who presented himself as a powerful, decisive leader. He promised hope, national strength and economic recovery at a time of unemployment and political chaos.

Election results · Reichstag seats

The Nazi breakthrough

May 1928 · 12 seats→Sept 1930 · 107→July 1932 · 230→Nov 1932 · 196

The rise was dramatic, although support dipped in November 1932. At the same time, the Communist Party also grew from 54 seats in 1928 to 100 in November 1932, increasing middle- and upper-class fear of communism.

4.2B
Organisation, fear and propaganda

How did Nazi methods help them grow?

The Depression created an opportunity, but Nazi organisation and campaigning helped the party exploit it.

Factor 4 · Fear of communism

Many middle- and upper-class Germans, landowners and business owners feared a communist revolution like Russia's. Hitler promised to fight communism. The SA also fought communist gangs, making the Nazis attractive to voters who feared losing property, wealth or social position.

Factor 5 · Party structure & tactics

The Nazis built local offices across Germany, expanded membership and used a mixture of persuasion and intimidation. Party membership rose from about 100,000 in 1929 to 400,000 by 1932.

SA

The Stormtroopers protected Nazi meetings and intimidated opponents and voters at elections.

Propaganda

Joseph Goebbels led Nazi propaganda from 1928. The party used radio broadcasts, cinema news reports, newspapers, leaflets and posters to spread its message.

Modern campaigning

Hitler appeared at rallies across Germany and in 1932 used an aeroplane to reach 20 cities in seven days. Radio amplified his image as a modern national leader.

Youth organisation: the Hitler Youth encouraged younger followers. Special clubs and camps were organised where young Nazis could exercise, read maps and build campfires.
4.3
A broad coalition of support

Who voted for the Nazis?

Nazi voters did not come from one single social group. The party tailored different promises to different fears and interests.

Farmers & rural voters

The Nazis were popular in the countryside. Farmers had suffered badly during the Depression as people bought less food and prices fell. Hitler promised higher prices, better living standards and protection from communism.

Middle classes

Small business owners, bank workers, doctors and managers feared unemployment, disorder and communism. They were frustrated by Weimar coalition politics and were attracted by Nazi promises of order, discipline and decisive government.

Women

The Nazis appealed to many women through messages about family life, morality and discipline, and claimed they would restore order after years of social change.

Young people

Hitler's military image, attacks on Versailles and promises of jobs, armed forces, homes, roads and hospitals appealed to some younger voters who wanted a stronger future.

Upper classes & business

Some wealthy voters and factory owners liked promises of strong leadership, national power and anti-communism. Hitler also promised to allow them to keep and run their businesses.

Important: not everyone voted Nazi. The textbook includes a later recollection from a former German soldier who disliked Hitler's appearance, shouting and simplistic message. Nazi support was large and diverse, but never universal.

1 · In what ways were the lives of Germans affected by the Depression? Explain your answer.

8 marks

Tip

  • Do not simply define the Depression.
  • Focus on effects on people's lives: work, money, housing, food and attitudes.
  • Explain several consequences rather than listing them.
Revision marking points

American loans were recalled; banks and firms failed; factories closed; unemployment rose to almost six million by 1932; families faced poverty, hunger and homelessness; anger at Weimar increased; and some Germans became more willing to support extremist parties.

2 · Explain why Nazi support increased between 1929 and 1932.

8 marks

Tip

  • Use linked causes, not a shopping list.
  • Possible factors: Depression, Weimar weakness, Hitler, communism, propaganda and organisation.
  • Show why each factor made people more likely to vote Nazi.
Revision marking points

Strong answers can connect mass unemployment and disappointment with Weimar to the attraction of Nazi promises, Hitler's leadership image, fear of communism, SA intimidation, Goebbels' propaganda, local party organisation and modern campaigning.

3 · Which was more important in increasing Nazi popularity: fear of communists or the appeal of Hitler and the Nazi Party?

12 marks

Judgement tip

  • Write about both bullet points.
  • For communism: consider middle/upper classes, business, property and the SA.
  • For Nazi appeal: consider Hitler, propaganda, organisation, promises and modern campaigning.
  • Conclude which had the edge and why.
Revision marking points

Fear of communism helped win support among middle and upper classes who feared revolution and loss of property. Nazi appeal reached more widely through Hitler's charismatic leadership, promises of work/order/national revival, propaganda, SA activity and a highly organised national campaign. A top answer makes a comparative judgement rather than treating the factors separately.

8 marks

Tip

Farmers wanted better prices and feared communism; middle classes wanted order and feared unemployment/communism; some women responded to messages about family and morality; some young people liked military strength and promised opportunities; some wealthy people and business owners valued anti-communism, strong government and protection of business.
