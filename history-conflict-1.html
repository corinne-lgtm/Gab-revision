Aims of the Peacemakers

Why did the winners of the First World War disagree about peace — and why did they compromise? Learn the motivations, clashes and pressures that shaped the Paris Peace Conference.

The big question: the war left Europe devastated. The peacemakers had to balance punishment, security, a lasting peace and pressure from voters at home. Those goals often contradicted one another.
1.1
Peacemaking · pages 8–9

The aims of Clemenceau, Wilson and Lloyd George

The ‘Big Three’ were the leaders of France, the USA and Britain at the Paris Peace Conference. They shared a victory — not a vision of peace.

The human cost

Approximately 8 million soldiers and 8 million civilians died in the First World War, according to the coursebook. Towns, farms, railways and industry were devastated.

Historical significance: leaders were under pressure to prevent another war, but disagreed about how.

Paris, January 1919

Representatives of 32 victorious countries attended the Paris Peace Conference at Versailles, near Paris. France, Britain and the USA dominated the negotiations.

Who was excluded?

The defeated powers, including Germany, did not negotiate the treaty on equal terms. This would later contribute to resentment over the peace settlement.

Additional exam context: Germany was presented with terms rather than treated as a negotiating equal.

Meet the Big Three · select a leader

Connect each leader's war experience to their political objectives.

Georges Clemenceau 🇫🇷

France: much fighting took place on French soil; immense damage and loss of life.

Aim: weaken Germany permanently: smaller armed forces, greater French security, the Rhineland as a protective frontier, and compensation to rebuild France.

Why? France feared another German invasion and voters wanted Germany punished.

David Lloyd George 🇬🇧

Britain: heavy wartime losses but little destruction on its own soil.

Aim: make Germany pay, reduce its navy and take its colonies, yet avoid permanently ruining Germany.

Why? Britain wanted trade, a powerful Royal Navy, a secure empire and a balance of power in Europe. British voters demanded toughness.

Woodrow Wilson 🇺🇸

USA: no fighting on American soil; the USA supplied the Allies during the war.

Aim: a fairer peace based on his Fourteen Points: self-determination, open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, disarmament and a League of Nations.

Why? He believed cooperation was safer than revenge and feared a harsh treaty would encourage another war.

1.2
Peacemaking · pages 10–11

Why were the ‘Big Three’ willing to compromise?

Each leader had strong principles and promises to voters, but their aims clashed. They also faced commitments made during the war, an unstable Europe and the need for a settlement.

Armistice or treaty? · interactive timeline

Select a date to see what happened. Do not confuse the agreement to stop fighting with the later peace settlement.

1 · The armistice and expectations

An armistice is an agreement to stop fighting. Germany signed one on 11 November 1918. The book links its terms and understandings to later demands for reparations, the return of Alsace-Lorraine and withdrawal from the Rhineland.

Key distinction: the armistice stopped the war; the Treaty of Versailles, signed in June 1919, set out the formal peace terms. Do not say the full Treaty of Versailles was signed in November 1918.

2 · Promises made during the war

Britain, France and their allies had made promises to other countries for their support. The book highlights Italy's claim to former Austro-Hungarian lands and Japan's claims in China.

Why compromise? These commitments competed with Wilson's idea of self-determination, creating further disagreements and a need to negotiate.

3 · Conflicting interests

Clemenceau wanted a weak Germany and protection for France. Wilson wanted a fair settlement. Lloyd George wanted Germany penalised but economically viable enough to trade and help keep Europe stable.

Domestic pressure: during the 1918 British election, the slogan about squeezing the German lemon until the pips squeaked captured public demands for punishment.

4 · Changing Europe and urgency

Austria-Hungary was disintegrating into successor states. The 1917 Russian Revolution brought the Bolsheviks/Communists to power and increased fears of further revolution.

Leaders were under pressure to make decisions and begin reconstruction. Negotiations began in January 1919; the Treaty of Versailles was signed in June, although the wider conference continued.

Why compromise was unavoidable

Different aims→Clashing promises→Unstable Europe→Pressure to reach agreement→Compromise

Crucial distinction: being willing to compromise does not mean agreeing on everything. Each leader accepted some limits to secure the priorities he considered most important.

Consequences · test the logic

France's devastation

Increased pressure for harsh security guarantees and reparations.

British trade interests

Made Lloyd George cautious about permanently destroying Germany's economy.

Wilson's Fourteen Points

Introduced the principles of fair peace and self-determination into disputes over borders and empire.

Revolution and instability

Made some leaders fear that a powerless Germany would leave central Europe vulnerable to communism.

GCSE Exam Tip · write an account (8 marks): Put developments into a logical sequence. For each stage explain how it made agreement difficult. A high-level answer links events and pressure rather than giving four unrelated facts.
Revision vocabulary: armistice = ceasefire agreement; treaty = formal peace agreement; reparations = payments for damage; disarmament = reduction in weapons and armed forces; self-determination = peoples deciding their political future; Rhineland = western German region on France's frontier; Allies = countries fighting together against Germany and its partners.
