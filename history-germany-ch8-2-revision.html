8.2 Women in Nazi Germany

Revision notes designed to make sense without needing the textbook.

🏠 Main hub 📚 History index 📚 Germany index ✏️ Quiz rapide
The Nazis said women should focus on children, church and cooking, but the reality was more complicated, especially once war began.
🎯

Objectives

  • Summarise Nazi attitudes towards women.
  • Explain how Nazi policies affected women and families.
  • Judge whether Nazi policies towards women were successful.

1. What did the Nazis believe about women?

🏠

Kinder, Kirche, Küche

The Nazis believed women’s main role was to be wives and mothers. Their slogan meant children, church and cooking.

👶

More babies

Hitler wanted a larger German population so Germany could expand. Women were encouraged to have lots of children.

🚫

Not equal

The Nazis did not believe women should have the same public role as men. Women were discouraged from politics, top jobs and university careers.

2. How did the Nazis try to control women?

Marriage loans

Newly married couples could receive a loan to encourage them to start a family. For each child, part of the loan was cancelled.

Motherhood medals

Women were rewarded for having many children. Mothers with eight children could receive the Gold Cross.

Contraception and abortion

Contraception and abortion were discouraged because the Nazis wanted more “Aryan” children.

Behaviour rules

Women were discouraged from smoking, wearing trousers or high heels, and slimming, because this was seen as “unfeminine”.

3. Nazi organisations for women

German Women’s League

This organisation coordinated women’s groups and spread Nazi ideas about family, motherhood, cooking and childcare.

Motherhood training

By 1939, about 1.7 million women had attended motherhood training courses.

4. Were the policies successful?

📈

Some success

The birth rate rose. There were around 970,000 babies born in Germany in 1933 and about 1,413,000 in 1939.

⚠️

Contradictions

The Nazis said women should stay at home, but as unemployment fell and war approached, more women were needed in factories and work.

🧑‍⚖️

Not everyone fitted the ideal

Some women were sterilised under the 1933 law if the Nazis judged them to have hereditary illness or “anti-social” behaviour. This was part of Nazi eugenics.

5. How women experienced Nazi Germany

Some felt stability

Some women remembered that children were obedient, people showed respect, and unemployment seemed to end.

Others had fewer choices

Many women lost opportunities in work, education and politics. Their lives were judged by whether they fitted the Nazi ideal of wife and mother.

Example of expectations

A newspaper advert from 1936 described an “ideal” wife as undemanding, able to do heavy work, plain, broad-hipped and without money. This shows how women were valued mainly for marriage, work and motherhood.
