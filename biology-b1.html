B1.1
The world of the microscope

Magnification makes bigger. Resolution reveals detail.

Light microscopes can view living cells. Electron microscopes have much greater magnification and resolving power, revealing finer sub-cellular structures.

Light microscope

Uses visible light and lenses. School microscopes are relatively inexpensive and can be used with living specimens.

Electron microscope

Uses electrons and has much higher magnification and resolution. TEM produces detailed 2D images; SEM gives surface images with a 3D appearance.

Exam maths

magnification = image size ÷ actual size

Convert units before calculating: 1 mm = 1000 µm; 1 µm = 1000 nm.

B1.2
Animal and plant cells

Same core machinery, extra plant structures.

Animal and plant cells share a nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, mitochondria and ribosomes. Plant cells also have a cellulose cell wall, and many have chloroplasts and a permanent vacuole.

B1.3
Eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells

Is the genetic material inside a nucleus?

Eukaryotic cells have genetic material enclosed in a nucleus. Bacteria are prokaryotes: their main DNA is a single loop free in the cytoplasm, and some also contain plasmids.

Eukaryotic

Animals, plants, fungi and protists. Cell membrane + cytoplasm + genetic material inside a nucleus. Animal and plant cells also contain structures such as mitochondria and ribosomes.

Prokaryotic

Bacteria. Usually much smaller. Cell membrane, cytoplasm and cell wall; main DNA loop not enclosed in a nucleus. May also have plasmids, a slime capsule or flagella.

Orders of magnitude

Each factor of 10 is one order of magnitude.

B1.4
Specialisation in animal cells

Structure follows function.

As animals develop, cells differentiate and become specialised. Their sub-cellular structures are adapted to a particular job.

Nerve cell

Long axon carries impulses over distance; branched dendrites/endings connect to other cells; nerve endings need energy for transmitter release.

Muscle cell

Contractile proteins shorten the cell; many mitochondria release energy for contraction; glycogen stores fuel for respiration.

Sperm cell

Long tail for movement; many mitochondria for energy; acrosome contains enzymes; nucleus carries the father's genetic information.

B1.5
Specialisation in plant cells

Absorb, photosynthesise, transport.

Plant cells can also specialise. The same rule applies: each structural adaptation should be linked to a function.

Root hair cell

Long extension gives a large surface area; large vacuole supports water uptake; many mitochondria provide energy for active transport of mineral ions.

Photosynthetic cell

Many chloroplasts absorb light; cells are arranged where they receive light; a large vacuole helps maintain rigidity.

Xylem

Dead cells form long hollow tubes. Lignin strengthens the walls and helps the vessel withstand water pressure.

Phloem

Living transport tissue carrying dissolved food. Sieve plates allow movement between cells; companion cells contain mitochondria that support transport.

B1.6
Diffusion

Net movement down a concentration gradient.

Particles move randomly, but diffusion gives a net movement from higher concentration to lower concentration. Oxygen, carbon dioxide and urea can be transported this way.

B1.7
Osmosis

Water across a partially permeable membrane.

Osmosis is the diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane.

Outside solution

Vocabulary

Hypotonic: lower solute concentration outside than inside.

Isotonic: same solute concentration.

Hypertonic: higher solute concentration outside than inside.

Exam wording: say whether you mean concentration of solute or concentration of water. Do not write that “water moves from high concentration to low concentration” without specifying water.
B1.8
Osmosis in plants

Turgid → flaccid → plasmolysed.

Water entering plant cells produces turgor, which supports non-woody tissues. Water loss reduces turgor; severe loss can cause plasmolysis.

Water enters

Turgid

The vacuole swells and presses the cytoplasm against the cell wall. The cell is firm.

Water lost

Flaccid

Turgor falls and the cell becomes less firm; the plant may wilt.

Severe loss

Plasmolysed

The cell contents shrink and pull away from the wall.

Required practical 2

Investigating osmosis in plant tissue

Place equal pieces of plant tissue in a range of known salt or sugar concentrations. Measure initial and final mass, blot samples consistently before reweighing, keep relevant control variables constant and calculate percentage change in mass.

B1.9
Active transport

Against the gradient — so energy is needed.

Active transport moves substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against the concentration gradient. It requires energy released by respiration.

Plant roots

Mineral ions can be absorbed from very dilute soil into root hair cells even when the concentration is already higher inside the cell.

Small intestine

Glucose can be absorbed into the blood even when the concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood.

B1.10
Exchanging materials

Big organisms need specialised exchange surfaces.

As an organism gets larger, its surface area to volume ratio falls. Simple diffusion across the outside surface becomes insufficient to meet the needs of all its cells.

Large surface area

More area for substances to cross at the same time.

Thin barrier

Short diffusion distance.

Blood supply

In animals, rapidly carries substances to or away from the surface and maintains gradients.

Ventilation

For animal gas exchange, refreshes air or water to maintain steep concentration gradients.

Examples: alveoli in lungs, villi in the small intestine, gills in fish, root hair cells and leaf surfaces in plants.
