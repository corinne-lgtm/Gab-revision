Big Ideas
☀️

Ultraviolet (UV)

Lies between visible light and X-rays. Shorter wavelength than visible light → carries more energy. Causes sunburn, skin cancer, and blindness.

🔬

X-rays

Produced when fast-moving electrons are stopped. Pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by dense materials like bone and lead.

☢️

Gamma rays

Produced by radioactive substances (unstable nuclei). Shorter wavelength than X-rays → penetrate more. Used to kill bacteria and cancer cells.

⚡

Ionising radiation

X-rays and gamma rays knock electrons out of atoms → atoms become charged ions. High doses kill cells; low doses cause gene mutations and cancer.

All three — UV, X-rays, and gamma rays — are part of the electromagnetic spectrum and travel at the speed of light. They differ in wavelength, energy, and source. The shorter the wavelength, the higher the energy and the more penetrating the radiation.

Uses

🔦 UV – Uses

  • Security marker pens (fluoresce under UV)
  • Detecting forgeries and valuable objects
  • Detecting fingerprints
  • Sterilisation (UV-C kills bacteria)

🩻 X-rays – Uses

  • X-ray images of bones and broken limbs
  • Detecting internal cracks in metal objects
  • Airport security scanners
  • Medical imaging

☢️ Gamma – Killing bacteria

  • ~20% of food spoiled by bacteria worldwide
  • Gamma rays kill 99% of disease-carrying organisms
  • Kills Salmonella and Clostridium
  • Sterilises surgical instruments in sealed packaging

🏥 Gamma – Cancer treatment

  • Cobalt-60 source used in radiotherapy
  • Narrow beam aimed at tumour from different directions
  • Kills cancerous cells without harming surrounding tissue
  • Source kept in thick lead container for safety
📝 Exam Tip
X-rays and gamma rays have similar properties (both ionising, both short wavelength) but differ in their source: X-rays come from fast electrons being stopped; gamma rays come from radioactive nuclei. Examiners love asking you to explain the difference — always mention the source!
X-rays vs Gamma rays — Key Differences

🩻 X-rays

  • Produced by electrons moving at high speed being stopped
  • X-ray tubes used to produce them
  • Longer wavelength than gamma rays
  • Less penetrating than gamma rays
  • Blocked by thick lead or dense bone

☢️ Gamma rays

  • Produced by radioactive substances (unstable nuclei)
  • Nuclear process — nucleus releases energy
  • Shorter wavelength than X-rays
  • More penetrating than X-rays
  • Still blocked by thick lead plates
Safety & Ionising Radiation

⚠️ What is ionisation?

  • X-rays/gamma rays knock electrons out of atoms
  • Atoms become charged (ions)
  • This is called ionising radiation
  • UV can also cause weak ionisation

💀 Dangers of ionisation

  • High doses → kill living cells
  • Low doses → damage DNA → gene mutations → cancer
  • UV → sunburn, skin cancer, blindness
  • X-ray/gamma → cell death or cancerous growth

🛡️ UV safety

  • Use sun cream outdoors to block UV
  • Wear UV-protective goggles with sunbeds
  • Don't exceed recommended sunbed time
  • UV lamps should point downward

🏷️ Film badge dosimeter

  • Worn by people working with ionising radiation
  • Measures how much radiation the wearer has received
  • If over-exposed → must stop working with equipment
  • Has a plastic case (not metal) to allow radiation through
⚠️ Common mistake
Don't confuse X-rays blocking by bone with gamma rays — gamma rays are more penetrating and are not blocked by bone. Both require thick lead to stop them, but gamma rays need even more shielding than X-rays.
Key Words
Ultraviolet (UV) — Electromagnetic waves with wavelengths shorter than visible light but longer than X-rays. Lie just beyond violet in the spectrum.
Ionising radiation — Radiation that carries enough energy to knock electrons out of atoms, turning them into charged ions. X-rays and gamma rays are examples.
Ionisation — The process of knocking electrons out of atoms so they become charged. Can damage or kill living cells.
Gamma rays — High-energy electromagnetic waves produced by radioactive substances (unstable nuclei releasing energy). Shorter wavelength than X-rays.
X-rays — Electromagnetic waves produced when high-speed electrons are stopped. Used in medicine and to detect flaws in metals.
Film badge (dosimeter) — A badge worn by radiation workers to monitor their total exposure to ionising radiation.
Cobalt-60 — A radioactive isotope used as a gamma ray source in cancer radiotherapy.
