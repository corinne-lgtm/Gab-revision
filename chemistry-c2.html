C2.1

Development of the periodic table

How scientists found a pattern — and why Mendeleev's table worked.

Learn
1808 · Dalton

Arranged known elements by their atomic weights. At this stage some compounds were still mistakenly thought to be elements.

1864 · Newlands

Also used atomic weight. He noticed that every eighth element often had similar properties: the law of octaves.

1869 · Mendeleev

Used atomic weight and chemical properties, but left gaps for undiscovered elements and sometimes changed the order to keep similar elements together.

Modern table

Elements are ordered by atomic (proton) number, which resolves the apparent mass-order problems.

Why was Mendeleev different?

Newlands' problem

He assumed all elements had already been discovered, so he did not leave gaps. His pattern worked mainly up to calcium and then put some dissimilar elements together.

Testing a prediction

If a newly discovered element has the properties predicted for a gap, that is evidence supporting the scientific idea.

Why atomic number wins

Some pairs, such as argon and potassium, look “wrong” by relative atomic mass. Ordering by proton number puts them correctly.

GCSE trap: Do not say Mendeleev simply “put elements in mass order”. His key achievement was allowing the chemical pattern to overrule strict mass order and leaving gaps for unknown elements.

Group number

For the main groups, the group number tells you the number of electrons in the outermost shell. Group 2 → 2; Group 6 → 6; Group 7 → 7.

Period number

The period tells you how many occupied electron shells the atom has.

Group 0

Noble gases have stable electronic structures. Helium has 2 electrons in its full first shell; the others have 8 in the outer shell.

Group 7 — the halogens

Diatomic non-metals that gain one electron. Reactivity decreases down the group.

Learn
HalogenMoleculeMelting pointBoiling pointAt 20°C
FluorineF₂−220°C−188°Cgas
ChlorineCl₂−101°C−35°Cgas
BromineBr₂−7°C59°Cliquid
IodineI₂114°C184°Csolid

What they are

Toxic non-metals with coloured vapours. They have low melting/boiling points compared with metals and are poor conductors.

How they exist

Halogen elements exist as diatomic molecules: F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂.

How they react

They have 7 outer electrons, so they tend to gain 1 electron and form 1− ions in ionic compounds.

Opposite trend to Group 1

FClBrIAt↓reactivity decreases

Melting and boiling points, however, increase down Group 7.

Displacement rule

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its salt.

Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq)

So chlorine displaces bromide and iodide. Bromine displaces iodide. Iodine cannot displace chloride or bromide.

Displacement reaction checker

GCSE trap: Distinguish the element from its ion: chlorine is Cl₂; chloride ion is Cl⁻. Bromine is Br₂; bromide ion is Br⁻.

C2.5

Explaining trends

Distance, shielding and nuclear charge explain why Group 1 and Group 7 go in opposite directions.

Higher reasoning

Going down a group: what changes?

1

More shells

Each new period adds an occupied electron shell.

2

Bigger atom

The outer shell is further from the positive nucleus.

3

More shielding

Inner electrons reduce the attraction felt by the outer/incoming electron.

4

Nuclear charge ↑

There are more protons too, but distance + shielding outweigh this effect here.
