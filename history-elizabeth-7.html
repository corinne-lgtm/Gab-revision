7.1

Why was Mary, Queen of Scots, seen as such a threat?

Mary was Elizabeth’s cousin, a Catholic and a possible claimant to the English throne. The textbook presents her arrival in England in 1568 as a major security problem: English Protestants feared a Catholic queen might replace Elizabeth, while plots repeatedly used Mary as their focus.

👑 Royal claim

A rival heir

Mary was Elizabeth’s cousin and had Tudor blood. For Catholics who rejected Elizabeth’s legitimacy, Mary could be presented as an alternative queen.

✝ Catholic

A focus for opposition

Mary remained Catholic while Scotland had become Protestant. In England, Catholic opponents of Elizabeth could rally around her.

🌍 Foreign links

An international danger

Mary had grown up in France and married Francis II. Her French connections and Catholic faith meant Elizabeth also had to think about support from Catholic powers abroad.

Mary’s path to England

1542

Mary became Queen of Scots when she was only six days old.

France

She grew up in France and married Francis, who became King Francis II. This briefly made Mary queen of both Scotland and France.

1560–61

Francis died in 1560. Mary returned to Scotland, where Protestantism had grown stronger during her absence.

1565–67

Mary married Lord Darnley in 1565. Their son James was born in 1566. Darnley was murdered the following year and suspicion fell on Mary and the Earl of Bothwell.

1567–68

Mary was forced to abdicate in favour of her infant son James VI. After escaping imprisonment, she fled to England in 1568 and sought Elizabeth’s help.

Why was Mary so difficult to deal with?

Mary’s arrival shocked English Protestants. The Privy Council saw her as a serious threat and there were calls for her execution, but Elizabeth resisted. Executing a queen simply because she might become the focus of rebellion was a step Elizabeth was reluctant to take. Instead Mary was kept under guard for the next 19 years. The problem was that imprisonment did not stop Catholic plotters from using her name.

Key idea: Mary’s danger changed over time. At first she was mainly a potential alternative monarch. Repeated plots — and finally the evidence uncovered in 1586 — made the threat appear much more direct.
7.2

Elizabeth’s treatment of Mary

Elizabeth kept Mary imprisoned for years. The key question was whether Mary was merely a symbol for plotters — or was actively involved in attempts to remove Elizabeth.

The final plot: why 1586 changed everything

1 · Babington’s planAnthony Babington and a group of Catholics planned to kill Elizabeth and rescue Mary.
2 · Coded correspondenceMary communicated with supporters through secret coded letters. Walsingham’s intelligence network was able to intercept the correspondence.
3 · The crucial evidenceMary’s reply was taken as proof that she had agreed to the plan. The government now believed it had evidence connecting her personally to a plot against Elizabeth’s life.
4 · TrialMary was put on trial in October 1586 before 36 noblemen, including Sir Francis Walsingham. She denied plotting against Elizabeth, but was found guilty and sentenced to death.

Why execute Mary?

Security: as long as Mary lived, plotters could try to replace Elizabeth with her.

Babington evidence: the government believed it could now prove Mary had supported a plan involving Elizabeth’s assassination.

Pressure: Parliament and Elizabeth’s advisers wanted the threat removed.

Why was Elizabeth reluctant?

She was an anointed queen: executing another monarch could set a dangerous precedent.

Foreign reaction: Catholic powers such as Spain and France might use the execution against England.

James VI: Mary was the mother of the Scottish king, whose reaction mattered.

Trial → death warrant → execution

Although Mary had been found guilty, Elizabeth still hesitated. She feared the consequences of executing a fellow queen and worried that Mary’s son, James VI of Scotland, or Catholic Spain might retaliate. Elizabeth eventually signed the death warrant. Mary was executed at Fotheringhay Castle on 8 February 1587.

ImpactThe immediate rival is gone, but hostility from Catholic powers remains.
Important judgement: Mary’s execution solved one problem — there was no longer a living Catholic Queen of Scots in England around whom rebels could organise. But it did not solve Elizabeth’s wider problems. Relations with Spain were already dangerous and the Spanish Armada followed in 1588.
