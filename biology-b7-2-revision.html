B7.2 Cancer

What a tumour is, the difference between benign and malignant tumours, and how cancer spreads.

📖 Revision ✏️ Questions
Cancer is uncontrolled cell division — a tumour forms when cells lose control of the cell cycle. Malignant tumours spread through the body; benign tumours stay in one place.
🎯

Learning objectives

  • What a tumour is
  • The difference between benign and malignant tumours
  • How cancer spreads

What is a tumour?

The cells in your body divide in a regular, controlled sequence — the cell cycle. A tumour forms when control of this sequence is lost and cells grow in an abnormal, uncontrolled way.

Tumour cells do not respond to the normal mechanisms that control the cell cycle. They divide rapidly with very little non-dividing time between divisions — producing a mass of abnormally growing cells.

Benign vs Malignant tumours

🟡

Benign tumour

  • Growth of abnormal cells contained in one place — usually within a membrane
  • Do not invade other parts of the body
  • Generally considered less dangerous
  • BUT can grow very large, very quickly — if they cause pressure or damage to an organ, this can be life-threatening
  • Example: benign brain tumours are very dangerous as there is no extra space for them to grow into
🔴

Malignant tumour (cancer)

  • Can spread around the body, invading neighbouring healthy tissues
  • The initial tumour splits up — small clumps of cells enter the bloodstream or lymphatic system
  • These cells circulate and may lodge in another organ → continue uncontrolled division → form secondary tumours
  • Cancer cells divide more rapidly AND live longer than normal cells
  • Can completely disrupt normal tissues and, if untreated, will often kill
  • Because of how they spread, malignant tumours are very difficult to treat

Causes of cancer

🧬

Genetic risk factors

Clear genetic risk factors exist for some cancers — including early breast cancer and ovarian cancer. Inherited gene mutations can predispose someone to developing cancer.

☢️

Mutations

Most cancers result from mutations — changes in the genetic material. Chemicals such as asbestos and the tar in tobacco smoke (carcinogens) can cause mutations that trigger tumour formation.

☀️

Ionising radiation

UV light from the sun can interrupt the normal cell cycle → melanomas (uncontrolled growth of pigment-forming skin cells). X-rays can also cause tumours.

🦠

Viruses

About 15% of human cancers are caused by virus infections. Example: HPV (human papilloma virus) causes cervical cancer. Teenagers in the UK are routinely vaccinated against HPV.

Treating cancer

☢️

Radiotherapy

Cancer cells are destroyed by targeted doses of radiation — stops mitosis in cancer cells. Can also damage healthy cells. Methods of delivering radiation in very targeted ways are improving cure rates.

💊

Chemotherapy

Chemicals used to either stop cancer cells dividing or to make them 'self-destruct'. Many types exist — scientists are making them more specific to cancer cells to reduce side effects on healthy tissue.

⚠️ Chemotherapy drugs affect rapidly-dividing cells — which is why they also damage hair follicles, skin cells, stomach lining cells, and blood cells (all of which divide rapidly). This causes hair loss and nausea as side effects.
