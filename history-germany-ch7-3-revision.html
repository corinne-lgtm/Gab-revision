AQA GCSE History · Germany 1890–1945

7.3 Were Germans better off under the Nazis?

How the Nazis controlled workers through the DAF, SDA and KDF — and whether working-class Germans truly benefited.

🏠 Main hub 📚 History index 📚 Germany index ✏️ Quiz rapide
The Nazis gave workers jobs and rewards — but took away their rights and freedom.

Background: who were working-class Germans?

Shop workers, builders, secretaries — the largest social group in Germany in the 1930s. Most had not supported the Nazis before 1933. They had voted for the Social Democrats or Communists, and were loyal to trade unions. Hitler needed them to support him — but also needed them under control.

Work and control

🏗️

More jobs

Through the National Labour Service and rearmament, the Nazis provided work. The army grew; schools, hospitals and roads were built.

💰

Wages

Wages didn't increase for a few years, but at least jobs existed. Food also cost more than before — Germany relied more on imports.

🚫

Trade unions banned

All trade unions were banned in 1933. Workers lost the right to strike or negotiate wages. Workers could not quit without government permission.

⏰

Long hours

People could be forced to work as many hours as the Nazis required.

The DAF — German Labour Front

The DAF (Deutsche Arbeitsfront) replaced trade unions. It promised to protect workers' rights and improve conditions. In reality, it kept workers under Nazi control.

The DAF ran two key schemes:

🌟 SDA — Beauty of Labour
Schönheit der Arbeit

  • Aimed to improve the working environment
  • Better lighting and safety equipment
  • New wash rooms
  • Low-cost canteens
  • Sports facilities

🎭 KDF — Strength through Joy
Kraft durch Freude

  • Organised leisure activities to encourage hard work
  • Cheap holidays abroad (e.g. Italy, cruises)
  • Trips to the theatre and concerts
  • Tickets to football matches
  • Skiing in Switzerland

⚠️ Many KDF holidays were still too expensive for most working-class Germans.

The Volkswagen — People's Car

🚗

The DAF had a savings scheme for the Volkswagen (People's Car), designed by Ferdinand Porsche. Workers saved 5 marks a week until they had 750 marks in a stamp book — then they would get a car. The price was set at 990 marks.

The reality: It was a swindle — the money was used to make weapons instead. Not one customer ever received their car.

Were Germans better off? — The balance

✅ Better off…

  • Unemployment fell dramatically
  • More jobs through rearmament and public works
  • Workers got leisure activities and holidays through KDF
  • Working environments improved via SDA
  • Sense of national pride and purpose

❌ Worse off…

  • Trade unions banned — no right to strike
  • Could not quit without government permission
  • Forced to work long hours
  • Wages stayed low; food cost more
  • Volkswagen savings scheme was a fraud
  • KDF holidays unaffordable for most

Interpretations

INTERPRETATION B — Konrad Morgen (2007), German lawyer and magistrate, interviewed in the 1970s:
"What did Hitler promise? Work and bread for the millions of unemployed and hungry masses. Nowadays in our prosperous society work and bread doesn't mean anything any more, but then it was a basic need of life, and this promise sounded like a promise of paradise."

What this shows: Many Germans supported Hitler because he promised to solve unemployment and hunger — which to desperate people felt miraculous.

INTERPRETATION D — Oral history project in Essen (1983–87), Ernest Bromberg, industrial worker at the Krupp steelworks:
"I had no time for [Nazi activities]. Regarding the [German] Labour Front, yes people kicked against it a bit and then just carried on. If you're on piecework, you didn't have any time to make speeches, you didn't extend your breaks because the money was very good. I didn't worry any more about the Nazis, I didn't have anything to do with them."

What this shows: Some workers were too busy and well-paid to care about Nazi politics — they kept their heads down and focused on earning.
