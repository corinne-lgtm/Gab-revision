P7.1 · textbook pages 84–85

Atoms and radiation

Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable atomic nuclei. Alpha, beta and gamma are different kinds of nuclear radiation. Decay is random and cannot be predicted for a particular nucleus.

Discovering radioactivity

Becquerel found uranium salts emitted radiation; Marie and Pierre Curie investigated radioactive substances.

Inside the atom

A tiny positive nucleus contains protons and neutrons, with electrons around it. Most mass is in the nucleus.

Geiger–Müller counter

A detector counts ionising radiation; measured counts include natural background radiation.

GCSE diagram · Atoms and radiation

Alpha, beta and gamma: quick comparison

Alpha consists of two protons and two neutrons; beta-minus consists of fast electrons emitted from the nucleus; gamma is electromagnetic radiation.

Exam alert: A radioactive atom does not have to absorb energy or collide with something to decay.

P7.2 · textbook pages 86–87

The discovery of the nucleus

Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at very thin gold foil. Most passed straight through; some deflected; very few rebounded. This overturned the plum pudding model and supported a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.

Plum pudding model

Positive charge spread through the atom, with embedded negative electrons: it could not explain large-angle scattering.

Evidence from gold foil

Most alpha particles pass through because atoms are mostly empty space. A few deflect strongly because the nucleus is small, massive and positive.

Bohr and Chadwick

Bohr proposed electron energy levels; Chadwick subsequently discovered the neutron.

Predict the alpha-particle path

Why did most particles pass through? Why did only a few rebound?

Most passed through because atoms are mainly empty space. Rare large deflections and rebounds reveal a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most atomic mass.

P7.3 · textbook pages 88–89

Changes in the nucleus

Isotopes have the same atomic number (protons) but different numbers of neutrons. In alpha decay mass number decreases by 4 and atomic number by 2. In beta-minus decay a neutron changes to a proton and an electron, so mass number stays the same and atomic number increases by 1. Gamma emission changes neither number.

Atomic notation

A is the mass number = protons + neutrons; Z is the atomic number = protons. Neutrons = A − Z.

Alpha decay

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus: ⁴₂He. Example: ²²⁸₉₀Th → ²²⁴₈₈Ra + ⁴₂He.

Beta-minus decay

A neutron becomes a proton and emits an electron: ⁴⁰₁₉K → ⁴⁰₂₀Ca + ⁰₋₁e. Gamma decay leaves A and Z unchanged.

P7.4 · textbook pages 90–91

More about alpha, beta and gamma radiation

Alpha is strongly ionising and stopped by paper; beta is moderately ionising and stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium; gamma is weakly ionising but highly penetrating and requires thick lead or concrete to reduce its intensity. Correct background counts when comparing sources.

Penetration and ionisation

Alpha: short range, strong ionisation. Beta: intermediate. Gamma: longest range, weak ionisation.

Irradiation vs contamination

Irradiation means exposure to radiation. Contamination means unwanted radioactive material is present on or in something.

Safety and applications

Minimise time, maximise distance and use suitable shielding. Smoke alarms can use alpha; thickness monitoring uses beta.

P7.5 · textbook pages 92–93

Activity and half-life

Activity is the number of nuclear decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq). Count rate from a detector is counts per second or minute; it is not automatically the same as the source activity. Half-life is the average time for the number of undecayed nuclei, or the activity, to halve. Radioactive decay is random.

Half-life equation

Remaining quantity = initial quantity × (½)ⁿ, where n = time ÷ half-life.

Correct for background

Source count rate = measured count rate − background count rate, using consistent units.

Graph interpretation

Read the time for the corrected count rate or activity to fall to half its current value; that time is the half-life.
