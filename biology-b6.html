B6 Preventing and treating disease

Understand vaccination, medicines, antibiotic resistance, drug discovery and clinical trials through clear explanations, visual models and exam practice.

B6.1

Vaccination

Learn

How vaccination works

Vaccines expose the immune system to antigens from a pathogen, or instructions for producing those antigens, without causing the full disease. Lymphocytes make specific antibodies and some become memory cells.

Memory and the secondary response

If the same pathogen enters later, memory cells respond much faster. The correct antibodies are produced rapidly, so the pathogen may be destroyed before significant symptoms develop.

Herd immunity

If enough people in a population are immune, transmission becomes less likely. This can indirectly protect people who cannot be vaccinated. The proportion needed depends on how easily the disease spreads and on vaccine effectiveness.

Vaccination

Harmless antigens are introduced to stimulate a specific immune response.

Concept check

Herd immunity

Herd immunity is about transmission through a population, not about one person having more antibodies. When more people are immune, the pathogen has fewer susceptible hosts to infect.

Remember: the required vaccination level is not the same for every disease.

B6.2

Antibiotics and painkillers

Learn

Painkillers treat symptoms

Painkillers such as paracetamol can reduce pain or fever, but they do not kill the pathogen causing an infectious disease. The immune system still has to deal with the infection.

Antibiotics treat bacterial disease

Antibiotics such as penicillin kill bacteria or stop them growing by targeting bacterial structures or processes. They do not work against viruses, which reproduce inside host cells.

Antibiotic resistance

Random mutations can make some bacteria resistant. Antibiotics kill susceptible bacteria, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, and resistance becomes more common through natural selection.

Bacteria

Bacteria can reproduce independently and may cause disease.

B6.3

Discovering drugs

Learn

Drugs from living organisms

Some medicines came from natural sources. Aspirin was developed from compounds associated with willow, and digitalis drugs came from foxglove. Natural origin does not automatically mean safe; dose and toxicity still matter.

Fleming and penicillin

In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed that Penicillium mould contaminating a bacterial culture had produced a clear zone where bacteria did not grow. This suggested the mould made an antibacterial substance: penicillin.

From discovery to medicine

Howard Florey and Ernst Chain later developed ways to purify and test penicillin. Large-scale production then made it possible to use penicillin widely to treat bacterial infections.

Disc A

Illustrative clear zone: 48 units. A larger zone can indicate greater inhibition, but diffusion and concentration must also be considered.

Working scientifically

What does a clear zone tell you?

A larger inhibition zone can suggest a more effective antibiotic against that bacterium under those conditions. It does not prove the drug is safe for humans or that it will work inside the body. Those questions require further testing.

B6.4

Developing drugs

Learn

Efficacy, toxicity and dosage

New medicines are tested to find out whether they work, whether they can cause harm, and what dose gives the best balance of benefit and risk.

Preclinical testing

Candidate drugs are first tested in the laboratory using cells and tissues. Animal studies may then be used where required to investigate effects in a whole organism.

Clinical trials

Initial human trials use very low doses to investigate safety. Later trials use larger groups and patients to assess effectiveness, side effects and optimum dosage.

Fair testing

Random allocation, control groups and double-blinding reduce bias. A placebo may be used when appropriate; an existing treatment may be used instead if withholding effective treatment would be unethical.

Clinical trials

Why use placebos, controls and double-blinding?

A control group gives a baseline for comparison. Random allocation reduces systematic differences between groups. Double-blinding reduces expectation and assessment bias because neither participants nor assessors know who received which treatment until the trial is unblinded.
