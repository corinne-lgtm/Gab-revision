B4.9 Factors affecting transpiration

The factors that affect the rate of transpiration, and ways of investigating the effect of environmental factors on water uptake.

📖 Revision ✏️ Questions
Transpiration is faster in hot, dry, windy, or bright conditions — anything that increases evaporation or steepens the concentration gradient speeds up water loss.
🎯

Learning objectives

  • Factors that affect the rate of transpiration
  • Ways of investigating the effect of environmental factors on rates of water uptake

Factors affecting transpiration rate

FactorEffect on transpirationWhy?
🌡️ Temperature ↑ Increases ⬆ Molecules move faster → evaporation faster. Rate of photosynthesis also increases → more stomata open for CO₂ → more water can escape.
💧 Humidity ↑ Decreases ⬇ More water vapour in the air → smaller concentration gradient between inside leaf and outside → slower diffusion of water vapour out.
🌬️ Air movement (wind) ↑ Increases ⬆ Wind removes water vapour from around the stomata → maintains a steep concentration gradient → diffusion of water vapour stays rapid.
☀️ Light intensity ↑ Increases ⬆ More light → more photosynthesis → more stomata open to let CO₂ in → more water vapour can escape through the open stomata.
💡 Summary: Hot + Dry + Windy + Bright = fastest transpiration. Cold + Humid + Still + Dark = slowest. Think of a desert vs a shaded, humid rainforest floor.

How plants control water loss

🟡

Waxy cuticle

Most leaves have a waxy, waterproof layer (cuticle) on the upper surface to prevent uncontrolled water loss. In very hot environments the cuticle may be very thick and shiny.

⬇️

Stomata on underside

Most stomata are on the lower epidermis — shielded from direct sunlight and heat of the sun, reducing the rate of evaporation.

🥀

Wilting

If water is lost faster than it is replaced, the plant wilts — leaves collapse and hang down. This reduces surface area exposed to the sun, limiting further water loss.

🚪

Closing stomata

Guard cells close stomata to prevent water loss when the plant is short of water. This also stops photosynthesis and may lead to overheating — but saves the plant.

Measuring transpiration — the potometer

🔬

A potometer measures the uptake of water by a plant — not transpiration directly, but almost all water taken up is lost through transpiration (only a tiny amount is used in photosynthesis).

How it works:

  • A shoot is cut and placed in water-filled tubing — sealed with no air gaps
  • An air bubble is introduced — its position along a scale is recorded over time
  • As the plant transpires, it pulls water up, moving the air bubble along the scale
  • Rate of bubble movement = rate of water uptake ≈ rate of transpiration
  • Conditions can be changed (fan, lamp, humidity) to investigate their effect

Exam tip

Common questions:
  • Name parts of the leaf that help reduce water loss under normal conditions [2 marks]
  • Explain the effect of a fan blowing onto the leaves of a plant [4 marks]
  • Describe the effect of coating upper/lower leaf surfaces with petroleum jelly + explain the difference [1+1+3 marks]
  • Water lilies have stomata on top — suggest why this is an adaptation + why controlling transpiration is not very important [2+2 marks]

Petroleum jelly question: Coating upper surface has little effect (few stomata there) → coating lower surface greatly reduces transpiration (most stomata) → comparison = much more water lost when lower surface is uncoated because most stomata are there.
