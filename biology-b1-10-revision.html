AQA GCSE Biology · B1 Cell structure and transport

B1.10 Exchanging materials

How the surface area to volume ratio affects exchange, and why large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces.

📖 Revision ✏️ Questions
As organisms get bigger, their surface area to volume ratio gets smaller — making it harder to exchange materials by simple diffusion alone.
🎯

Learning objectives

  • How SA:V ratio varies with size
  • Why large multicellular organisms need special exchange surfaces
  • Adaptations of effective exchange surfaces

Surface area to volume ratio (SA:V)

🔬

Small organism (1 cm cube)

6 : 1

High SA:V ratio — large surface relative to volume. Simple diffusion is sufficient. Substances can reach every cell quickly.

🐘

Large organism (3 cm cube)

2 : 1

Low SA:V ratio — small surface relative to volume. Simple diffusion is not enough. Cells deep inside cannot be supplied fast enough.

💡 As size doubles, volume increases much faster than surface area — so SA:V ratio falls. Large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems.

Why large organisms need special surfaces

🚫

Problems for large organisms

  • Gases and food molecules can no longer reach every cell by simple diffusion
  • Metabolic waste cannot be removed fast enough
  • The distance from surface to centre is too large
✅

The solution

Large organisms have specialised exchange surfaces adapted to be as effective as possible, plus transport systems (e.g. blood) to move substances to and from cells.

Adaptations of effective exchange surfaces

📐

Large surface area

More area means more exchange can happen at the same time — substances can move in and out much faster overall.

📏

Thin walls / membrane

Thin walls provide a short diffusion path — substances don't have to travel far, so exchange is faster.

🩸

Efficient blood supply (animals)

Blood constantly carries substances away from the exchange surface, maintaining a steep concentration gradient — keeping diffusion fast.

💨

Ventilation (animals)

Moving air or water over the surface (e.g. breathing) maintains a steep concentration gradient for gas exchange.

Examples of exchange surfaces

🫁

Human alveoli (lungs)

Millions of tiny air sacs give an enormous surface area. Very thin walls, rich blood supply, and constant ventilation maintain steep concentration gradients for O₂ and CO₂.

🐟

Fish gills

Stacks of thin filaments give a large surface area with a rich blood supply. Fish pump water over gills using an operculum flap to maintain concentration gradients.

🌱

Plant leaves

Flat, thin leaves with air spaces in leaf tissue and stomata provide a large surface area and maintain steep concentration gradients for gas and water exchange.

🌿

Plant root hair cells

Long, hair-like extensions give a large surface area to absorb water and mineral ions more efficiently from the soil.

🐢

Fitzroy river turtle

Has two large sacs lined with finger-like folds at its rear opening — giving a large surface area and rich blood supply for absorbing oxygen from water. A muscular opening pumps water in and out, ventilating the folds.

🧫

Small intestine villi

Finger-like projections (villi) give a large surface area, short diffusion paths, and a rich blood supply to absorb digested food efficiently.

Exam tip

Common questions:
  • Describe two adaptations of an effective exchange surface [2 marks]
  • Explain how SA:V ratio affects the way an organism exchanges materials [2 marks]
  • Compare the gas exchange system of fish with that of the Fitzroy river turtle [5 marks]
  • Summarise the adaptations of effective exchange surfaces and explain the importance of each [6 marks]

Remember: For every adaptation, always explain why it helps — don't just name it. E.g. "thin walls → short diffusion distance → faster exchange".
