C5.1

The reactivity series

Use observations with water and acids to rank metals.

Learn

With water

Very reactive metals form a metal hydroxide + hydrogen. Magnesium reacts only slowly with cold water.

With dilute acid

Metals above hydrogen form a salt + hydrogen. The faster the fizzing, the more reactive the metal.

Oxidation / reduction

Oxidation can mean gain of oxygen. Reduction can mean loss of oxygen.

C5.3

Extracting metals

The reactivity series tells you which extraction method can work.

Learn

Native metals

Very unreactive metals such as gold may occur as the element itself.

Below carbon

Metal oxides can be reduced by carbon.

metal oxide + carbon → metal + carbon dioxide

Above carbon

Too reactive to be reduced by carbon: extract by electrolysis of the molten compound (C6).

Salts from insoluble bases

Required Practical 8: make a pure, dry soluble salt.

Learn
REQUIRED PRACTICAL 8

Making copper sulfate from copper(II) oxide + sulfuric acid

1 Heat gently
dilute sulfuric acid
2 Add CuO in excess
stir until no more reacts
3 Filter
remove excess insoluble CuO
4 Evaporate gently
until crystals start to form
5 Cool
allow crystallisation
6 Dry crystals
filter paper
H₂SO₄(aq) + CuO(s) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

Why excess solid?

Ensures all acid is neutralised.

Why filter?

Removes the unreacted insoluble solid.

Why not boil dry?

Stop when concentrated; cooling gives larger, purer crystals.

Formula builder reminder

Total positive charge must equal total negative charge. Fe³⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → Fe₂(SO₄)₃.

C5.7

Neutralisation and the pH scale

Acids make H⁺(aq); alkalis make OH⁻(aq).

Learn

Acid

Produces H⁺(aq) ions in water.

Alkali

A soluble base that produces OH⁻(aq) ions in water.

Neutral

pH 7. Neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

pH scale

01234567891011121314

0–6 acidic · 7 neutral · 8–14 alkaline. Universal indicator gives an approximate pH; a pH probe gives a numerical reading.

Think like the exam

Adding strong acid slowly to a strong alkali makes pH fall: slowly at first, very sharply around neutralisation, then more slowly again.

C5.8

Strong and weak acids

Higher tier: strength is about ionisation, not concentration.

Learn

Strong acid

Completely ionised in water. Examples: hydrochloric, nitric, sulfuric acid.

HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻

Weak acid

Partially ionised; reversible equilibrium. Examples: ethanoic, citric, carbonic acid.

CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻

Concentrated ≠ strong

Concentration = amount of acid per volume. Strength = proportion ionised.

The factor-of-10 rule

[H⁺] mol/dm³pH
1 × 10⁻¹1
1 × 10⁻²2
1 × 10⁻³3
1 × 10⁻⁴4

For whole-number pH questions: pH down by 1 → [H⁺] ×10. pH down by 2 → ×100.

Classic trap: a dilute strong acid can have a higher pH than a concentrated weak acid. “Strong” does not mean “concentrated”.
